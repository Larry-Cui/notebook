## 9.0: Overview

In this Module we examine the ethical and professional considerations **unique to lawyers in organization settings.**  

We have touched on some of these issues before, for example in our discussion of Crown prosecutors as government employees.  However in this Module we **focus** specifically on **how being part of an organization affects a lawyer’s ethical and professional duties and obligations.**  

Our examination of this area is divided into two parts. 

- First we will review the procedural duties and practical work of lawyers in **private organizational settings**, usually corporations or other large private organizations.  The ethical considerations for in-house counsel can be a bit different than lawyers operating in other environments, and they may also differ from lawyers who act as external counsel to corporations or other businesses.  We will look at specific case studies of lawyers implicated in corporate ethical scandals and some of the policy and legal actions taken in response to these scandals.  

- Then we will specifically review the ethical and professional duties and obligations of lawyers in **government**.  We have already examined the role of Crown prosecutors, but government lawyers also work in many other departments in various other capacities.  With these government lawyers, there are some unique ethical requirements in particular that may overlap or add additional responsibilities to the lawyer.

## 9.1: Corporate External and In-House Counsel

!!! note "Reading"

    Hugh Gunz and Sally Gunz, [*Ethical Challenges in the Role of In-House Counsel*, (2019) 69 Case W. Res. L. Rev. 953](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1qn6V8X7UjlvW500NbKiYwqa9B13_EhAB/view?usp=sharing){:target="_blank"}

!!! info "Hugh Gunz and Sally Gunz, [*Ethical Challenges in the Role of In-House Counsel*, (2019) 69 Case W. Res. L. Rev. 953](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1qn6V8X7UjlvW500NbKiYwqa9B13_EhAB/view?usp=sharing){:target="_blank"}"

    **Core Thesis**
  
    Professional codes of conduct and the "bad apple" explanation are insufficient to explain ethical lapses by in-house counsel. Rather than focusing solely on outright fraud or morally bankrupt actors, the authors argue that **subtle organizational pressures, role expectations, and identity shifts** shape how ordinary, well-intentioned lawyers navigate everyday ("low-intensity") ethical dilemmas inside non-professional organizations (NPOs).
  
    - **Evolution of Ethical Decision-Making & OPC**
        - **Beyond "Bad Apples":** Blaming isolated "bad apples" lets organizations off the hook. Corporate failures often involve professionals who know ethical rules yet bend or overlook them within their workplace context.
        - **Organizational-Professional Conflict (OPC):** Employed professionals face **competing duties:** serving the public/profession's ethical standards while also owing loyalty to the employing organization.
        - **Surprising Findings:** Despite lawyers having strong professional traditions and bar oversight, empirical research shows in-house counsel often report relatively low levels of perceived OPC, potentially because they view themselves as managers, balance roles effectively, or are sidestepped by executives when sensitive issues arise.
    - **The "Doing Law" vs. "Managing" Tension**
        - **Two Polar Models:** In-house legal departments generally balance "doing law" (legal expertise and compliance) with managing (preventive planning, overseeing outside counsel, and supporting business operations).
        - **Risks of Each Extreme:**
            - Over-focusing on "doing law" risks turning the department into an insular, inward-looking "captive law firm" **disconnected from broader organizational realities.**
            - Over-focusing on management/strategy risks **blurring the lawyer's independent legal judgment**, fostering personalized loyalty to senior executives rather than the corporate entity itself, or becoming a gatekeeper that controls information flow in ways that compromise objectivity.
    - **Identity Theory, Context & "Client Capture"**
        - **Professional vs. Organizational Identity:** Drawing on identity theory and a survey of 484 Canadian corporate counsel, the authors distinguish between **a professional identity** ("How does my profession require me to handle this?") and **an organizational identity** ("How should I handle this as an employee of this organization?").
        - **What Shifts Identity:** Counsel who spent more time on non-professional/managerial work, or who felt vulnerable to having their legal work outsourced, were more likely to adopt an organizational identity and choose organizational responses in realistic ethical dilemma vignettes (including a scenario modeled on the Texaco boardroom controversy).
        - **"Client Capture":** Deep **operational integration** makes in-house counsel more useful to management in some ways, yet creates a paradox where their advice may become **less detached and objective.**
    - **Practical Takeaways**
        - **Rules Alone Aren't Enough:** Legal ethics education and compliance rules must be paired with behavioral awareness of how workplace context subtly shifts reasoning.
        - **Anticipate Low-Intensity Dilemmas:** Everyday ethical pressures happen frequently in ordinary practice; counsel must recognize environmental influences early and plan ahead for how to respond when role expectations and professional duties collide.

An important contextual factor note here, as we consider ethics, professionalism and corporate counsel, is that there is actually some **variations** in how **lawyers can represent corporations as organizations**.  It can be done as part of a lawyer’s, or a firm’s, **private practice,** which likely includes other clients.  It can also be done as **in-house counsel**, which is a lawyer being employed by the business or corporation.  In-house practice is becoming increasingly common in Canada and globally as well. 

The **Gunz** article considers these issues from the viewpoint of in-house counsel.  It does not focus on ethical transgressions by individual lawyers.  Lawyers are human and may be susceptible to misconduct where personal profit is at stake and ethics are not properly considered as an obligation rather than an option.  The article points toward a more systemic problem here rather than the individual actions of a few lawyers as “bad apples.” 

The **argument** that **Gunz & Gunz** make is a contextual one:  

> it rests **upon the contextual shifts** that occur within an organization that **affect the ethical decision-making processes** of all the professionals who work for that organization, including the lawyers.  

This is what arguably happened in many of the case studies that we will examine in this Module.  When you read the **Gunz** article consider this context and the issue of lawyers not only being willing participants in ethically dubious business practices but actually facilitating those practices through their legal expertise.

The mini-lecture that follows highlights a few of the distinctions between **working in a private a law firm with an organizational client** and **working as in-house counsel.**  This should help you frame some of the ethical considerations for each type of arrangement.  There is some **overlap** in legal ethics when representing an organization, whether you are in private law firm practice or working in-house as an employee of the organization.  But there are some important **differences** as well.

**Framing an action** is an attempt to rationalize what an actor is doing at a particular time point to fit in with their belief system. 


**Framing** describes how our **responses** to situations, including our ethical judgments, are **impacted just by how those situations are posed or viewed.**

!!! info "Summary from transcript of what seems like a video lecture"

    **The Core Theme**

    Ethical failures in corporate law often happen because how a situation is "framed" (mentally packaged or rationalized) narrows a person's perspective, causing otherwise smart professionals to overlook broader ethical duties in favor of profit, loyalty, or self-interest.

    **In-House Counsel vs. Private Firm Lawyers**

    - Shared baseline: Both have the same fundamental ethical duties (avoiding conflicts of interest, giving impartial advice, maintaining professionalism).
    - Private firm lawyers: Represent the corporation alongside other clients.
        - Small firms / solo practitioners: Get early client contact and hands-on work, do their own paperwork, and face higher Law Society disciplinary rates.
        - Large firms: Offer structured ethics training and close supervision by senior associates/partners as a "safety net" (in theory, though not always in practice).
    - In-house counsel: Employees with only one client (their employer).
        - Pros: Usually more experienced; no pressure to bring in new clients or hit billable hours; greater job stability (less financial stress that can trigger wellness/ethical lapses).
        - Cons: In-house ethics is a newer, less-developed field, and experience doesn't automatically prevent ethical blind spots.

    **The Enron & Arthur Andersen Lesson**
    
    - During the 2001 Enron scandal, corporate lawyers and Arthur Andersen lawyers failed to prevent the collapse.
    - In depositions, lawyers rationalized their inaction afterward ("I just didn't think about it that way at the time"). This is framing in action—rationalizing what you're doing in the moment to fit your self-image.

    **What "Framing" Is & How It Distorts Ethics**
    
    - Definition: How a situation is posed or viewed changes the decision—even when the facts are identical (e.g., meat labeled "75% fat-free" vs. "25% fat", or a student spinning a forgotten assignment as "I've been really busy").
    - Real-world examples from the transcript:
        - Space Shuttle Challenger: Engineers opposed launching on safety grounds until a manager told them to "put on their management hats"—reframing safety into a financial/business decision, leading them to reverse course.
        - Enron travel expenses: An employee ignored spending cuts and booked luxury hotels/restaurants, framing it around "I work hard and deserve this" rather than company policy.
        - Enron financial manipulation: CFOs and accountants framed their actions around company loyalty and boosting stock price, blinding themselves to the impact on employees' pension funds.

    **How to counter narrow framing:**
    
    - Ask how the decision would look on the front page of a newspaper.
    - Seek full information and outside perspectives from neutral third parties.
    - Actively challenge your own assumptions and biases.
