## 9.0: Overview

In this Module we examine the ethical and professional considerations **unique to lawyers in organization settings.**  

We have touched on some of these issues before, for example in our discussion of Crown prosecutors as government employees.  However in this Module we **focus** specifically on **how being part of an organization affects a lawyer’s ethical and professional duties and obligations.**  

Our examination of this area is divided into two parts. 

- First we will review the procedural duties and practical work of lawyers in **private organizational settings**, usually corporations or other large private organizations.  The ethical considerations for in-house counsel can be a bit different than lawyers operating in other environments, and they may also differ from lawyers who act as external counsel to corporations or other businesses.  We will look at specific case studies of lawyers implicated in corporate ethical scandals and some of the policy and legal actions taken in response to these scandals.  

- Then we will specifically review the ethical and professional duties and obligations of lawyers in **government**.  We have already examined the role of Crown prosecutors, but government lawyers also work in many other departments in various other capacities.  With these government lawyers, there are some unique ethical requirements in particular that may overlap or add additional responsibilities to the lawyer.

## 9.1: Corporate External and In-House Counsel

!!! note "Reading"

    Hugh Gunz and Sally Gunz, [*Ethical Challenges in the Role of In-House Counsel*, (2019) 69 Case W. Res. L. Rev. 953](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1qn6V8X7UjlvW500NbKiYwqa9B13_EhAB/view?usp=sharing){:target="_blank"}

!!! info "Hugh Gunz and Sally Gunz, [*Ethical Challenges in the Role of In-House Counsel*, (2019) 69 Case W. Res. L. Rev. 953](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1qn6V8X7UjlvW500NbKiYwqa9B13_EhAB/view?usp=sharing){:target="_blank"}"

    **Core Thesis**
  
    Professional codes of conduct and the "bad apple" explanation are insufficient to explain ethical lapses by in-house counsel. Rather than focusing solely on outright fraud or morally bankrupt actors, the authors argue that **subtle organizational pressures, role expectations, and identity shifts** shape how ordinary, well-intentioned lawyers navigate everyday ("low-intensity") ethical dilemmas inside non-professional organizations (NPOs).
  
    - **Evolution of Ethical Decision-Making & OPC**
        - **Beyond "Bad Apples":** Blaming isolated "bad apples" lets organizations off the hook. Corporate failures often involve professionals who know ethical rules yet bend or overlook them within their workplace context.
        - **Organizational-Professional Conflict (OPC):** Employed professionals face **competing duties:** serving the public/profession's ethical standards while also owing loyalty to the employing organization.
        - **Surprising Findings:** Despite lawyers having strong professional traditions and bar oversight, empirical research shows in-house counsel often report relatively low levels of perceived OPC, potentially because they view themselves as managers, balance roles effectively, or are sidestepped by executives when sensitive issues arise.
    - **The "Doing Law" vs. "Managing" Tension**
        - **Two Polar Models:** In-house legal departments generally balance "doing law" (legal expertise and compliance) with managing (preventive planning, overseeing outside counsel, and supporting business operations).
        - **Risks of Each Extreme:**
            - Over-focusing on "doing law" risks turning the department into an insular, inward-looking "captive law firm" **disconnected from broader organizational realities.**
            - Over-focusing on management/strategy risks **blurring the lawyer's independent legal judgment**, fostering personalized loyalty to senior executives rather than the corporate entity itself, or becoming a gatekeeper that controls information flow in ways that compromise objectivity.
    - **Identity Theory, Context & "Client Capture"**
        - **Professional vs. Organizational Identity:** Drawing on identity theory and a survey of 484 Canadian corporate counsel, the authors distinguish between **a professional identity** ("How does my profession require me to handle this?") and **an organizational identity** ("How should I handle this as an employee of this organization?").
        - **What Shifts Identity:** Counsel who spent more time on non-professional/managerial work, or who felt vulnerable to having their legal work outsourced, were more likely to adopt an organizational identity and choose organizational responses in realistic ethical dilemma vignettes (including a scenario modeled on the Texaco boardroom controversy).
        - **"Client Capture":** Deep **operational integration** makes in-house counsel more useful to management in some ways, yet creates a paradox where their advice may become **less detached and objective.**
    - **Practical Takeaways**
        - **Rules Alone Aren't Enough:** Legal ethics education and compliance rules must be paired with behavioral awareness of how workplace context subtly shifts reasoning.
        - **Anticipate Low-Intensity Dilemmas:** Everyday ethical pressures happen frequently in ordinary practice; counsel must recognize environmental influences early and plan ahead for how to respond when role expectations and professional duties collide.

An important contextual factor note here, as we consider ethics, professionalism and corporate counsel, is that there is actually some **variations** in how **lawyers can represent corporations as organizations**.  It can be done as part of a lawyer’s, or a firm’s, **private practice,** which likely includes other clients.  It can also be done as **in-house counsel**, which is a lawyer being employed by the business or corporation.  In-house practice is becoming increasingly common in Canada and globally as well. 

The **Gunz** article considers these issues from the viewpoint of in-house counsel.  It does not focus on ethical transgressions by individual lawyers.  Lawyers are human and may be susceptible to misconduct where personal profit is at stake and ethics are not properly considered as an obligation rather than an option.  The article points toward a more systemic problem here rather than the individual actions of a few lawyers as “bad apples.” 

The **argument** that **Gunz & Gunz** make is a contextual one:  

> it rests **upon the contextual shifts** that occur within an organization that **affect the ethical decision-making processes** of all the professionals who work for that organization, including the lawyers.  

This is what arguably happened in many of the case studies that we will examine in this Module.  When you read the **Gunz** article consider this context and the issue of lawyers not only being willing participants in ethically dubious business practices but actually facilitating those practices through their legal expertise.

The mini-lecture that follows highlights a few of the distinctions between **working in a private a law firm with an organizational client** and **working as in-house counsel.**  This should help you frame some of the ethical considerations for each type of arrangement.  There is some **overlap** in legal ethics when representing an organization, whether you are in private law firm practice or working in-house as an employee of the organization.  But there are some important **differences** as well.

!!! info "Summary from transcript of what seems like a video lecture"

    **The Core Theme**

    Ethical failures in corporate law often happen because **how a situation is "framed"** (mentally packaged or rationalized) **narrows a person's perspective**, causing otherwise smart professionals to **overlook broader ethical duties in favor of profit, loyalty, or self-interest.**

    **In-House Counsel vs. Private Firm Lawyers**

    - **Shared baseline:** Both have the same fundamental ethical duties **(avoiding conflicts of interest, giving impartial advice, maintaining professionalism)**.
    - **Private firm lawyers:** Represent the corporation alongside other clients.
        - **Small firms / solo practitioners:** Get early client contact and hands-on work, do their own paperwork, and **face higher Law Society disciplinary rates**.
        - **Large firms:** Offer structured ethics training and close supervision by senior associates/partners as a **"safety net"** (in theory, though not always in practice).
    - **In-house counsel:** Employees with only one client (their employer).
        - **Pros:** Usually more experienced; no pressure to bring in new clients or hit billable hours; greater job stability (less financial stress that can trigger wellness/ethical lapses).
        - **Cons:** In-house ethics is a newer, less-developed field, and experience doesn't automatically prevent ethical blind spots.

    **The Enron & Arthur Andersen Lesson**
    
    - During the 2001 ***Enron*** scandal, corporate lawyers and **Arthur Andersen** lawyers failed to prevent the collapse.
    - In depositions, lawyers rationalized their inaction afterward ("I just didn't think about it that way at the time"). This is **framing in action**, rationalizing what you're doing in the moment to fit your self-image.

    **What "Framing" Is & How It Distorts Ethics**
    
    - **Definition:** How a situation is posed or viewed changes the decision, even when the facts are identical (e.g., meat labeled "75% fat-free" vs. "25% fat", or a student spinning a forgotten assignment as "I've been really busy").
    - **Real-world examples from the transcript:**
        - *Space Shuttle Challenger*: Engineers opposed launching on safety grounds until a manager told them to "put on their management hats"—reframing safety into a financial/business decision, leading them to reverse course.
        - *Enron travel expenses:* An employee ignored spending cuts and booked luxury hotels/restaurants, framing it around "I work hard and deserve this" rather than company policy.
        - *Enron financial manipulation:* CFOs and accountants framed their actions around company loyalty and boosting stock price, blinding themselves to the impact on employees' pension funds.

    **How to counter narrow framing:**
    
    - **Ask** how the decision would look on the front page of a newspaper.
    - **Seek** full information and outside perspectives from neutral third parties.
    - Actively **challenge** your own assumptions and biases.

!!! info "lecture summary"

    **Central theme:** The ***Enron*** scandal illustrates how lawyers can compromise their professional independence by **prioritizing corporate interests over ethical obligations.** The lecture also examines **whether securities regulators should have authority to discipline lawyers alongside law societies.**

    **Enron's in-house counsel**

    Enron's lawyers actively participated in creating special purpose entities (SPEs), structuring transactions, and preparing financial disclosures.

    - **Conflicts of interest:** Christina Mordaunt advised on transactions while personally investing in an SPE, earning over $1 million from a $5,800 investment.
    - **Failure to investigate:** Internal lawyers warned about accounting irregularities, but senior management and counsel dismissed their concerns without meaningful investigation.
    - **Corporate culture:** Enron rewarded aggressive risk-taking and financial success, undermining lawyers' professional independence.

    **Key lesson:** In-house lawyers must exercise independent judgment and cannot allow corporate culture or management pressure to override professional responsibilities.

    **Vinson & Elkins (V&E): Outside counsel**

    V&E was Enron's principal outside law firm and helped structure questionable transactions.

    - V&E argued that lawyers could assist with management-approved transactions provided they were not illegal.
    - The professor criticized this narrow view because corporate executives often treat legal advice as an endorsement of their decisions.
    - When executive Sharon Watkins warned about accounting irregularities, V&E was retained to investigate despite its prior involvement in the transactions.
    - The Powers Report criticized V&E's investigation as predetermined and inadequate.

    **Key lesson:** Lawyers must recognize conflicts of interest and provide genuinely independent advice. **Merely avoiding illegal conduct does not necessarily satisfy their professional obligations.**

    **Arthur Andersen and Nancy Temple**

    Arthur Andersen was Enron's accounting firm. Its lawyer, Nancy Temple, reminded employees about the firm's document retention policy when litigation and investigations were anticipated.

    Although the email appeared innocent, it potentially encouraged the destruction of relevant documents.

    Arthur Andersen was convicted of obstruction of justice in 2002, although the conviction was overturned in 2005. Its reputation and business nevertheless collapsed.

    **Key lesson:** A lawyer's advice must be evaluated in its factual context. Lawyers must not facilitate the destruction of evidence relevant to anticipated litigation.

    ***Sarbanes-Oxley Act* (2002)**

    Following ***Enron*** and other corporate scandals, the United States enacted the *Sarbanes-Oxley Act* to strengthen corporate accountability.

    Its **major reforms** included stronger **financial reporting** requirements, **document retention** obligations, **criminal penalties**, and **whistleblower protections**.

    The professor emphasizes that employees and lawyers may hesitate to report misconduct because of potential retaliation. Whistleblower protections are intended to encourage reporting and prevent similar scandals.

    ***Wilder v. Ontario:* Regulation of lawyers**

    A lawyer, Wilder, sent a deliberately misleading letter to the Ontario Securities Commission. The Commission sought to reprimand him.

    Wilder and the Law Society argued that the Law Society had exclusive jurisdiction over lawyers' professional conduct.

    The Ontario Court of Appeal rejected that argument.

    **Key principles:**

    - **Law societies do not** necessarily possess **exclusive jurisdiction** over lawyers' conduct.
    - **Securities commissions may regulate lawyers** acting within their statutory jurisdiction.
    - Solicitor-client **privilege** must remain protected, but it does **not provide blanket immunity** from regulatory proceedings.
    - Regulatory proceedings must **respect procedural fairness** and clients' substantive rights.

    The professor also raises whether lawyers should face discipline from both a law society and a securities commission for the same conduct.

    **Broader questions raised by the professor**

    The lecture invites students to consider whether professional self-regulation adequately protects the public interest, particularly when lawyers participate in corporate misconduct.

    It also questions whether overlapping regulation creates unnecessary interference with lawyers' independence or provides an important additional layer of accountability.

    The professor briefly refers to ***Canada (Attorney General) v. Federation of Law Societies of Canada*** (2015), emphasizing the constitutional importance of lawyers' commitment to their clients' causes.

    **Overall takeaway**

    The lecture develops two interconnected principles:

    - **First**, professional **independence**: **Lawyers must not become instruments of corporate misconduct.** Their responsibilities require independent judgment, meaningful attention to wrongdoing, and proper management of conflicts of interest.

    - **Second**, professional **accountability**: **Lawyers' conduct may be regulated by bodies other than law societies**, particularly securities commissions, provided that solicitor-client privilege and procedural safeguards are respected.

    The ***Enron*** scandal demonstrates why professional independence matters; ***Wilder*** illustrates how the law may enforce accountability beyond traditional professional self-regulation.

## 9.4: Values & Ethics: Government Lawyers in Canada

!!! note "Readings"

    - Jennifer Leitch, [*A Less Private Practice: Government Lawyers and Legal Ethics* (2020) 43:1 Dal LJ](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1DhQkUlSw-mlGA_18Ws6Xi4cXRxgNO6mj/view?usp=sharing){:target="_blank"}

    - Cases:
        - ***Everingham v. Ontario***, [1991 CanLII 8322 (ON CTGD)](https://canlii.ca/t/gctnh){:target="_blank"}
        - ***Everingham v. Ontario***, [1992 CanLII 7681 (ON CTGD)](https://canlii.ca/t/g1559){:target="_blank"}

Canadian lawyers working for governments perform that function in a variety of contexts.  We have already looked at the role of **Crown Prosecutor**, and their associated ethical and professional duties and obligations.  

However, government lawyers perform a variety of other roles throughout several departments in both federal and provincial governments.  These **government lawyers** can:

- bring or defend civil (non-criminal) litigation to which the government is a party;
- prosecute regulatory offences and work as decision-makers for regulatory tribunals (examples include labour, environmental or immigration tribunals);
- design and write legislation or policy recommendations;
- can negotiate government contracts;
- advise governments on international law and foreign relations; and
- they can **negotiate with First Nations** and represent the government in administrative hearings with Indigenous individuals and First Nations groups.

The issue we are considering in this topic involves the extent to which **a lawyer is a Crown employee affects their ethical obligations.**  Put another way, when the government is the client and the lawyer is the employee, can a lawyer be required to be more critical of any instructions they may receive?  

In Canada there has been very little discussion in the traditional non-criminal context of **whether government lawyers owe a “higher” ethical duty than other lawyers.**  In the lecture for this topic I have also included the federal policy of Reconciliation and how this impacts the conduct of government lawyers, particularly lawyers working for the federal government and their professional relationship and conduct with Indigenous individuals and First Nations.

For this topic, please also read the **Leitch** article.  Her article looks at government lawyers as both public servants and members of the legal profession.  She uses the case study of government lawyers’ conduct during the Indian Residential School settlement process, specially involving the case of St. Anne’s Residential School.  Some obvious problems arise here that pitted government lawyers’ role as **employees of the government vs their ethical and professional duties as lawyers.**   

!!! info "***Everingham v Ontario***"

    **Is there a Special Public Interest Duty for Government Lawyers?**

    Canadian courts have clearly held that **government lawyers are subject to the same standards of professional conduct** as lawyers generally. In the words of the Ontario Divisional Court in ***Everingham v. Ontario***:

    > It is one thing to say that a particular branch of the Crown law office or a particular law firm or lawyer has earned a reputation for a high standard of professional conduct. It is quite different to say that any lawyer or group of lawyers is subject to a higher standard of liability than that required of every lawyer under the *Rules of Professional Conduct*.

    We will examine in the lecture the ***Everingham*** decisions, the facts of the cases and government lawyers involved, and what the court said about their liability under the *Rules of Professional Conduct* vs the Public Interest duties associated with their role as Crown counsel."

!!! info "Leitch: *A Less Private Practice: Government Lawyers and Legal Ethics* (2020)"

    Central thesis: Government lawyers should have a distinct duty to seek justice, rather than simply follow the traditional adversarial model of private lawyers.

    **1, Why government lawyers are different**

    **Government lawyers** exercise public power and **must consider the rule of law and public interest**, not merely their client's immediate interests.

    Leitch argues that ordinary professional conduct rules do not adequately address these additional responsibilities.

    **2, St. Anne’s Residential School**

    Leitch uses the compensation proceedings involving survivors of St. Anne’s Residential School to illustrate the **problem**.

    - **Inadequate disclosure:** Government lawyers failed to provide important police investigation records documenting abuse.
    - **Adversarial tactics:** The government resisted disclosure and relied on technical legal arguments.
    - **Consequences:** Survivors faced delays, repeated proceedings, and difficulties obtaining compensation.
    
    **Key criticism:** The government's litigation strategy **prioritized defending claims over achieving justice and reconciliation.**

    **3, Proposed reform: A justice-seeking ethic**

    Leitch proposes **extending the justice-oriented duties of Crown prosecutors** to government lawyers handling civil litigation.

    Government lawyers should:

    - **Disclose** relevant evidence fairly.
    - **Avoid** unnecessary procedural **obstacles**.
    - Prioritize **just outcomes** over winning.
    - Consider **reconciliation** and the broader **public interest**.

    These responsibilities should be supported by clear, practical professional rules.

    **4, Challenges**
    
    Leitch acknowledges that justice is difficult to define and that government lawyers may face conflicting instructions. She therefore recommends **developing specific ethical standards rather than relying on abstract principles.**

    **Overall takeaway**

    **Government lawyers should be advocates for justice, not merely defenders of government interests.**

    The St. Anne’s case demonstrates why government lawyers need ethical responsibilities that go beyond traditional adversarial advocacy, particularly when dealing with Indigenous peoples and vulnerable claimants.

!!! info "lecture summary"

    **Central theme:** Government lawyers are subject to the **same** professional conduct standards as private lawyers, **but** their **public office may impose additional responsibilities.** These responsibilities become particularly important when government lawyers **deal with Indigenous peoples and reconciliation.**

    ***1, Evringham v. Ontario:* Professional standards for government lawyers**

    The professor discusses ***Evringham v. Ontario*** (1991–1992), which establishes an important principle:

    > **Government lawyers are subject to the same standards** under the rules of professional conduct as lawyers in private practice.

    *Facts:*

    - A government lawyer communicated directly with a represented applicant detained in a secure psychiatric facility.
    - The original motion judge found a breach of the professional rule prohibiting contact with represented parties.
    - The judge also considered government lawyers to have higher ethical obligations because they represent the public interest.

    *Decision:*

    The Divisional Court **rejected the proposition** that government lawyers should be held to **a higher standard** under the rules of professional conduct.

    However, it recognized that government lawyers **may have special public interest duties associated with their offices.**

    Although the court found no breach of the professional rule, it upheld the lawyer's removal based on the court's inherent jurisdiction to deny counsel the right of audience when the interests of justice require it.

    **Key lesson:** Government lawyers cannot be held to a higher standard of liability under professional conduct rules merely because they work for the Crown. Their public office may **nevertheless carry additional responsibilities.**

    **2, The distinction between higher and additional obligations**

    The professor emphasizes a subtle but important **distinction**.

    - **Higher professional standards:** Applying stricter professional conduct rules to government lawyers than to private lawyers. ***Evringham*** rejects this approach.
    - **Additional public interest obligations:** Responsibilities arising from government lawyers' public positions, including acting lawfully, protecting the public interest, and respecting the rule of law.

    The professor suggests that ***Evringham*** should be interpreted narrowly. Although government lawyers do not face higher standards under the professional conduct rules, they may still have additional ethical obligations arising from their public responsibilities.

    **Key lesson:** **Equal** professional conduct standards **do not** necessarily mean **identical** responsibilities.

    **3, Truth and Reconciliation Commission: Calls to Action**

    The professor identifies several important recommendations from the **Truth and Reconciliation Commission (TRC)** that affect government lawyers' **dealings with Indigenous** peoples.

    | TRC Call to Action | Main principle                                                                                                                                                                        |
    | ------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
    | 26                 | Governments should review litigation practices concerning historical abuse, including reliance on limitation defences.                                                                |
    | 42                 | Recognition and implementation of Indigenous justice systems consistent with Aboriginal and treaty rights and international principles.                                               |
    | 51                 | Government **transparency** concerning legal opinions about the scope and extent of Aboriginal and treaty rights.                                                                     |
    | 52                 | **Recognition of Aboriginal title** based on established occupation and appropriate allocation of the burden of justifying limitations on title rights.                               |
    | 57                 | **Education and professional training** for public servants concerning Indigenous history, residential schools, Aboriginal rights, intercultural competency, and conflict resolution. |

    The professor particularly emphasizes **Calls to Action 51, 52, and 57**.

    **Key lesson:** Government lawyers should consider reconciliation, transparency, Indigenous rights, and the honour of the Crown when performing their professional duties.

    **4, Government lawyers and adversarial litigation**

    The professor raises an important **ethical question**:

    > Should government lawyers **pursue every available legal argument** when litigating against Indigenous peoples?

    Traditional adversarial advocacy encourages lawyers to advance arguments vigorously in their clients' interests.

    **However**, government lawyers also represent public institutions that have constitutional and reconciliation-related responsibilities.

    The professor asks students to distinguish between several **situations**:
    
    - An Indigenous nation pursuing an Aboriginal title **claim against** the Crown.
    - An Indigenous individual involved in an **ordinary commercial dispute** with the government.
    - A residential school survivor **pursuing compensation** for historical sexual abuse.

    The professor suggests that the nature of the dispute and the government's responsibilities toward Indigenous peoples may affect how Crown counsel should conduct litigation.

    **Key lesson:** Government lawyers must consider **whether aggressive adversarial tactics are consistent with their public responsibilities**, especially in litigation involving historical injustices.

    **5, Professional ethics and reconciliation**

    The professor emphasizes that government lawyers' responsibilities **extend beyond** simply defending the government's legal position.

    **Relevant considerations** include the **honour** of the Crown, Aboriginal and treaty **rights**, the constitutional **relationship** between the Crown and Indigenous peoples, and the broader objectives of **reconciliation**.

    The lecture does **not establish that government lawyers must abandon adversarial advocacy.** Rather, it challenges students to consider how traditional advocacy obligations should operate alongside these public responsibilities.

    The professor specifically recommends reviewing the questions on page 622 of the course materials, indicating that these issues may appear in the weekly quiz.

    **Overall takeaway**

    The lecture develops two interconnected **principles**:

    - **First**, equality under professional conduct rules: Under ***Evringham***, government lawyers are subject to the **same** professional conduct standards as private lawyers. However, they may have **additional** public interest responsibilities arising from their governmental positions.

    - **Second**, ethical obligations in reconciliation: When representing the Crown in disputes involving Indigenous peoples, government lawyers must consider whether traditional **adversarial tactics are consistent** with the **honour** of the Crown, Indigenous **rights**, and **reconciliation**.

    The central question is whether government lawyers should act merely as vigorous advocates for their government clients or whether their public responsibilities require a more justice-oriented approach to legal practice.

## 9.5: Summary

This Module examined lawyers within two distinct organizational setting.  

**Corporate lawyers** have competing interests based on their role within the organization as either external or in-house counsel. 

**Government lawyers** have a similar competing ethic that differs in the explicitly public interest nature of their role.  

Is there really a difference though between lawyers in these organizations and other “regular” lawyers?  We learned in this Module that the application of **ethical and professional rules and duties applies regardless of organizational context.**  

However, that context can inform, and make more difficult, carrying out those obligations in a way consistent with corporate culture or governmental responsibilities.
