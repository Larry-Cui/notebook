## 3.1: Statutory and Code Provisions

Prior to examining the concepts and principles of advertising in the legal profession we will examine the relevant *Legal Profession Act* and B.C. **Code** provisions.  These statutory and codes sections govern your relationship as a lawyer to your client in relation to **client solicitation, legal fees and ethics**. 

**Fees & Disbursements**

The key sections of the *Legal Profession Act* and B.C. **Code** Rules to know in the area of client fees and agreements are:

- Sections **64 & 65**, which **define agreements** for legal services, contingency fees, bill, charges and other related terms.
- Sections **66 & 67**, which specifically outline the requirements pertaining to **Contingency Fee Agreements and related restrictions**.  
    - BC **Code** Rule 3.6 elaborates on fees disbursements.  They state that a lawyer who enters into a **contingent** fee agreement with a client must ensure that, under the circumstances existing at the time the agreement is entered into is **fair and reasonable**.  
    - Contingency fee agreements have to be **in writing** and there other rules relating to their form.  
    - Note that there are also **maximum amounts for contingency fee agreements for personal injury actions**. 
    - Rule 3.6 also outlines the requirements pertaining to **fee sharing agreements among lawyers**.  Fee sharing agreements occur when a lawyer meets with a client but then believes that the client would be better served by another lawyer. This will typically occur when the lawyer learns more about the client's case and discovers that it may be more complex or enters an area of law with which they do not have sufficient experience or specialization.  Fee Sharing arrangements are **fine as long as the ethics Rules 3.6-5 to 3.6-7 are followed**.  The rules mainly deal with restrictions on referrals to other lawyers and how fees may be split with another lawyer to avoid conflicts of interest.  
    - They ==**prohibit**== lawyers from entering into arrangements to **compensate or reward non-lawyers for the referral of clients**. It does ==**not prevent**== a lawyer from engaging in **promotional activities involving reasonable expenditures** on promotional items or activities that might result in the referral of clients generally by a non-lawyer.
- Section **68** allows for a client to apply for a hearing to the registrar of the court for **a review of a lawyer’s bill to a client as well as an examination of a retainer agreement**. If there is disagreement regarding the amount of a bill, **both lawyers and clients have the right** to have a lawyer’s bill reviewed by a BC Supreme Court Registrar, under Section 70 of the Act. (This is also referred to as **taxation of lawyers bills**”). **Only final bills may be reviewed** and a client may retain another lawyer to represent them in a hearing before the registrar.

**Competency and Quality of Service to Clients**

In terms of competency and quality of service in lawyer dealings for clients, the specific requirements are outlined in the B.C. **Code** in Rules 3.1 & 3.2.

**Rule 3.1 specifically addresses the competency of lawyers.** The provisions define competence using mandatory language:

> a lawyer must perform all legal services undertaken on a client’s behalf to the standard of a competent lawyer.  

This means being “**knowledgeable, skilled and capable**” in the practice of law. The reason for this provision is to reassure the public in general that the legal profession displays general competence, and the lawyer’s client in particular is entitled to assume that the lawyer has the ability and capacity to deal adequately with all of the client’s specific legal matters.  The concept of competence is based on principles of ethics and law, but this rule addresses the ethical principles. 

**Competence** involves **more than an understanding of legal principles**: it involves **an adequate knowledge of the practice and procedures** by which such principles can be ethically and effectively applied. Part of this for responsibility for lawyers involves **keeping up to date and knowledgeable about any changes** in areas of law in which you practise.

When we refer here the standard of the “**reasonably competent**” lawyer we are examining the **ethical considerations and ==not== liability for negligence**.  For ethics,  some of the factors that are analyzed here are more specific and flexible in terms of how lawyers should act on behalf of their clients.  The commentaries to this Rule note that there are many factors looked at to see if lawyers have employed the required skill or knowledge on behalf of their client, including:

- the complexity and specialized nature of the matter;
- the lawyer’s general experience;
- the lawyer’s training and experience in the field;
- the preparation and study the lawyer is able to give the matter; and
- whether it is appropriate or feasible to refer the matter to, or associate or consult with, a lawyer of established competence in the field in question.

**Rule 3.2 specifically outlines the requirements pertaining to quality of service provided to a client.**  Quality of service is related to acting as a competent lawyer.  Some of the examples given in the commentaries should be **fairly obvious matters** such as:

- keeping a client **reasonably informed** on their file;
- **responding** to a client’s telephone calls;
- **keeping appointments** with a client, or providing an explanation and apologizing for missing a meeting;
- doing promised work in a **timely manner** for maximum value to a client and providing them with **complete and accurate information** about the matter they have entrusted to you
- informing a client about the **status of their file**, including making a report to them when the matter is concluded
- **not drinking alcohol or using drugs** excessively, especially if it interferes with the lawyer’s ability to provide competent representation


**Being Civil**

The **honesty and candour requirement** also require a lawyer to be very open with a client about the real prospects for their case and occasionally requires being firm with the client.  Your opinion as a lawyer is the professional opinion.  The general principle is be **civil: firm but not rude**.

This rule also calls for out of court dispute resolution whenever possible (e.g. ADR, Mediation, etc.)  **Small claims cases generally have built in structures for mediation and claims resolution.** 

Resolution of a claim cannot generally include threatening criminal proceedings for private law actions.  The classic example here is debt collection.  Debt collectors in Canada and the United States are also known as skip tracers and are notorious for these types of threats.  **A letter from a competent lawyer in a civil debt matter should not threaten a ==criminal== proceeding** unless it is explicitly permitted by statute in certain circumstances (e.g. fraud).

This rule also **prohibits dishonesty and fraud in assisting a client**.  The language here is “**knows or ought to know**” that their client’s actions assists or encourages any dishonesty crime or fraud.  This is obviously a serious concern with criminal activities like mortgage fraud or money laundering and lawyers often legitimately provide services like purchasing or selling real estate or arranging financing for the purchase or sale of businesses or their assets. 

Finally, when a lawyer’s relationship to the client is in question because of the client's capacity (e.g. **diminished mental capacity** because of age or a medical condition) **an assessment is required** to demonstrate that they can actually understand any decisions that they are asking you to make on their behalf.  This assessment could involve a general perception of the person as having sufficient capacity to make decisions, or it could involve asking some probing questions about the matter to the person to reassure you that **there is sufficient capacity to allow you, as their lawyer, to act for them**.  Note that there is an ethical obligation to act for certain parties under some circumstances even where capacity is at issue (e.g. protection from self-harm) in order to protect a person until a legal representative can be appointed.  The Public Trustee or Guardian can also act as legal representative under some circumstances. 

## 3.2: Advertising

Ethical & Professional considerations relating to marketing and advertising of legal services in B.C. is governed by **Rule 4.2** and associated sub-rules of the **Code**.

- **Rule 4.2-3** notes that the section applies to **any “marketing activity”** undertaken or authorized by a lawyer in which they are identified as a lawyer, mediator or arbitrator.
- **Rule 4.2-4** defines “marketing activity” to **include any publication or communication** in the nature of an advertisement, promotional activity or material, letterhead, business card, listing in a directory, a public appearance or any other means by which professional legal services are promoted or clients are solicited.
- **Rule 4.2-5** outlines the **requirements for content and format** of marketing activities, stating that any marketing activity undertaken or authorized by a lawyer **must not be false, inaccurate, unverifiable, reasonably capable of misleading** the recipient/intended recipient or **contrary to the best interests of the public**.

Regarding the **formation of the lawyer-client relationship** there are certain ethical and professional requirements relevant to solicitation of clients.  Keep in mind the following key points:

- A formal legal relationship exists after a retainer agreement is signed but some **ethical obligations can arise even before retainer agreement is signed**.
- Tension often arises when **a client’s expectations** formed during the solicitation process do not meet up with the **practical obligations of the lawyer and the reality of a case**. Whether the lawyer charges more or less, and whatever they may promise, they are obligated to be a “reasonably competent lawyer.  They have to carry out this obligation within ethical and legal limits. Ethical issues sometimes arise for a lawyer when they feel pressured to go beyond ethical and possibly legal limits to fulfill a client’s expectations.

## 3.3: Solicitation & Public Appearances

In this topic we examine lawyer communications and legal ethics within the context of **client solicitation** and **public appearances**.  Both can involve sensitive situations where clients may be vulnerable or still suffering the effects of litigation many years after a final court decision.   There are some **general principles** that apply throughout Canadian jurisdictions in this area, as well as **specific** B.C. **Code** provisions that will be highlighted as well.

**Solicitation of Clients**

The solicitation of clients is one of the frequent areas where legal ethics comes into play.  It is related of course to the issue of advertising and the concerns involve taking advantage of particularly vulnerable people, particularly when a lawyer may be dealing with victims of abuse.  There are also concerns in personal injury law over so-called “ambulance chasing” and creating excessive litigation.

**On the other hand**, one can argue that vulnerable people are the very ones who might need lawyers the most to protect their rights, and that litigation, or at least the threat of litigation, is often necessary to make sure that injury victims get the damages that they are entitled to motor vehicle accidents for example.  The protection of victims' rights also figures prominently in many public appearances by lawyers.


**Case Study:** ***Law Society of Saskatchewan v Merchant*** [2000] L.S.D.D. No. 24 [Law Society of Saskatchewan Hearing Committee]

The case excerpt is in *Wooley* at p. 89-94

The facts of this case involve a prominent Canadian lawyer, **Anthony Merchant**, and potentially vulnerable victims of physical and sexual abuse.   Merchant’s law firm, the Merchant Law Group (“MLG”) wrote letters to survivors of residential schools in an attempt to solicit clients for the purposes of suing the federal government. The Law Society of Saskatchewan became involved after it received a complaint. 

There were two concerns for the Law Society to consider:

- A letter to “B” on September 3, 1998: this letter contained an Assignment and a Retainer Agreement. In the letter to B, MLG stated: “we believe the compensation that we can achieve for you will be significant and you have nothing to lose.” The Retainer Agreement said “should the client elect to change lawyers, the client will [...] pay for legal services performed up to that time [...]”. On October 23, 1998, B forwarded the letter and the Retainer to a lawyer with whom B was familiar. 
- A letter to “H” on November 20, 1998: which requested the contact information of other people who might have been victims of the residential school system. MLG said that the information provided would remain confidential. On January 6, 1999, H forwarded a formal letter of complaint to the Law Society of Saskatchewan.

The **first concern** alleged that Merchant created **an unjustified expectation** about the results that he may achieve. In the letter to B, Merchant had said that for sexual abuse, the amount of compensation could range from $50,000 to $150,000.  The Law Society held that if Merchant did not know of B’s personal circumstances then the quantum of damages was a pure speculation.  However, the letter did not create an unjustified expectation in B. Based on subsequent court decisions and the facts of B’s circumstances, the speculated damages did fall into a range of possible outcomes.  B had also stated that no amount of compensation could compensate her anyway.

The **second concern** of the complaint involved an allegation that the letters were reasonably capable of misleading the intended recipient. Here, the Law Society held that **Merchant was guilty of conduct unbecoming of a lawyer because the correspondence was capable of misleading the parties.** The letter disregarded the possibility of the recipients not having sustainable causes of action; failed to explain the complexity/length of litigation; the letter was misleading when read in conjunction with the Retainer; referring to the Retainer as an authorization was misleading. Additionally, in the letter to H it was said that the contact information provided to MLG would be confidential.  The Law Society stated that this is misleading because the information may become compellable evidence during the course of litigation.

The Law Society further held that that Merchant was guilty of **conduct unbecoming of a lawyer** in that he undertook marketing activities which were **“undignified, in bad taste, offensive and inimical to the best interest of the public or the members, or tending to harm the standing of the legal profession.”**  The letters were held to be “undignified” as they made assumptions about the recipients of the letters. However, the hearing committee did not have the power to subject Merchant to any discipline on this issue because of a problem with the wording of the Rule.

Merchant was ordered to pay a $5000.00 fee and $10,000.00 in costs. Merchant appealed the decision, and it was affirmed by the Saskatchewan Court of Appeal in ***Law Society of Saskatchewan v Merchant*** [2002] SJ No 288, 2002 SKCA.

**Communications with the Public**

The example in the *Wooley* text at page 89 of the **NAACP** in the United States is interesting because it shows how a civil organization used **tactics** that southern American law societies labelled “**unethical**” at the time.  However, they were necessary to ensure that African-Americans knew their legal rights.  This was a period when African Americans suffered state sanctioned segregation and discrimination.  Others suffered even worse abuse like beatings and lynchings. The **NAACP** spoke to audiences of African Americans recruit and fundraising for test cases and even provoking confrontations with police to create facts for test cases.  At the time in Mississippi considered unethical by the Mississippi State Bar association.

In the Canadian context, communications with the public and public appearances are regulated by the ethics codes.  In B.C., **Rule 7.5** outlines the requirements for public appearances and public statements.  Generally, **as long as there is no infringement of lawyer’s obligations** to the client, and to the general obligations to the profession, the courts, or the administration of justice, **a lawyer is allowed to communicate information to the media and make public appearances and statements.** They should behave in the same manner as they do with their clients, their fellow practitioners, the courts, and tribunals. Dealings with the media are treated as an extension of the lawyer’s conduct in a professional capacity.

The most important duty here is to the client.  Before making a public statement concerning the client's affairs, the lawyer must first be satisfied that **any communication is in the best interests of the client and within the scope of the retainer.**  Public communications about a client’s affairs should **not be used for the purpose of publicizing the lawyer** and should be free from any suggestion that a lawyer’s real purpose is self-promotion or self-aggrandizement. 

Some interesting questions can be raised here:  How do you separate the “best interests of the client” and “self-promotion” of the lawyer?  Do you need to separate them?  In many cases, the answer may be no.  It some cases it may involve whether the lawyer ought to have known that such an appearance may cause the client some distress, affect their personal or professional lives by bringing more attention to their case, and not be in the client’s best interests. And in others, **the issue of obtaining the client's consent becomes key**, particularly in Canada.


**Case Study:**  ***Stewart v. Canadian Broadcasting Corp.***, [1997 CanLII 12318 (ON CTGD)](https://canlii.ca/t/1w5hb){:target=" \_blank"}

Discussed in *Woolley* at p. 95, full decision provided above.

The facts of this case involved a prominent lawyer, **Edward Greenspan**, and a former client.  From 1979-1981, **Greenspan** had acted as counsel for **Stewart**’s charge and conviction of criminal negligence causing death. 10 years later **Greenspan** appeared on a CBC TV show which caused renewed interest in **Stewart**’s matter. **Stewart** brought a claim against **Greenspan** for a breach of his fiduciary duty of loyalty.  The court took the opportunity to comment on **a lawyer’s obligations to former clients** when in the media by stating that a lawyer **must not engage in behavior that is motivated by self-promotion or self-aggrandizement**.

In ***Jaber v. Canada (Citizenship and Immigration)***, [2026 FC 179](https://canlii.ca/t/kj2sd){:target=" \_blank"}, a refugee claimant alleged that his former immigration representative breached duties of competence, loyalty, and procedural fairness by allegedly altering evidence, failing to disclose a procedural fairness letter, representing both spouses despite a possible conflict of interest, and failing to advance certain legal arguments. The Federal Court ultimately dismissed the application because the claimant could not show a reasonable probability that the outcome would have been different absent the alleged misconduct.

Should Canadian courts require refugee claimants alleging ineffective assistance of counsel to prove that the outcome likely would have changed, or is the existence of serious professional misconduct itself enough to undermine confidence in the fairness of the proceeding?

In answering you may consider any of the following but please come to a clear conclusion:

- the ethical duties of competence, candour and loyalty;
- the vulnerability of refugee claimants and the power imbalance in lawyer-client relationships;
- whether the “reasonable probability of a different outcome” test adequately protects procedural fairness;
- the practical consequences of lowering or maintaining the threshold for setting aside administrative decisions based on counsel misconduct;
- whether ethical obligations should be interpreted differently in refugee law compared with other areas of practice

!!! quote "Answer"

    The issue raised by the application in ***Jaber*** is whether he “was denied procedural fairness and natural justice in his appeal before the RAD (and also the case before RPD) because of the Consultant’s (a) breach of trust, loyalty, honesty and conflict of interest; and (b) Incompetence.”

    In ***Jaber***, paras [10-11](https://www.canlii.org/en/ca/fct/doc/2026/2026fc179/2026fc179.html#par10){:target=" \_blank"}, the Court held that,

    > To establish that counsel’s incompetence resulted in a breach of procedural fairness, an applicant must satisfy the following tripartite test: (a) the representative’s alleged acts or omissions constituted incompetence; (b) there was a miscarriage of justice in the sense that, but for the alleged conduct, there is a reasonable probability that the result of the original hearing would have been different; and (c) the representative was given notice and a reasonable opportunity to respond (***Stephen v Canada (Citizenship and Immigration)***, [2019 FC 1331](https://www.canlii.org/en/ca/fct/doc/2019/2019fc1331/2019fc1331.html){:target=" \_blank"} at para 11, citing ***Mcintyre*** at para [33](https://www.canlii.org/en/ca/fct/doc/2016/2016fc1351/2016fc1351.html#par33){:target=" \_blank"}). The second branch of the test is determinative of this application.

    Because the applicant did not provide any new evidence to rule out the IFA possibility, either before the RPD, RAD, or during the judicial review process, the Court concluded that he did not satisfy the second branch of the test and dismissed the application.

    I agree with the decision because the tripartite test seems to me like a reasonable balance between efficiency and fairness.

    Admittedly, the applicant’s consultant behaved unprofessionally, at least at some points during the retainer. But if the consultant had handled the applicant’s matter in a more competent way, would the result actually have been different? Maybe, or maybe not.

    If the courts allowed every unhappy client to bring an application simply because their consultant made a mistake or did not handle the file perfectly, while ignoring the second prong of the test, there could be a flood of cases. Most failed applicants might see judicial review as the last course of remedy, even when the consultant’s mistake was relatively minor. It would also be pretty difficult, if not impossible, to draw a clear line between a minor mistake and a major mistake that would justify judicial review.

    On the other hand, the tripartite test still leaves room for cases that have real merit. In fact, during the hearing, the applicant was given an opportunity to present any new evidence that he claimed he had failed to submit because of the consultant’s incompetence.

    I think if the applicant had presented new evidence that gave the Court a reasonable basis to believe the result could have been different, the Court might have allowed the application. That way, procedural fairness is still protected without making every mistake by a representative grounds for judicial review.

## 3.4: Choice of Client Representation & Withdrawal

!!! note "Readings"

    - Chapter 3: The Lawyer-Client Relationship, Pages 147-176;184-191 (2017 Edition), OR
    - Chapter 2: The Lawyer-Client Relationship **Pages 77-107; 115-137 (4th Edition)**
    - Code: 6.3; 3.7

For our final topic in this Module, we will bookend the lawyer-client relationship by examining the procedure and requirements for **choosing to represent** a client, and the considerations involved when considering **a withdrawal from representing** a client.  

Once you get beyond reaching out for clients through advertising or soliciting publicly, taking on a client is the next step and as Hutchinson says in the readings it's arguably the most important step.  It is the equivalent of taking on a new job or in some complex cases even getting married in the sense that it creates a whole new relationship that is then governed by ethical rules and moral obligations.  **Some decisions to refuse to take on a client should be clear.**  An example would be if you know there would be **a conflict of interest** in representing someone or some entity.  Once you get beyond these clear scenarios then you wade into the debate between these **two principles of “moral non-accountability” vs “taking it personally.”**

**Choice of Client**

There is **a general right of course to decline** client representation, subject to certain limitations to **prevent discrimination and promote access to justice** for individuals who may have no other recourse for legal representation.  The choice of clients is subject to the anti-discrimination provisions in the **Code**.

The **Code** in Rule **6.3** deals with harassment and discrimination in choosing to represent a client.  As Hutchinson notes in the readings, client selection can be one of the most important decisions that a lawyer make.  Once a lawyer decides to represent a client, the lawyer becomes committed to a whole host of moral and ethical obligations. Rule **6.3-5** states more specifically that “a lawyer must not discriminate against any person.”

This is where we turn to these concepts of **“moral non-accountability” and “taking it personally.”**   In this context we are situating these concepts when choosing **whether or not to legally represent a potential client**.  

As noted in the readings and statements from **Layton** and **Proulx**, these are important considerations and perhaps **the best way to look at it is to take a “middle ground” approach.**  

A lawyer’s legitimate exercise of personal morality should not be taken into consideration because their personal views may affect their decision as to whether or not to accept a potential client. However, a lawyer should reject a retainer where they can conclude that their quality of representation would suffer because of their personal distaste concerning a potential client.  

It is important to note that in more serious cases, particularly in criminal law, the potential client will be facing a serious impingement on the individual’s liberty. **Access to the protections offered by the legal systems, access to justice, through the help of counsel is an essential element of how the system operates.** The strength of this interest must not be ignored when considering whether or not to represent a potential client. If a lawyer is not striking a middle ground in client selection it can lead to several issues such as clients with less popular causes needing to pay more for legal services. This would potentially lead to legal services in Canada becoming even more expensive and more of an economic/business industry. 

The other extreme of course is the principle of not taking certain cases unless you truly believe in them because otherwise you would not be able to offer adequate representation. Again, **the idea of the middle ground approach would seek to balance these often-competing interests**.

**Legal ethics and client representation**

As a lawyer, how do you approach defending clients?  How do you approach defending clients you may find personally objectionable?  Legal ethicists generally divide approaches to legal defense into **two approaches**: 

- Moral Non-Accountability, and
- Taking it Personally

**Legal Defense & Moral Non-Accountability: "When the Nazis Came to Skokie"**

**"Moral non-accountability"** is the principle that **emphasizes the lawyer’s role in society over personal moral imperatives.**  There is no personal consideration of morality in taking on legal representation for accused child abuser, for example, because you are doing society a social good in defending someone against unproven allegations.  In such a case even when the allegations are proven you are there to make sure that the punishment is as fair as it can be for your client. This principle is embodied in **Rule 2.1-3** in the context of criminal proceedings.  The **Code** specifically states that it is a lawyer’s right to undertake the defence of a person accused of crime, **regardless of the lawyer’s own personal opinion** as to the guilt of the accused. 

<div class="highlight red" markdown="1">Once a client is retained however, **the lawyer is bound to present, by all fair and honourable means and in a manner consistent with the client’s instructions, every defence that the law of the land permits, to the end that no person will be convicted except by due process of law.**</div>

A good example of this philosophy of lawyers and moral non-accountability can be drawn from the American Civil Liberties Union in the United States (ACLU). The ACLU's "Why We Do What We Do" page explains the organization's legal philosophy in defense of certain people or groups:

> We do not defend them because we agree with them. **Rather we defend their right to free expression and free assembly**.

> Historically, the people whose opinions are the most controversial or extreme are the people whose rights are most often threatened. **Once the government has the power to violate one person’s rights, it can use that power against everyone.** We work to stop the erosion of civil liberties before it’s too late.

In summary, morally non-accountability requires **NOT** taking it personally when you defend the most objectively reprehensible individual or organization.

**Legal Defense & Taking it Personally**

The “Taking it Personally” approach to client representation acknowledges these societal values are important but leaves a greater role for subjective individual humanity.   

Although the taking it personally view seems more self-serving it can be argued in many instances that it is not.  For example, if a lawyer knows that their own personal distaste for a particular client or action is such that it would impede their ability to successfully defend them, and they know that alternate representation is likely available to that client, then **they are probably doing the client, and society, a service by refusing to take them on.**    

It can also be argued that, in some instances, lawyers who use the societal good argument for not taking moral accountability for their selection of clients are also being self-serving, if the client happens to be wealthy or would provide national publicity, for example.

**Withdrawing from Client Representation.**

Notwithstanding the importance of access to justice and the principle of competent legal representation being universally available, there are situations where **continuing to provide effective legal representation** to a client becomes extremely **difficult**, if not impossible.  It is important to remember that the client has the right to terminate the lawyer-client relationship at will, a lawyer does not enjoy the same freedom of action. **Once there is a client representation that is undertaken, it should be completed as competently as possible.** 

**Rule 3.7** deals with withdrawal from representation.  **3.7-1** specifically notes that a lawyer “must not withdraw from representation of a client **except for good cause and on reasonable notice to the client**.”  There are **provincial and superior court rules that govern adequate notice for withdrawal**.  

An essential element of reasonable notice is **notification to the client**, unless the client cannot be located after what are considered “reasonable efforts.”  The **Code** commentaries note that “no hard and fast rules can be laid down” in defining reasonable notice before withdrawal and the time period for withdrawal after notification will depend on facts and relevant circumstances and context of the case. 

**The general guidelines are that the timing of the withdrawal should not be at a critical stage of the case whenever possible or timed to put the client into a more disadvantageous position.**  The general rule is that the **client should be given enough time to find replacement counsel**. 

Law Society of B.C. “Procedure for Withdrawing Services” outlines some basic notice and procedural requirements relating to withdrawing legal services.  It also notes there may be other statutory requirements as well depending on the circumstances.

In relation to what constitutes **“good cause”** for lawyer withdrawal, **there is no clear statutory or code definition.**  Apart from instances where a lawyer is clearly discharged by a client, it can sometimes be unclear when withdrawal is permitted.  However, many instances where withdrawal is permitted are clear and even mandatory.  **Rule 3.7-7** briefly outlines these **circumstances**.   

The Lawyers Insurance Association of Nova Scotia goes into more detail and outlines some definite circumstances where a lawyer **must withdraw** from representing a client:

- the client persists in instructing the lawyer to act contrary to professional ethics;
- the lawyer is instructed by the client to do something that is inconsistent with the lawyer’s duty to the court;
- the lawyer’s continued representation of the client will lead to a breach of the Rules of Professional Conduct; or
- the lawyer is not competent to handle the case

Some of these considerations fall into B.C. **Code** **Rule 3.7-2**, which deals with optional withdrawal when there has been a “serious loss of confidence” between a lawyer and their client.    It is generally **inappropriate** for a lawyer to withdraw on what are considered **“capricious or arbitrary grounds.”**  

However, **Rule 3.7-3** states that a lawyer is **permitted to withdraw for non-payment** of fees after reasonable notice is given of the non-payment.  With reasonable notice you can withdraw for non-payment of fees.  The **situation is a bit different in criminal matters**, the court may exercise its discretion to refuse counsel’s withdrawal. The court’s order refusing counsel’s withdrawal can even be enforced by the court’s contempt power. 

This is from a 2010 Supreme Court of Canada case, ***R. v. Cunningham***, that we will examine in an upcoming Module when we review confidentiality provisions.

There are specific considerations for withdrawal from criminal proceedings due to non-payment of fees, or other causes, because of the general greater legal peril and potential consequences for a client.  They are noted in **Rule 3.7-4**.  There is a requirement for the lawyer to provide written notification to the client that they are withdrawing because the fees have not been paid or for other adequate cause, and accounts to the client for any monies received on account of fees and disbursements.  There is also an obligation to notify Crown counsel in writing that you are no longer acting for the client and in some cases notify the clerk or registrar of the appropriate court in writing that you are no longer for the client.

## 3.5: Summary

We have reviewed in this Module how the **lawyer-client relationship** is formed and the obligations that then ensue to the lawyer.  

**Advertising and client solicitation** in Canada are areas that have seen some lawyers push the limits of ethical requirements, in some cases on the premise of advancing the rights of victims to legal representations.  

The Code provisions here are heavily influenced by conceptions of **access to justice** for all individuals, balanced with the need to provide competent legal representation.  There is an implicit acknowledgement that, on occasion, a lawyer’s personal feelings may be so strong that client representation could be compromised.  

**Choice of client** representation and **withdrawal from representation** are two areas that can present difficult ethical and professional considerations for lawyers in certain cases.
