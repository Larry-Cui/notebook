## 3.1 Airspace

Land surveys usually describe property in two dimensions on a map or survey plan. However, in order for us to physically occupy land, we need the capacity to use something more than a two-dimensional plane. As a result, **a property interest in land that provides a right to possession must include some right to use the airspace above, and/or some right to use the soil beneath.** Remember Jeremy Waldon’s observation (Unit 2) in “Homelessness and the Issue of Freedom” that we are “embodied beings, we always have a location.” But how high do property interests extend? And how low? And with what qualifications? This section considers the height of interests in land.

**A. How High?**

The starting point in determining the extent to which the ownership of land extends upwards is the Latin maxim:

<div class="highlight red" markdown="1">***Cujus est solum ejus est usque ad coelum et ad inferos***</div>

The translation: “**Whoever owns the soil also owns from the heavens to hell.**”

You will see that ***Didow v Alberta Power*** begins with this maxim, and so do the cases that Justice **Haddad** refers to in the decision. However, as you will also see in the analysis, **the maxim is qualified, at least in respect of the extent to which ownership reaches to the heavens**.

One of the lines of argument in ***Didow*** revolves around the distinction between the torts of trespass and nuisance. Both trespass and nuisance involve a claim that an activity interferes with the property interest of another. 

The important distinction is that **nuisance involves harm or interference in the use of property**; **trespass involves interference with possession, but not necessarily use.** A complainant may establish trespass by showing physical presence in the property of another; nuisance is made out when one party creates an unreasonable interference in a neighbour’s use of their property.

The topic concludes with a brief discussion of the **subdivision of property interests in a vertical column**. This was made possible with legislation that enables the subdivision of land into air space parcels, and further with condominium or strata property legislation that enables subdivision of parcels of land, including airspace parcels, into condominium units or strata lots. 

**Condominium** legislation, which provinces and states across North America introduced in the 1960s, produces **a particular package of rights**: a private interest in a unit in a multi-unit building, an undivided share of the common property, and a right to participate in the collective governance of private and common property. 

Airspace parcels and condominium have facilitated massive increases in the density of private property interests. Where there was once a single private interest, there may now be several hundred stacked in a vertical column. We will return to condominium in **Unit 11 Shared Ownership**.

## 3.2 Subsurface

How far does the ownership of land extend underground? The starting point, as in the analysis of airspace, is the Latin maxim, ***Cujus est solum ejus est usque ad coelum et ad inferos***, and its declaration of expansive interests below the surface for the person who owns the surface. We saw in Topic 1 that the maxim has been curtailed with respect to airspace. Here we consider the extent to which it applies under the surface.

The famous decision of the Kentucky Court of Appeal in ***Edwards v Sims*** reveals a dispute over a cave, **the Great Onyx Cave**, and whether it belonged to its discoverer or to that person who owned the surface immediately above it. The question became important in the 1910s and 1920s in Kentucky because the caves had become a marketable tourist attraction.

## 3.3 Lateral Boundaries – Land

The definition of the lateral boundaries of interests in land was once the domain of community practice and based primarily on patterns of use. Boundaries came to be formalized as nation/states began surveying and recording property interests in central registries. As a result, **common law doctrine is relatively unimportant in defining lateral boundaries, except in a few areas of dispute or where surveying is incomplete.** However, the common law does have a say about the responsibilities of neighbouring owners when it comes to the **issue of support**.

At common law, landowners have an absolute right to the support of their land in a natural state. **A neighbour whose activities cause a property owner’s land to subside is liable for the damage caused.** The excavating party does not have a defence in establishing that it has been careful or met the necessary standard of care. Due diligence is not a defence. Or at least, that has been the rule.

## 3.4 Lateral Boundaries – Water

The common law may not be much involved in determining boundaries between adjoining neighbours where the boundary is on land. However, it is involved where the boundary is marked by a body of water. Here we consider the boundaries of coastal states in international law, the doctrines of accretion and avulsion, and the rights of riparian owners.

**A. Boundaries of Coastal States in International Law**

The [United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea (UNCLOS)](https://www.un.org/Depts/los/convention_agreements/convention_overview_convention.htm){:target=" \_blank"} creates a number of concentric spheres with varying rights within each.

**Using the low-water mark as the “baseline”**, all waters within the baseline are known as **“internal waters”** and over which Coastal states are fully sovereign (UNCLOS Article 7). A Coastal state’s **“territorial sea” extends 12 nautical miles from the baseline** (Article 3). These are considered as waters within the Coastal state, subject to **a right of innocent passage** (Article 17).

The area **12-24 nautical miles** from a Coastal state’s baseline is known as the **“contiguous zone”**. A Coastal state may exercise control within its contiguous zone to the extent necessary to **enforce its customs, fiscal, immigration, or sanitary laws** within its territory (including territorial sea) (Article 33). 

Finally, Coastal states enjoy an **“exclusive economic zone”** within a **200-mile limit** and in which they hold sovereign rights for the purpose of exploring and exploiting, and conserving and managing natural resources (Article 56). **Beyond the 200-mile** limit, the oceans are understood to be the **“common heritage”** of humankind (Articles. 136-137).

If you are interested in Canada’s attempts to establish an extended EEZ based on continental shelves, then see Fisheries and Oceans Canada discussion of “[Sovereignty and UNCLOS](https://www.dfo-mpo.gc.ca/science/hydrography-hydrographie/UNCLOS/index-eng.html){:target=" \_blank"}” (8 May, 2019).

!!! info ""

    Accretion and avulsion are legal terms for **how water changes property boundaries over time**, differing mainly by the speed and visibility of the change.

    **Accretion**
    
    - Definition: The slow, gradual buildup of land from sand, soil, or sediment deposited by water.
    - Process: It happens so slowly that you cannot see it happening day by day. The added material itself is called alluvion or alluvium.
    - Property Effect: The legal **property boundary moves right along with the water line.** The owner of the shore or riverbank gains the new land.
    
    **Avulsion**
    
    - Definition: The sudden, rapid tearing away or shifting of land caused by a violent natural event like a flood, hurricane, or earthquake.
    - Process: It changes the landscape almost overnight, such as a river violently carving a brand-new channel through a property.
    - Property Effect: The legal boundary does not move. The **original owner keeps the title to the displaced land**, even if it is now sitting on the other side of a new river stream.

**C. Riparian Rights at Common Law**

At common law, the holder of interest in land that adjoins a body of fresh water **also holds a right to the natural flow of the water** in the watercourse. This interest is **severable**, meaning it can be separated from the interest in land, but the presumption is that the right to the natural flow accompanies the interest in land. 

Each property owner along a watercourse holds a similar right to the natural flow so **one owner’s use cannot diminish another’s right** to the natural flow. This regime developed in a context there was plenty of water and use was relatively light (pre-industrial England); **it works less well where water is scarce or where use becomes more intense.**

In addition to a right to the natural flow, the presumption at common law is that **the owner of land adjacent to a body of water also owns the solum or the bed of the body of water to the midpoint**. This is known as the ==***ad medium filum aquae*** **doctrine**==. An owner who owns land on both sides of the body of water, owns the solum across the body of water.

**In England, this presumption only applied to non-navigable waters,** which were understood in law as **non-tidal waters**. In short, the presumption only applied in non-tidal waters. For all non-tidal lakes, rivers, streams, or bodies of surface water, the owner of the adjacent land was presumed to own the solum to the midpoint of the body of water.  **Where land abutted tidal waters, the rights of ownership extended to the high-water mark.**

The common law also presumes that **the owner of the solum also holds the right to fish in the waters** above it. The owner of the solum does not own the fish in those waters, but instead holds **the right to catch fish**. The fish themselves are not owned until caught.

**The Crown holds the right to fish in tidal waters, but ownership is constrained by a doctrine known as the public right to fish.** Because of the public right to fish, the Crown may not allocate exclusive fisheries except where Parliament grants it the authority to allocate exclusive fisheries.

## 3.5 Fixtures

**Fixtures are things that exist at and, in effect, mark the boundary between** that which is land (**real property**) and that which is not land (**chattels**). The law of fixtures has developed to help identify the point at which chattels become land. **‘Chattel’ is the legal term for a privately-owned thing that is moveable.** 

The **test** to determine whether something is a fixture, and therefore considered part of the real property, revolves around **the degree of attachment**.

Defining the boundaries of land and chattels is important when land is being transferred and it becomes essential to know exactly what is being transferred.

The common law developed **a two-part test of “objective intention”** to mark the **boundary** between land and chattels:

**1, What is the degree of annexation?**

Under the common law, chattels are presumed to be part of the land if they are attached to the land, no matter how slight the attachment. Conversely, objects are presumed to be chattels if resting on their own weight and unattached. The strength of the presumption depends on the degree of attachment. **The greater the connections, the more likely the objects are to be part of the land.**

**2, What is the object of annexation?**

Either presumption may be rebutted in the second part of the test, which looks to the **object or purpose of the attachment**. Was the chattel attached to enhance the land (and therefore become a fixture), or for the better use of the chattel as chattel?
