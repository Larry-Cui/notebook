## 3.1 Airspace

Land surveys usually describe property in two dimensions on a map or survey plan. However, in order for us to physically occupy land, we need the capacity to use something more than a two-dimensional plane. As a result, **a property interest in land that provides a right to possession must include some right to use the airspace above, and/or some right to use the soil beneath.** Remember Jeremy Waldon’s observation (Unit 2) in “Homelessness and the Issue of Freedom” that we are “embodied beings, we always have a location.” But how high do property interests extend? And how low? And with what qualifications? This section considers the height of interests in land.

**A. How High?**

The starting point in determining the extent to which the ownership of land extends upwards is the Latin maxim:

<div class="highlight red" markdown="1">***Cujus est solum ejus est usque ad coelum et ad inferos***</div>

The translation: “**Whoever owns the soil also owns from the heavens to hell.**”

You will see that ***Didow v Alberta Power*** begins with this maxim, and so do the cases that Justice **Haddad** refers to in the decision. However, as you will also see in the analysis, **the maxim is qualified, at least in respect of the extent to which ownership reaches to the heavens**.

One of the lines of argument in ***Didow*** revolves around the distinction between the torts of trespass and nuisance. Both trespass and nuisance involve a claim that an activity interferes with the property interest of another. 

The important distinction is that **nuisance involves harm or interference in the use of property**; **trespass involves interference with possession, but not necessarily use.** A complainant may establish trespass by showing physical presence in the property of another; nuisance is made out when one party creates an unreasonable interference in a neighbour’s use of their property.

The topic concludes with a brief discussion of the **subdivision of property interests in a vertical column**. This was made possible with legislation that enables the subdivision of land into air space parcels, and further with condominium or strata property legislation that enables subdivision of parcels of land, including airspace parcels, into condominium units or strata lots. 

**Condominium** legislation, which provinces and states across North America introduced in the 1960s, produces **a particular package of rights**: a private interest in a unit in a multi-unit building, an undivided share of the common property, and a right to participate in the collective governance of private and common property. 

Airspace parcels and condominium have facilitated massive increases in the density of private property interests. Where there was once a single private interest, there may now be several hundred stacked in a vertical column. We will return to condominium in **Unit 11 Shared Ownership**.

But a low-flying drone does intrude into the airspace that Justice Griffiths described as within the necessary height for the landowner’s use, thus trespassing on Watson’s land.

    The neighbour trespasses on Watson’s land by flying the drone over his property at a height reserved for Mr. Watson’s full enjoyment.

## 3.3 Lateral Boundaries – Land

The definition of the lateral boundaries of interests in land was once the domain of community practice and based primarily on patterns of use. Boundaries came to be formalized as nation/states began surveying and recording property interests in central registries. As a result, **common law doctrine is relatively unimportant in defining lateral boundaries, except in a few areas of dispute or where surveying is incomplete.** However, the common law does have a say about the responsibilities of neighbouring owners when it comes to the **issue of support**.

At common law, landowners have an absolute right to the support of their land in a natural state. **A neighbour whose activities cause a property owner’s land to subside is liable for the damage caused.** The excavating party does not have a defence in establishing that it has been careful or met the necessary standard of care. Due diligence is not a defence. Or at least, that has been the rule.

## 3.4 Lateral Boundaries – Water

The common law may not be much involved in determining boundaries between adjoining neighbours where the boundary is on land. However, it is involved where the boundary is marked by a body of water. Here we consider the boundaries of coastal states in international law, the doctrines of accretion and avulsion, and the rights of riparian owners.

**A. Boundaries of Coastal States in International Law**

The [United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea (UNCLOS)](https://www.un.org/Depts/los/convention_agreements/convention_overview_convention.htm){:target=" \_blank"} creates a number of concentric spheres with varying rights within each.

**Using the low-water mark as the “baseline”**, all waters within the baseline are known as **“internal waters”** and over which Coastal states are fully sovereign (UNCLOS Article 7). A Coastal state’s **“territorial sea” extends 12 nautical miles from the baseline** (Article 3). These are considered as waters within the Coastal state, subject to **a right of innocent passage** (Article 17).

The area **12-24 nautical miles** from a Coastal state’s baseline is known as the **“contiguous zone”**. A Coastal state may exercise control within its contiguous zone to the extent necessary to **enforce its customs, fiscal, immigration, or sanitary laws** within its territory (including territorial sea) (Article 33). 

Finally, Coastal states enjoy an **“exclusive economic zone”** within a **200-mile limit** and in which they hold sovereign rights for the purpose of exploring and exploiting, and conserving and managing natural resources (Article 56). **Beyond the 200-mile** limit, the oceans are understood to be the **“common heritage”** of humankind (Articles. 136-137).

If you are interested in Canada’s attempts to establish an extended EEZ based on continental shelves, then see Fisheries and Oceans Canada discussion of “[Sovereignty and UNCLOS](https://www.dfo-mpo.gc.ca/science/hydrography-hydrographie/UNCLOS/index-eng.html){:target=" \_blank"}” (8 May, 2019).

!!! info ""

    Accretion and avulsion are legal terms for **how water changes property boundaries over time**, differing mainly by the speed and visibility of the change.

    **Accretion**
    
    - Definition: The slow, gradual buildup of land from sand, soil, or sediment deposited by water.
    - Process: It happens so slowly that you cannot see it happening day by day. The added material itself is called alluvion or alluvium.
    - Property Effect: The legal **property boundary moves right along with the water line.** The owner of the shore or riverbank gains the new land.
    
    **Avulsion**
    
    - Definition: The sudden, rapid tearing away or shifting of land caused by a violent natural event like a flood, hurricane, or earthquake.
    - Process: It changes the landscape almost overnight, such as a river violently carving a brand-new channel through a property.
    - Property Effect: The legal boundary does not move. The **original owner keeps the title to the displaced land**, even if it is now sitting on the other side of a new river stream.

**C. Riparian Rights at Common Law**

At common law, the holder of interest in land that adjoins a body of fresh water **also holds a right to the natural flow of the water** in the watercourse. This interest is **severable**, meaning it can be separated from the interest in land, but the presumption is that the right to the natural flow accompanies the interest in land. 

Each property owner along a watercourse holds a similar right to the natural flow so **one owner’s use cannot diminish another’s right** to the natural flow. This regime developed in a context there was plenty of water and use was relatively light (pre-industrial England); **it works less well where water is scarce or where use becomes more intense.**

In addition to a right to the natural flow, the presumption at common law is that **the owner of land adjacent to a body of water also owns the solum or the bed of the body of water to the midpoint**. This is known as the ==***ad medium filum aquae*** **doctrine**==. An owner who owns land on both sides of the body of water, owns the solum across the body of water.

**In England, this presumption only applied to non-navigable waters,** which were understood in law as **non-tidal waters**. In short, the presumption only applied in non-tidal waters. For all non-tidal lakes, rivers, streams, or bodies of surface water, the owner of the adjacent land was presumed to own the solum to the midpoint of the body of water.  **Where land abutted tidal waters, the rights of ownership extended to the high-water mark.**

The common law also presumes that **the owner of the solum also holds the right to fish in the waters** above it. The owner of the solum does not own the fish in those waters, but instead holds **the right to catch fish**. The fish themselves are not owned until caught.

**The Crown holds the right to fish in tidal waters, but ownership is constrained by a doctrine known as the public right to fish.** Because of the public right to fish, the Crown may not allocate exclusive fisheries except where Parliament grants it the authority to allocate exclusive fisheries.

## 3.5 Fixtures

**Fixtures are things that exist at and, in effect, mark the boundary between** that which is land (**real property**) and that which is not land (**chattels**). The law of fixtures has developed to help identify the point at which chattels become land. **‘Chattel’ is the legal term for a privately-owned thing that is moveable.** 

The **test** to determine whether something is a fixture, and therefore considered part of the real property, revolves around **the degree of attachment**.

Defining the boundaries of land and chattels is important when land is being transferred and it becomes essential to know exactly what is being transferred.

The common law developed **a two-part test of “objective intention”** to mark the **boundary** between land and chattels:

**1, What is the degree of annexation?**

Under the common law, chattels are presumed to be part of the land if they are attached to the land, no matter how slight the attachment. Conversely, objects are presumed to be chattels if resting on their own weight and unattached. The strength of the presumption depends on the degree of attachment. **The greater the connections, the more likely the objects are to be part of the land.**

**2, What is the object of annexation?**

Either presumption may be rebutted in the second part of the test, which looks to the **object or purpose of the attachment**. Was the chattel attached to enhance the land (and therefore become a fixture), or for the better use of the chattel as chattel?

## 3.6 Tangible and Intangible Property

This section introduces two of the three principal forms of intellectual property (IP): **copyright and patent**. In doing so, it explores the boundary between forms of expression that receive protection and those that do not. It leaves the third principal form, **trademark**, for upper year courses in intellectual property.

**A. Copyright**

**Copyright protects a variety of forms of original expression.** 

**It does not protect information or ideas, but does protect the expression of that information or those ideas.** The holder of copyright has the sole right to produce or reproduce the work covered.

Copyright is a creature of statute, the *Copyright Act*, [RSC 1985, c C-42](https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/C-42/index.html){:target=" \_blank"}. The *Act* defines the rights of copyright holders and establishes certain defences that **allow for certain uses of material protected by copyright**. 

However, the *Act* says nothing about the **purpose** of copyright. The courts have filled this void, until the 1990s by specifying that copyright served to protect authors. In ***Théberge v Galerie d’Art du Petit Champlain***, the SCC described a different purpose: copyright legislation was to **balance just reward for creators with the promotion of the public interest**.

Canada’s *Copyright Act* establishes a number of conditions for the subsistence of copyright in works, including the requirement that the work be original and the result of the exercise of skill and judgment. 

There is a **Register of Copyrights** in Canada, but no additional act beyond creation is required to secure copyright protection; **it accrues automatically provided the criteria of the *Copyright Act* are satisfied.** Copyright also lasts a relatively long time (compared to patents), but it does expire. In Canada, copyright lasts for **the life or the author, plus 70 years** (this duration was extended from 50 years in 2022 in keeping with requirements of the Canada-United States-Mexico Agreement (CUSMA, known as USMCA in the U.S.)).

The *Copyright Act* protects two distinct categories of rights: **economic rights and moral rights**. 

- Economic rights are designed to protect the economic value of original cultural expression and include such things as the rights to copy, to distribute, and to perform in public. 
- **Moral rights protect the reputation and integrity of an author.** The right to integrity protects an author against certain acts and associations that are prejudicial to an author’s honour and reputation. In one of the leading Canadian cases involving moral rights, ***Snow v The Eaton Centre Ltd.***, the Ontario High Court determined that festooning a sculpture with ribbons violated the sculpture’s moral rights.

**B. Patent**

A patent is a document that describes an invention and how to make that invention. In exchange for describing the invention and registering it in a **public patent registry**, the holder of the patent receives **a monopoly (20 years in Canada)** on the use of that invention. In order to receive a patent, one **must apply for a patent and satisfy a number of criteria** set out in the *Patent Act*, [RSC 1985, c P-4](http://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/P%2D4/){:target=" \_blank"}.

**2, Land: Airspace and Subsurface Rights**

!!! info "*Didow v. Alberta Power Limited*, [1988 ABCA 257 (CanLII)](https://canlii.ca/t/2dm47){:target=" \_blank"}"

    The single issue in this appeal is to determine, **whether the respondent has traspassed the air space above the appellants' land**.

    The respondent constructed a power line ... the cross-arms conductors and attaching wires at the top of each pole protrude six feet into the air space above the appellants' land.

    I wold **allow the appeal**.

    A resolution of the isse before the court turns on **the extent of the rights acquired by the appellants' to the column of air above and within the boundaries of the land**.

    The jurispurdence in this area of the law has developed from early English cases, where decisions were influenced by the maxim ***cujus est solum ejus est usque ad coelum et ad inferos*** which means that the owner of a piece of land owns everything above and below it to an indefinite extent.

    The authorities cited can, generally speaking, be divided into two groups:

    - Cases involving permanent structural projections into th air space above another's land;
    - Cases involving a transient invasion into the air space above another's land at a height not likely to interfere with the land owner.

    With respect to cases in the first group, the weight of authority favors the view that **a direct invasion by a permanent artifical projection constitutues a trespass**. ... both case law and statute law now decree that **a landowner cannot object to air traffic** which does not interfere with the use and enjoyment of his property.

    ... in ***Bernstein of Leigh v Skyviews & General Ltd*** [1978] 1 QB 479, **Griffiths J.** qualified the application of the Latin maxim by suggesting **"a balance" in compromising the rights of landowners against the general public**. 

    > It may be a sound and practical rule to regard any incursion into the air space at a height which may interfere witht the ordinary urse of the land as a trespass rather than a nuisance. But wholly different considerations arise when considering the passage of aircraft at a height which in no way affects the user of the land.

    **Griffiths J.** took a broader view by recognizing the landowner's right to enjoy the use of air space abvoe his land without limitation except as to the height. **Beyond the height necessary for the landowner's use, the air space becomes public domain.**

    I view this test as saying a landowner is entitled to freedom from permanent structures which in any way impinge upon the **actual or potential** use and enjoyment of his land. 

The celebrated case of ***Edwards v Sims*** concerns the ownership of part of the **Great Onyx Cave**, which is now part of **Mammoth Cave National Park** in Kentucky. The **Great Onyx cave** was developed as a tourist attraction by Edwards, who located an entrance on land that he owned. Lee, who owned a neighbouring property, claimed that a portion of the cave lay within his land. The cave was inaccessible from Lee’s land, so when Lee commenced an action against Edwards for trespass, he sought a prior court order to allow surveyors to enter the cave in order to map the extent of the cave system. Sims, the judge in that case, ordered the survey to proceed. Edwards sought to overturn the order. 

The question for the Kentucky Court of Appeal was **whether Edwards could be compelled to permit the surveyors on to his property to undertake the survey**.

!!! info "*Edwards v Sims*, 24 SW 2d 619 (Ky CA 1929) (US)"

    **Stanley C.:**

    ... ***Cujus est solum, ejus est usque ad coelum ad inferos*** is an old maxim and rule. It is that the owner of realty, unless there has been a division of the estate, is entitled to the free and unfettered control of his own land above, upon and beneath the surface. So whatever is in a direct line between the surface of the land and the center of the earth belongs to the owner of the surface.

    There are, however, **certain limitations on the right of enjoyment** of possession of all property, such as its use to the detriment or interference with a neighbor and burdens which it must bear in common with property of a like kind ...
    
    It seems to the court, however, that there can be little differentiation, so far as the matter now before us is concerned, between caves and mines. And as declared in 40 C. J. 947:

    > A court of equity, however, has the inherent power, independent of statute, **to compel a mine owner to permit an inspection** of his works at the suit of a party who can show reasonable ground for suspicion that his lands are being trespassed upon through them, and may issue an injunction to permit such inspection.

    We can see no difference in principle between the invasion of a mine on adjoining property to ascertain whether or not the minerals are being extracted from under the applicant's property and an inspection of this respondent's property through his cave to ascertain whether or not he is trespassing under this applicant's property...

    **Logan J. (dissenting):**

    The rule should be that he who owns the surface is the owner of everything that may be taken from the earth and used for his profit or happiness. I concede the soundness of that rule, which is supported by the cases cited in the majority opinion; but they have no application to the question before the court in this case. 

    A cave or cavern should **belong absolutely to him who owns its entrance**, and this ownership should extend even to its utmost reaches if he has explored and connected these reaches with the entrance.

    The true principle should be announced to the effect that a man who owns the surface, without reservation, owns not only the land itself, but everything upon, above, or under it **which he may use for his profit or pleasure, and which he may subject to his dominion and control**. But further than this his ownership cannot extend. It should not be held that he owns that which he cannot use and which is of no benefit to him, and which may be of benefit to others.

Much law and economics analysis begins with the “Coase theorem”, a proposition articulated by Ronald Coase in “The Problem of Social Cost” (1960) 3 Journal of Law and Economics. Richard Posner, Economic Analysis of Law, 5th ed (Aspen Business) at p 8, stated the Coase theorem as follows:

<div class="highlight green" markdown="1">[I]f transactions are costless, the initial assignment of a property right will not affect the ultimate use of the property.</div>

At first glance, Coase’s point seems banal. What Coase appears to be saying is that, **whatever the decision in a case over who has the property interest, market forces will drive property into the hands of the party that values it most.** As between the parties, the outcome matters, but when it comes to the efficient allocation of resources, the outcome does not matter. A closer look at the decisions in ***Didow v Alberta Power*** and ***Edwards v Sims***, help to reveal the explanatory potential of the Coase theorem.

Successful bargaining is not assured even when the dispute involves a limited number of parties. In Edwards v Sims, for example, Edwards could bargain with Lee and no one else. This is referred to as a **bilateral monopoly**. Hold-outs, free riders, and bilateral monopolies have the potential to block the transfer of interests in the market, but these are not the only impediments. For instance, Coase assumed that the initial allocation is itself a neutral event. However, empirical studies into the economic behaviour of owners suggest the presence of what is referred to as the “**endowment effect**” (sometimes called the **ask/offer problem**) that can affect subsequent strategic conduct. There is some evidence that a party is likely to ask more for an item they currently hold, than what they would be willing to pay to acquire that same item. The bargains are still efficient in themselves, but the final use of property does depend on the initial allocation.

    **The Application of the *Ad Medium Filum Aquae* Presumption**

    Assuming without deciding that the doctrine of ***ad medium filum aquae*** should apply to Indian Reserves, it is not applicable in this case for three reasons.

    To understand why the **presumption does not apply** requires a review of the common law rules concerning water. H. J. W. Coulson and Urquhart A. Forbes in *The Law relating to Waters* (2nd ed. 1902), at p. 100, explain the application of the ***ad medium filum aquae*** presumption in the following terms:

    > When the lands of two conterminous proprietors are separated from each other by a running non-tidal stream of water, each proprietor is *prima facie* owner of the soil of the alveus, or bed of the river, ***ad medium filum aquae***. The soil of the alveus is not the common property of the two proprietors, but the share of each belongs to him in severalty ... Where the same person is the proprietor of the ground on both sides of the stream, he is *prima facie* the proprietor of the whole of the channel ...

    One of the rights that flow from this possession of the bed is **the right to the fishery**. This is explained by the authors, at p. 104, in the following terms:
    
    > The right of fishery being a right of property, the presumption is that each owner of land abutting on a non-tidal stream has the right of fishing in front of his land, ***usque ad medium filum aquae***; and where a man possesses land on both sides of the water, he has the sole right of fishing.

    However, the authors go on to explain that **this right of fishery is severable from the title**, with the result that the right can be granted to another, or reserved from a grant ...

    Clearly the fishery, even where the ad medium filum aquae presumption otherwise applies, can be severed from the ownership of the river bed. **The evidence presented clearly establishes that there was no intent on the part of the Crown to grant an exclusive fishery.** 

    **The Presumption is Rebutted**

    It may now be helpful to summarize what I consider to be the relevant evidence and the applicable principles of law which determine the first issue.
    
    1. The Crown in all of its manifestations was consistently clear in its statements that **no exclusive fishery should be granted** to Indian bands in British Columbia. This is consistent with the fact that the Crown had no power to grant an exclusive fishery, and that after Confederation this would involve the grant of provincial property.
    
    2. **The correct test for an assessment of navigability is to consider the entire length of the river**. A section of the river which is non-navigable in fact does not necessarily render either the river as a whole or that section non-navigable in law if it is found to be substantially navigable throughout. The Bulkley River is navigable both above and below the Moricetown Canyon, and is therefore a navigable river.
    
    3. The presumption ***ad medium filum aquae*** does not apply on the facts of this case because:
    
        - a. Correctly considered the river is navigable, and the application of ***ad medium filum aquae*** to navigable rivers was not adopted into the common law of British Columbia since it was unsuited to local conditions.
        - b. **Fishing as a right can be the subject of a separate grant or reservation.** On the facts of this case it is clear that the fishery was reserved from the allotment.

**4, Fixtures**

When a chattel becomes a fixture, it ceases to be personal property and the title to that item is subsumed into that of the realty. The first case reproduced below, ***La Salle Recreations Ltd v Canadian Camdex Ltd***, demonstrates how Canadian courts determine whether an item has been transformed into a fixture.

***La Salle Recreations Ltd*** involved a dispute between secured creditors over the right to repossess wall-to-wall carpeting. The carpeting had been purchased under a conditional sales agreement, under which the carpet vendor retains title as security until the full purchase price is paid. The building within which the carpet was installed was itself subject to a mortgage. 

The mortgage was duly registered in the land titles office, and the conditional sales agreement was registered in the companies register. By virtue of the combined effect of sections 12 and 15 of the *Conditional Sales Act*, 1961 (since replaced), **where goods become affixed to the land, the security interest created under a conditional sales agreement is not binding against a subsequent mortgagee of land unless the agreement was also properly registered in the appropriate land registry office.** 

That step was not taken. **Priority over the carpets thus turned on whether or not the carpets had become fixtures.** If so, the mortgagee of the land obtained priority over the conditional vendor of the carpets.

!!! info "*La Salle Recreations Limited v. Canadian Camdex Investments Limited*, [1969 CanLII 740 (BC CA)](https://canlii.ca/t/gddbx){:target=" \_blank"}"

    A study of these and other authorities has led me to the conclusion that the principles to be applied are stated accurately by Meredith, C.J. speaking for a divisional court in ***Stack v. Eaton*** (1902) 4 O.L.R. 335 at 338, as follows:
    
    > I take it to be settled law:

    > (1) **That articles not otherwise attached to the land than by their own weight are ==NOT== to be considered as part of the land**, unless the circumstances are such as shew that they were intended to be part of the land.

    > (2) **That articles affixed to the land ==even slightly== are to be considered part of the land** unless the circumstances are such as to shew that they were intended to continue chattels.
    
    > (3) That the circumstances necessary to be shewn to alter the *prima facie* character of the articles are circumstances which shew the degree of annexation and object of such annexation, which are patent to all to see.

    > (4) That the intention of the person affixing the article to the soil is **material** only so far as **it can be presumed from the degree and object of the annexation**.

    Delivering the judgment of the Supreme Court of Canada (in ***Haggert v. Brampton (Town)***, [1897 CanLII 14 (SCC)](https://canlii.ca/t/1ttdc){:target=" \_blank"}) King, J. after referring to certain authorities, commented on the **object of annexation** as follows at p. 182:

    > In passing upon the object of the annexation, the purposes to which the premises are applied may be regarded; and if the object of setting up the articles is to enhance the value of the premises or improve its usefulness for the purposes for which it is used, and if they are affixed to the freehold even in a slight way, but such as is appropriate to the use of the articles, and showing an intention not of occasional but of permanent affixing, then, both as to the degree of annexation and as to the object of it, it may very well be concluded that the articles are [to] become part of the realty, at least in questions as between mortgagor and mortgagee.

    Special attention must be given to the use of the word “**permanent**” in this context. When used with reference to affixing or annexing chattels to realty I cannot believe that “permanent,” a relative term, means remaining in the same state and place forever or even for an indefinitely long period of time. In my opinion the word “permanent,” as used by King, J., should be interpreted for the purposes of this appeal as indicating the object of having the carpeting remain where it is **so long as it serves its purpose**. 

    Turning to the **object of annexation**, the question is whether the goods were affixed to the building, though slightly, for the better use of the goods as goods, or for the better use of the building as a hotel building.

    Weighing all these circumstances, I am of the opinion that the object of the annexation was the better and more effectual use of the building as a hotel and not the better use of the goods as goods. It follows that, in my opinion, **the carpeting and accessories were annexed to the land in such a manner and under such circumstances as to constitute fixtures**. 

The decision in ***Re Davis***, [1954] OWN 187, 1953 CarswellOnt 448 (HCJ) involved a dispute between a widow, whose deceased husband had owned the property in question, and the claimants who were entitled to the property under his will. The administrator of the estate intended to sell the property and distribute the proceeds to those entitled under the will, but the widow claimed her right to dower. Dower was the right of a married woman to a life interest in **one-third of any real property** in which her husband had held a fee simple interest at any time during the marriage. **When a husband died, the right of dower preceded any other claim against his estate.**

The second case, ***Diamond Neo***, illustrate some of the consequences of that designation (fixuture).

!!! info "***Diamond Neon (Manufacturing) Ltd. v. Toronto-Dominion Realty Co.***, [1976 CanLII 1513 (BC CA)](https://canlii.ca/t/gbk5d){:target=" \_blank"}"

    Issue: Whether certain attached signs and structures were fixtures (part of the realty) or chattels (personal property belonging to the plaintiff), determining if the defendant's subsequent sale of these items constituted a conversion of chattels.

    The plaintiff (Diamond Neon) manufactured and installed a metal pole pursuant to a contract with a used car dealer (Uptown Motors Ltd.). The contract specified that the display "shall remain the property of the Owner [Diamond Neon] and shall not by reason of attachment to any realty be deemed a fixture" and could be removed upon the lease's termination. The defendant later sold the signs and pole to a third party (Nettie Holdings Ltd.), prompting the plaintiff to sue for wrongful conversion of its chattels.

    **Majority Opinion (Robertson J.A.):**

    **Degree and Object of Annexation:** Both factors forced the conclusion that **the items had become part of the realty** before the defendant bought the land.

    The defendant had no notice of the leasing contracts when it bought the land, and notice received six months later could not affect the character of articles whose title had already passed with the sale of the land. The court **rejected the argument that the defendant was under an obligation to inquire** whether affixed items belonged to a third party.

    **Dissenting Opinion (Carrothers J.A.):**

    - The Nature of the Sign: a sign by its very wording has a unique ability to speak for itself regarding the intention of whether it was meant to become part of the land or remain a chattel.
    - Conclusion: The wording and degree of annexation showed an intention for the items to continue as chattels, and would have allowed the appeal.

**6, The Boundaries of Intangible Resources**

(a) Copyrights

!!! info "*Théberge v. Galerie d'Art du Petit Champlain inc.*, [2002 SCC 34](https://canlii.ca/t/51tn){:target=" \_blank"}"

    We are required in this appeal to determine **the extent to which** an artist, utilizing the statutory rights and remedies provided by the *Copyright Act*, [R.S.C. 1985, c. C-42](https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/C-42/Index.html){:target=" \_blank"}, can **control the eventual use or display of an authorized reproduction** of his or her work in the hands of a third party purchaser.

    In my opinion, for the reasons which follow, **the appellants did not thereby “copy” the respondent's artistic works.** They purchased lawfully reproduced posters of his paintings and used a chemical process that allowed them to lift the ink layer from the paper (leaving it blank) and to display it on canvas. They were within their rights to do so as owners of the physical posters (which lawfully incorporated the copyrighted expression). At the end of the day, no new reproductions of the respondent's works were brought into existence. **Nor, in my view, was there production (or reproduction) of a new artistic work “in any material form”** within the meaning of s. 3(1) of the *Copyright Act*. What began as a poster, authorized by the respondent, remained a poster.

    **II. The Respondent's Copyright**

    The respondent easily meets the first hurdle, which is to satisfy the statutory requirements for a copyright. **These works were protected by copyright from the moment of their expression, without any requirement of registration or other formality on his part.** The protection extends not only to the original painting (“artistic work”) but also to subsequent copies which embody the work. The question, however, is whether he went too far in obtaining a seizure before judgment of works which he, necessarily viewing the matter in his own interest, found to be infringing.

    In my view, with respect, **my colleague Gonthier J. gives too little scope to the property rights of the purchaser who owns the poster**, i.e., the physical object incorporating the copyrighted expression, and excessive rights to the artist who authorized the printing and sale of the poster purchased.

    More specifically, I think that if modification of these posters were to give rise to any legitimate objection on the part of the artist, it must be as a result of violation of his **“moral” right** to the integrity of his work. ... seizure before trial is not a remedy available in an action based on the alleged breach of the artist's “moral” right in a work.

    **III. The Content of the Respondent's Rights Under the *Copyright Act***

    The *Act* provides the respondent with **both economic and “moral” rights** to his work. The distinction between the two types of rights and their respective statutory remedies is crucial.

    The principal **economic benefit** to the artist or author was (and is) the “sole right to produce or reproduce the work or any substantial part thereof in any material form whatever” (s. 3(1)) for **his or her life plus fifty years** (s. 6). Consistently with this view, such rights can be bought and sold **either wholly or partially**, and **either generally or subject to territorial limitations**, and **either for the whole term of the copyright or for any part thereof** (s. 13(4)).

    **Moral rights**, by contrast, descend from the civil law tradition. They adopt a more elevated and less dollars and cents view of the relationship between an artist and his or her work. They treat the artist's oeuvre as an extension of his or her personality, possessing a dignity which is deserving of protection. They focus on the **artist's right** (which by s. 14.1(2) is not assignable, though it may be waived) **to protect throughout the duration of the economic rights** (even where these have been assigned elsewhere) both the integrity of the work and his or her authorship of it (or anonymity, as the author wishes).

    The important feature of moral rights in the present statute is that the integrity of the work is infringed **only if the work is modified to the prejudice of the honour or reputation of the author** (s. 28.2(1)).

    **V. Expansion of the Economic Right**

    My colleague, Gonthier J., proposes that we should treat the movement of the same physical layer of inks around different substrates as a violation of the respondent's s. 3(1) right “to produce or reproduce the work … in any material form whatever”. In my view, with respect, **this expansive reading of the s. 3(1) economic rights tilts the balance too far in favour of the copyright holder** and insufficiently recognizes the proprietary rights of the appellants in the physical posters which they purchased. 
    
    The *Copyright Act* is usually presented as **a balance** between **promoting the public interest** in the encouragement and dissemination of works of the arts and intellect and **obtaining a just reward for the creator**. ... it would be as inefficient to overcompensate artists and authors for the right of reproduction as it would be self-defeating to undercompensate them. Once an authorized copy of a work is sold to a member of the public, it is generally for the purchaser, not the author, to determine what happens to it.

    The appellants purchased on the open market a quantity of posters of the respondent's artistic works. They subjected these posters to a technique which involved spreading a special resin or laminating liquid across the face of a poster. The resin is designed to bond with the surface inks. After the applied coating is dried (or cured), the coated poster is submerged in a bath of solvent which loosens the paper substrate but leaves intact the fixed ink/resin layer, thus allowing the latter to be peeled off the former. The rear of the ink/resin layer is then coated with a suitable adhesive resin and transferred to a canvas substrate, which is then smoothed and finished.

    The process began with a single poster and ended with a single poster. The image “fixed” in ink is the subject-matter of the intellectual property and it was not reproduced. It was transferred from one display to another.

    **Did “reproduction” occur when the paper backing was peeled away?** If the resulting film of inks had then been framed and suspended in front of a window like a piece of Tiffany glass, I would think the respondent could not complain. A purchaser has the right to cut up a poster into strips or divide it as he or she wishes. **Division cannot logically be characterized as reproduction.**

    **VI. Conclusion with Respect to the Substantive Issue**

    My conclusion is that in this case the respondent is asserting a moral right in the guise of an economic right, and the attempt should be rejected.

**(b) Patents**

!!! info "*Monsanto Canada Inc. v. Schmeiser*, [2004 SCC 34](https://canlii.ca/t/1h3pt){:target=" \_blank"}"

    This case concerns a large scale, commercial farming operation that grew canola containing a **patented cell and gene** without obtaining licence or permission. The **main issue is whether it thereby breached** the *Patent Act*, [R.S.C. 1985, c. P-4](https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/p-4/){:target=" \_blank"}. We believe that it did.

    Our sole concern is with the application of established principles of patent law to the essentially undisputed facts of this case.

    **III. Analysis**
    
    **A. The Patent: Its Scope and Validity**

    ... in ***Harvard College v. Canada (Commissioner of Patents)***, [2002 SCC 76](https://canlii.ca/t/1k9){:target=" \_blank"} (“***Harvard Mouse***”)... plants and seeds were found to be unpatentable “higher life forms”.

    This case is different from ***Harvard Mouse***, where the patent refused was for a mammal. The Patent Commissioner, moreover, had allowed other claims, which were not at issue before the Court in that case, notably **a plasmid** and **a somatic cell culture**. The claims at issue in this case, for a gene and a cell, are somewhat analogous, suggesting that to find **a gene and a cell to be patentable** is in fact consistent with both the majority and the minority holdings in ***Harvard Mouse***.

    **Whether or not patent protection for the gene and the cell extends to activities involving the plant is not relevant to the patent's validity.** It relates only to the factual circumstances in which infringement will be found to have taken place, as we shall explain below.

    **B. Did Schmeiser “Make” or “Construct” the Patented Gene and Cell, Thus Infringing the Patent?**

    The *Patent Act* confers on the patent owner “the exclusive right, privilege and liberty of making, constructing and using the invention and selling it to others to be used” (s. 42). ... we have in any event concluded that Schmeiser infringed s. 42 by “using” the patented cell and gene.

    In summary, it is clear on the findings of the trial judge that the appellants saved, planted, harvested and sold the crop from plants containing the gene and plant cell patented by Monsanto. **The issue is whether this conduct amounted to “use”** of Monsanto's invention—the glyphosate-resistant gene and cell. Saving and planting seed, then harvesting and selling the resultant plants containing the patented cells and genes appears, on a common sense view, to **constitute “utilization” of the patented material** for production and advantage, within the meaning of s. 42.

    ... the first and fundamental question is whether **Monsanto was deprived** in whole or in part, directly or indirectly, **of the full enjoyment of the monopoly that the patent confers**. And the answer is “**yes**”.

    Invoking the concepts of **implied licence and waiver**, the appellants argue that this Court should grant an exemption from infringement to “innocent bystanders”. The simple answer to this contention is that on the facts found by the trial judge, **Mr. Schmeiser was not an innocent bystander**; rather, he actively cultivated Roundup Ready Canola.

    We conclude that the trial judge and Court of Appeal were correct in concluding that the appellants “used” Monsanto's patented gene and cell and hence infringed the *Patent Act*.
