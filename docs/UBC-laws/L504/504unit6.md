There is a fundamental distinction in the **common law** between **rules of law** and **rules of equity**. The oddness comes from the peculiar disjunction between some rules that are **legal** and others that are **equitable**. 

However, the common law evolved with these two separate streams of rules co-existing within it. The different streams were the preserves of entirely separate courts, but **in the nineteenth century the separate courts were fused into a single structure with jurisdiction over law and equity.** 

This unit introduces the common law and equitable courts, and then focuses on some aspects of **equitable doctrine** that have been particularly important in the development of the law of property. 

It does so through a brief foray into the **origins of equity**, and then by looking in more detail at the **emergence of the use and the trust**. It pays particular attention to the resulting trust and to the **constructive trust as a remedy for unjust enrichment**. The final topic turns to the **priority rules** at common law and in equity.

## 6.1 Origins of Equity

Canada is a federal state. One consequence of this federal structure is that Canada has provincial and federal courts, so in that sense the court structure is divided. 

- The lowest level of the criminal courts and small claims court are **provincial courts**; 
- supreme or superior courts and the courts of appeal in each province are **federal courts**. The federal government appoints the judges in these supreme and appellate courts. 

However, in another important sense, **the courts are unified in that they hold equitable and legal jurisdiction.** This means they have the capacity, within their subject-area spheres, to apply and enforce legal and equitable rules. 

This was not always the case in the common law. In the 12th and 13th centuries, three common law courts emerged out of the administrative practices and groups of officials with administrative expertise who, at least initially, answered to the King. 

- These courts were the **Exchequer Court** (responsible for matters relating to the King’s financial interests), 
- the **Court of Common Pleas** (which handled disputes between citizens), and 
- the **Court of King’s Bench** (which dealt with criminal matters, operated as an appellate court from Common Pleas, and handled matters of exceptional concern to the king, such as treason).

As the workload of these courts grew, and as the rules they administered became increasingly formalized, another judicial body emerged, **the Court of Chancery**, presided over by the Chancellor. **It served as an avenue of appeal to the Crown**, or the Crown’s representative, from the three common law courts. 

The Chancery Court began as a highly discretionary body that operated with a mandate to protect the King’s or Queen’s conscience by **rectifying those occasions where the application of the common law might do an injustice**. Over the centuries, the practices of the Chancery Court also formalized and hardened into a separate body of rules that was separate from those of the common law courts and which came to be known as **equity**.

Perhaps most importantly, the Chancery Court developed **a set of remedies**, known as **equitable remedies**, that only it would dispense. **There remedies were not available in the common law courts and their application did not overturn common law doctrine.** Rather, **equity existed in parallel to the common law.**

He describes the modern understanding of equity as a substantive body of law which acts as “a kind of **‘gap-filler’** that provides guidance in areas where the common law remained undeveloped or underdeveloped” (490). 

Historically, **lawyers and judges thought less in terms of substantive areas of law and more in terms of processes that would lead to various forms of relief.** That relief might be sought through the common law courts or the Court of Chancery.

## 6.2 The Principles and Maxims of Equity

The courts of equity were guided by a number of pithy statements of principle known as the **maxims of equity**. The included statements such as:

- Equity looks on that as done which ought to have been done;
- Equity will not allow a wrongdoer to profit by a wrong; and
- One who seeks equity must do equity.

There are other maxims, some of which are listed in the excerpt from Gray and Gray (the *Reader*, **497**) and all of which act as **guiding principles rather than binding rules**. Indeed, Gray and Gray describe the maxims as operating 

<div class="highlight green" markdown="1">
merely as background formulae of powerful thematic content, exerting an almost subliminal influence on the reasoning processes of lawyers (497).</div>

!!! note "Activity"

    Read the excerpt from Gray and Gray, *Elements of Land Law*, and comments 1 & 2 that follow it in the *Reader*, **495-501**.

What of the relationship between the rules of law and of equity? In most circumstances, **where there is a conflict the rules of equity will prevail**. See comment 2 (Reader, 500-01). 

In British Columbia, the *Law and Equity Act*, [RSBC 1996 c 253](https://www.bclaws.gov.bc.ca/civix/document/id/complete/statreg/96253_01){:target=" \_blank"}, includes a similar provision:

<div class="highlight yellow" markdown="1">
**44** Generally in all matters not particularly mentioned in this *Act* in which there is any conflict or variance between the rules of equity and the rules of the common law with reference to the same matter, **the rules of equity prevail.**</div>

However, this **does not mean that the equitable interest will always prevail** over the legal interest in a conflict between. As you will see in the discussion of common law priorities below, the **equitable rules might well recognize the legal interest as having priority.**

Intended as a salve to the rigidity of the common law courts, and to heal the King’s conscience from the injustice that the application of the formal legal rules might create, the **Court of Chancery** itself became burdened by its formality, but also by **a quagmire of uncertainty that its discretionary principles, the maxims of equity, created**. This is captured in John Selden’s famous aphorism of the 17th century that if feet were the measure of justice, then there was as much variability in the exercise of justice as in the length of each Chancellor’s foot:

<div class="highlight green" markdown="1">
Equity is a roguish thing: for law we have a measure, know what to trust to; equity is according to the conscience of him that is Chancellor, and as that is larger or narrower, so is equity. 'Tis all one as if they should make the standard for the measure we call a foot, a Chancellor’s foot; what an uncertain measure would this be? One Chancellor has a long foot, another a short foot, a third an indifferent foot: 'tis the same thing in a Chancellor’s conscience.</div>

**Charles Dicken**’s magnificent novel, *Bleak House* (1853), which centred on the interpretation of a will that was mired in the tangle of discretion in the Court of Chancery, was even more scathing. If you have an inclination to read the book (highly recommended!), but don’t have an opportunity, the BBC version is magnificent. 


<img src="../../../assets/img/U6T1 chancery.png" style = "width: 50%"/>

> The Court of Chancery in Westminster Hall

Popular condemnation and ridicule of Chancery, such as that in the engraving reproduced above, helped create the conditions that led to the *English Judicature Act* of 1873. **It united the common law and equitable courts, fusing legal and equitable jurisdiction within a single court structure.** 

In British Columbia, the ability of plaintiffs and defendants to seek out equitable remedies or have recourse to equitable defences in the courts of law is confirmed in the *Law and Equity Act*, [RSBC 1996 c 253](https://www.bclaws.gov.bc.ca/civix/document/id/complete/statreg/96253_01){:target=" \_blank"}, **sections 4 & 5**. Plaintiffs and defendants are entitled to have their claims for **equitable estates, rights, and forms of relief heard in the courts of law**.
