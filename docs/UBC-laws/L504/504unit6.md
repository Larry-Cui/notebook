There is a fundamental distinction in the **common law** between **rules of law** and **rules of equity**. The oddness comes from the peculiar disjunction between some rules that are **legal** and others that are **equitable**. 

However, the common law evolved with these two separate streams of rules co-existing within it. The different streams were the preserves of entirely separate courts, but **in the nineteenth century the separate courts were fused into a single structure with jurisdiction over law and equity.** 

This unit introduces the common law and equitable courts, and then focuses on some aspects of **equitable doctrine** that have been particularly important in the development of the law of property. 

It does so through a brief foray into the **origins of equity**, and then by looking in more detail at the **emergence of the use and the trust**. It pays particular attention to the resulting trust and to the **constructive trust as a remedy for unjust enrichment**. The final topic turns to the **priority rules** at common law and in equity.

## 6.1 Origins of Equity

Canada is a federal state. One consequence of this federal structure is that Canada has provincial and federal courts, so in that sense the court structure is divided. 

- The lowest level of the criminal courts and small claims court are **provincial courts**; 
- supreme or superior courts and the courts of appeal in each province are **federal courts**. The federal government appoints the judges in these supreme and appellate courts. 

However, in another important sense, **the courts are unified in that they hold equitable and legal jurisdiction.** This means they have the capacity, within their subject-area spheres, to apply and enforce legal and equitable rules. 

This was not always the case in the common law. In the 12th and 13th centuries, three common law courts emerged out of the administrative practices and groups of officials with administrative expertise who, at least initially, answered to the King. 

- These courts were the **Exchequer Court** (responsible for matters relating to the King’s financial interests), 
- the **Court of Common Pleas** (which handled disputes between citizens), and 
- the **Court of King’s Bench** (which dealt with criminal matters, operated as an appellate court from Common Pleas, and handled matters of exceptional concern to the king, such as treason).

As the workload of these courts grew, and as the rules they administered became increasingly formalized, another judicial body emerged, **the Court of Chancery**, presided over by the Chancellor. **It served as an avenue of appeal to the Crown**, or the Crown’s representative, from the three common law courts. 

The Chancery Court began as a highly discretionary body that operated with a mandate to protect the King’s or Queen’s conscience by **rectifying those occasions where the application of the common law might do an injustice**. Over the centuries, the practices of the Chancery Court also formalized and hardened into a separate body of rules that was separate from those of the common law courts and which came to be known as **equity**.

Perhaps most importantly, the Chancery Court developed **a set of remedies**, known as **equitable remedies**, that only it would dispense. **There remedies were not available in the common law courts and their application did not overturn common law doctrine.** Rather, **equity existed in parallel to the common law.**

## 6.2 The Principles and Maxims of Equity

The courts of equity were guided by a number of pithy statements of principle known as the **maxims of equity**. The included statements such as:

- Equity looks on that as done which ought to have been done;
- Equity will not allow a wrongdoer to profit by a wrong; and
- One who seeks equity must do equity.

There are other maxims, some of which are listed in the excerpt from Gray and Gray (the *Reader*, **497**) and all of which act as **guiding principles rather than binding rules**. Indeed, Gray and Gray describe the maxims as operating 

<div class="highlight green" markdown="1">
merely as background formulae of powerful thematic content, exerting an almost subliminal influence on the reasoning processes of lawyers (497).</div>

What of the relationship between the rules of law and of equity? In most circumstances, **where there is a conflict the rules of equity will prevail**. See comment 2 (Reader, 500-01). 

In British Columbia, the *Law and Equity Act*, [RSBC 1996 c 253](https://www.bclaws.gov.bc.ca/civix/document/id/complete/statreg/96253_01){:target=" \_blank"}, includes a similar provision:

<div class="highlight yellow" markdown="1">
**44** Generally in all matters not particularly mentioned in this *Act* in which there is any conflict or variance between the rules of equity and the rules of the common law with reference to the same matter, **the rules of equity prevail.**</div>

However, this **does not mean that the equitable interest will always prevail** over the legal interest in a conflict between. As you will see in the discussion of common law priorities below, the **equitable rules might well recognize the legal interest as having priority.**

Intended as a salve to the rigidity of the common law courts, and to heal the King’s conscience from the injustice that the application of the formal legal rules might create, the **Court of Chancery** itself became burdened by its formality, but also by **a quagmire of uncertainty that its discretionary principles, the maxims of equity, created**. This is captured in John Selden’s famous aphorism of the 17th century that if feet were the measure of justice, then there was as much variability in the exercise of justice as in the length of each Chancellor’s foot:

<div class="highlight green" markdown="1">
Equity is a roguish thing: for law we have a measure, know what to trust to; equity is according to the conscience of him that is Chancellor, and as that is larger or narrower, so is equity. 'Tis all one as if they should make the standard for the measure we call a foot, a Chancellor’s foot; what an uncertain measure would this be? One Chancellor has a long foot, another a short foot, a third an indifferent foot: 'tis the same thing in a Chancellor’s conscience.</div>

## 6.3 The Trust

One of the most significant developments in the courts of equity was a legal device known as the **“use”**, the precursor to what is now called the **“trust”**. 

In brief, the “use” enabled a transfer of a property interest to one party who would hold that interest for the use or benefit of somebody else. Equity intervened when the common law courts refused to enforce the condition that the person who held title did so for the use of another party. In this way, **one party would hold legal title** (the party to whom the interest had been transferred), **another party would hold the equitable or beneficial interest**. The one interest was legal in the sense that it could be enforced in the courts of law; the other was equitable in the sense that a remedy for a failure to honour the use was to be found in the courts of equity. The earlier excerpt from Philip Girard’s “*History and Development of Equity*” describes the extension of the chancellor’s jurisdiction over uses.

The **use**, although problematic for the King’s coffers, was enormously useful in dividing interests in land between legal and equitable interests. The *Statute of Uses* (1535) would largely eliminate the device, but it would be resurrected by lawyers and recognized by Chancery (the Court of Equity) as **an instrument that came to be known as the trust.**

**In a trust, legal title is allocated to a trustee, but the equitable or beneficial interests can be allocated to someone else**, such that the trustee holds legal title and the beneficiary holds the equitable interest. 

Trusts have many **functions**. 

- They are used to enable the holding of property for the benefit of children or some other dependent; 
- to allow someone to have access to interest income while protecting the capital; 
- to facilitate the management of a deceased person’s estate; 
- to hold funds for a charity; 
- to shield property from taxation; and 
- to do a great many other things. 

A trust relationship is not a simple one, and trust documents are not simple to create. **The large body of law that surrounds express trusts is the subject of courses in trust law.** 

Here we are concerned with some of the features of two forms of trust: the **resulting trust** and the **constructive trust**.

**A. Resulting Trusts**

Resulting trusts may arise in the transfer of property. They result from a presumption that one party (the recipient in the case of a transfer) holds the property received in trust for another party. In particular, resulting trusts arise in **three circumstances**:

- Where the beneficial entitlement under the trust is **not fully or property disposed of**;
    - In the circumstance where **A holds the fee simple interest and transfers it to B to hold in trust to C for life**, A has transferred the legal interest to B and the equitable or beneficial interest to C. However, A has only transferred part of the beneficial interest. A began with a fee simple interest and while they have transferred the full legal interest to B, they have only transferred an equitable life estate, not the full equitable fee simple. 
    - **What happens to the part left over?** Equity created a presumption that A holds the beneficial reversionary fee simple. In other words, **B holds the fee simple in trust to C for life and then in trust to A.** The beneficial reversionary fee simple arises in the form of a resulting trust because the beneficial interest had not been fully disposed of.
- When property is transferred as a **gift**.
    - See the discussion of ***Pecore v Pecore*** below.
- Where there is **a common intention** to create a trust.
    - The common intention resulting trust is a variant of the idea that a trust arises in favour of the party advancing the purchase price. It arises where it appears that **the party on title intended another party (commonly a spouse) to share beneficial ownership because the other party contributed in some way to the acquisition of the property.** 
    - The trust is triggered by some agreement, usually implicit, that the person who is not on title should acquire some property interest owing to their contributions to the acquisition, preservation, or enhancement of the property. In some cases, it is **a way of rewarding the non-monetary contributions of one spouse when the other held title.**

**B. Constructive Trusts**

Constructive trusts arise in certain contexts **where there has not been an express intention to make a trust** and **where there is no presumption of a resulting trust because there has been no transfer of property.** 

There are two categories of constructive trust : 

- the **institutional** constructive trust, arising in **a narrow and defined set of circumstances**, and 
- the **remedial** constructive trust, developed by Canadian courts as **a means of remedying unjust enrichment**. 

**1, Remedial Constructive Trusts**

The story of the remedial constructive trust as a remedy for unjust enrichment begins with the case of **Irene Murdoch**, whose former husband denied her a share of the family farm and to whom the SCC denied a remedy. The case and the decision of the SCC, which appeared to many as fundamentally unjust, were significant catalysts for dramatic reform in Canadian family law in the late 1970s.

The institutional constructive trust arises in two circumstances:

## 6.4 Priorities at Common Law and in Equity

Who should be awarded or be able to recover a property interest when there are **two innocent parties** that have been wrongfully deprived on a property interest by a third party? 

To take a classic example, a rogue purports to hold an interest in land and to transfer that interest to a purchaser who is unaware that they are dealing with a rogue. The purchaser secures title, the rogue disappears, and the former owner claims title. Who should prevail? The *bona fide* **purchaser** or the **former owner** who has been deprived of his interest?

Another way of putting this question is to ask **who should have priority?** The answer lies in the priority rules at common law and in equity, but the choice of answer depends on a policy decision about the allocation of risk. Should the rule protect settled interests in land and, by doing so, provide static security, or should it protect those transacting in good faith for interests in land, thereby providing dynamic security? 

We will see here that the **common law priority rules chose the former—static security**. **Title registration systems**, the focus in Unit 7, are designed to **protect the latter: dynamic security**. 

Lord Denning characterized the tension between the two approaches this way:

<div class="highlight yellow" markdown="1">
In the development of our law, two principles have striven for mastery. The **first** is for the protection of property: no one can give a better title that [she/he/they] ... possesses. The **second** is for the protection of commercial transactions: the person who takes in good faith and for value without notice should get a good title. The first principle has held sway for a long time, but it has been modified by the common law itself and by statute so as to meet the needs of our own times.

***Bishopgate Motor Finance Corp. v Transport Brakes Ltd.***, [1949] 1 KB 322 at 336-37.
</div>

One thing that you may note in this material is that the phrase “common law rules” sometimes refers to the common law rules as opposed to the rules developed in the courts of Equity, and other times refers to the rules of common law and equity as opposed to those developed in statute-based title registration systems. It is important that you be clear **when “common law” is being used to distinguish a rule in law from an equitable rule** and **when it is being used to distinguish judge-made rules in law and equity from their statute-based counterparts**.

**Priority Problems:**

At the **core** of common law priorities is the principle of *nemo dat quod non habet* or **no one can give that which they do not own.** Another accompanying principle, and one that you are probably familiar with, *caveat emptor* or **buyer beware**, supports it. 

These principles protect the rights of those holding interests in property. **Put another way, they place all the risk in a transaction on the purchaser.**

!!! danger "Priority Problems:"

    1. Ringo and Paul sign a contract to transfer Ringo's fee simple interest in Strawberry Fields to Paul. Ringo then receives a better offer from John and contracts to transfer the interest to John.

        **What is the nature of Paul’s claim? of John’s claim?**

        **A:** Both Paul and John hold equitable interests in Strawberry fields by virtue of the **institutional constructive trust** that arises following a contract for purchase and sale.

        **Who has the better claim?**

        **A:** **Paul**. As **between two equitable interests, the first to arise has priority over subsequent equitable interests.** One way to explain this result is that Ringo has already transferred the equitable interest to Paul and so does not have it to transfer to John. *Nemo dat quod non habet*. Note the qualification in ***Rice v Rice*** at 934 that “priority of time is the ground of preference last resorted to.” **All other things being equal, first in time has priority.**

    2. Ringo held the fee simple interest in Strawberry Fields. Paul concluded a written agreement with Ringo that Paul would lease Strawberry Fields for seven years. Paul took possession of the property under the lease, although did not occupy it immediately. At this point, John approached Ringo with an offer to purchase the fee simple interest in Strawberry Fields. Ringo, who did not tell John about the lease, accepted the offer and, several weeks later, transferred the fee simple interest to John. John then discovered that Paul holds a seven-year lease. John wants immediate possession. Paul insists on occupying Strawberry Fields under the lease.

        **A:** Paul holds a legal interest, a 7-year lease. John holds a legal fee simple interest.

        **Who has the better claim and why?**

        **A:** **Paul has the better claim because the prior legal interest has priority over John’s subsequent legal interest.** As a result, John would take the fee simple interest subject to Paul’s lease.

    3. Prudence held the fee simple interest in Strawberry Fields. She was also the manager of Apple Records. Apple Records agreed to loan Prudence $500,000. Some months later, Prudence negotiated another loan of $300,000 from Wilson Records, which did not know about the prior loan. Prudence, who had access to the Apple Records vault, retrieved the title deed to Strawberry Fields and gave it to Wilson Records as security for the loan. Prudence then defaulted on the loans.

        **Who has the better claim, Wilson Records or Apple Records?**

Mostly commonly, a lending institution such as a bank or credit union takes a mortgage from a borrower to secure a loan. The bank grants a loan; the property owner grants a mortgage. The bank, which holds the mortgage, may take the property interest if the borrow fails to repay the loan. At common law, the mortgage contract was strictly construed. If a lender missed a payment, then the lender could take possession of the property, even if the loan had been largely repaid. To rectify this apparent injustice, equity intervened to grant the borrower **a right of redemption:** **the borrow could redeem the property by repaying the loan even if the payment were late.**

As a result, the **first** mortgage (enforced by the courts of law) was **a legal mortgage**, the **second** mortgage (recognized by the courts of equity) was **an equitable mortgage.**

        > In the question above, Apple holds the first mortgage, Wilson holds the second mortgage. As a result, Apple holds a legal mortgage, Wilson holds an equitable mortgage. Who has the better claim? In addition to the discussion above, have another look at ***Northern Counties of England Fire Insurance v Whipp*** before trying to answer the question..

        **A:** **Apple’s prior legal interest has priority** over Wilson’s subsequent equitable interest.

    4. Ringo and Paul sign a contract to transfer Ringo's fee simple interest in Strawberry Fields to Paul, the transfer to complete at a future date.         **A:** Paul holds a prior equitable interest; John holds a subsequent legal interest.

        **What additional information do you need to determine whether Paul or John has the better claim?** 

        **A:** You need to know **if John had notice of the prior equitable interest.** 
        
        If John is the *bona fide* **purchaser** for value of the legal interest without notice of the prior equitable interest, then John’s legal interest would have priority over Paul’s prior equitable interest. Note Lord Denning’s reference to notice in the passage from ***Bishopgate Motor Finance Corp v Transport Brakes Ltd*** excerpted above. As you will discover in Unit 7, **notice does not require actual notice.** It can also be **implied** (through an agent) or **constructive** (the person ought to have known).


## Textbook Summary

**Chapter 6. The Origins and Nature of Equitable Interests**

Common law courts, administering **common law principles** (as interstitially altered by statute) were complemented by courts whose province was to apply **principles of equity**.

The principle invention of the Chancery Court, and the primary vehicle for creating and managing equitable interests in property, is the **trust**. **The modern trust arose in Chancery in the exercise of its equitable jurisdiction.**

A trustee holds legal title “in trust” for a beneficiary, who holds the equitable or beneficial interest. There are three types of trusts: 

- express trusts, 
- resulting trusts, and 
- constructive trusts. 

Express trusts are established through the **“three certainties”**: certainty of **intention**; certainty of **subject** matter; and certainty of **objects**. 

The details of an express trust, including the intention to create a trust, and identification of the subject matter (the property) and the object (beneficiary) of the trust, are set out in a trust document. 

This chapter focuses on **resulting and constructive trusts**, the trusts **imposed by courts to provide an equitable remedy** in the face of a perceived shortcoming in the common law.

!!! info "P. Girard, *History and Development of Equity*"

    Today we regard equity as a body of substantive law supplementary to the common law: a kind of **“gap-filler”** that provides guidance in areas where the common law remained undeveloped or underdeveloped. **Trusts**; certain remedies such as **specific performance**, **injunction**, and **tracing**; **unconscionability**; and **estoppel** are all part of **modern equity**.

!!! info "K. Gray & S.F. Gray, *Elements of Land Law*"

    **The fifth dimension of ‘land’**
    
    If land is capable of description in three physical dimensions, and of extension into a fourth dimension by the component of time, English law soon added a further dimension of analysis. **This extra dimension turned on the ‘legal’ or ‘equitable’ quality** accorded to the various abstract rights which had emerged from the medieval conceptualism of estates. 
    
    Indeed, much of the rich complexity of today's law of property results from **the potential duality of estate ownership, the institution of the trust.** 

    The **maxims of equity** are redolent with such notions as:

    - equity has regard to **intent** rather than form
    - equity will not suffer a wrong to go without **remedy**
    - he who comes to equity must come with **clean hands**
    - equity will **not assist a volunteer (one who provides no consideration)**
    - **equality** is equity
    - equity **follows the law**
    - equity looks on that as done which ought to be done.

    These maxims of equity (and there are others) are **far from being binding rules** of inflexible application. In fact they are not rules at all, but operate merely as background formulae of powerful thematic content, exerting an almost subliminal influence on the reasoning processes of lawyers.

    In some important sense, particularly in the context of the trust, legal rights can often be said to represent form, whereas equitable rights reflect substance. 
    
    - **Legal rights** give expression to the **external or formal** elements of ownership, the nominal or paper title frequently carrying a connotation more of responsibility than of entitlement. 
    - By contrast, **equitable rights** tend to acknowledge the **inner reality** of transactions in respect of land, thereby locating rather more accurately the substance of intended beneficial enjoyment.

    Of all the keynotes of equitable principle, perhaps the most influential is ultimately the idea that ‘equity acts *in personam*’. Courts of equity have always intervened by **ordering the defendant personally to do, or refrain from doing, a specific act.**

    In civil matters the characteristic **remedy of the common law is the award of damages**. **In equity, however,** the remedial range extends more widely, embracing forms of relief which act directly *in personam*.

    This procedural difference between law and equity acquires a vast significance **in the context of land law.** In most cases, therefore, **a specifically enforceable contract** relating to land must be completed by the delivery of the contracted performance. Again, the remedy for non-compliance with **a restrictive covenant** is not only the award of compensation to the aggrieved party, but frequently also an injunction restraining further breach of the covenant on pain of liability for contempt. 

**6:7 Resulting Trusts**

The trusts described above are mainly **express in nature**: the person who owned the property (the settlor) transferred the property to trustees (settled the property on trustees), who were expressly directed to hold the interest for the benefit of the settlor or other designated beneficiaries. 

However, **trusts may arise in other ways**, including by implication from the circumstances of a particular property transaction. These trusts are called **resulting trusts**, because the beneficial or equitable interest in the property results back to the settlor or that person’s estate. Equity recognizes resulting trusts in **two main circumstances**.

- First, when a trust document has failed to dispose fully of all the beneficial interests in the property, **equity presumes a resulting trust for the benefit of the settlor to fill in the gap.**
    - To provide one example, a settlor who holds land in fee simple transfers their interest **“To Acme Trust in fee simple to hold in trust for B for life”**. **What happens when the life estate ends?** ... equity imposes a resulting trust, for the benefit of the settlor or the settlor’s estate. 
    - equitable maxim, **“once a trustee, always a trustee”**.

- The second situation in which equity will recognize a resulting trust involves **gratuitous transfers.** Among the equitable maxims, **equity will not assist the volunteer**, or put another way, **equity prefers bargains not gifts.** 
    - In consequence, when A purchases property and asks that title be placed in the name of B, **equity presumes that A intended to retain the equitable interest by making B a trustee**, holding legal title for A’s benefit. 
    - The same result would follow if A transferred a currently held interest to B. It might look like a gift to the outside world but **equity starts with the view that A intended to create a trust.** In sum, in disputes over who holds the beneficial interest in this case, **equity will apply the presumption of resulting trust**, in the absence of evidence as to A’s intentions.

**Some exceptions** to the presumption of resulting trust have been recognized **in the case of gratuitous transfers between family members**. As discussed in ***Pecore v Pecore***, below, with transfers between some family members, equity was willing to presume that the transferor intended to make a gift of the property to the transferee, so that the **transferee** does not hold legal title on a resulting trust for the benefit of the transferor but **holds both legal and equitable title** themselves. This presumption is called the **presumption of advancement**.

Background of ***Pecore v Pecore***

In the context of Michael’s application for a divorce and a division of marital property, **Michael** claimed half of **the value of the accounts** that Paula had held jointly with her father, arguing that **on her father’s death, they became part of the residue of Edwin’s estate, to be divided equally between Paula and Michael according to Edwin’s will.** 

**Paula** resisted the claim, arguing that the **accounts became hers entirely on the death of her father, by the right of survivorship** (the right of the surviving holders of property held in joint title to the share of the deceased title holder, so that it does not become part of the deceased’s estate).

!!! info "*Pecore v. Pecore*, [2007 SCC 17](https://canlii.ca/t/1r9pm){:target=" \_blank"}"

    Given their widespread use, the law relating to **how these [joint] accounts are to be treated** by courts **after the death of one of the account holders** is a matter appropriate for this court to address.

    **Depending on the terms of the agreement between the bank and the two joint account holders**, each may have the legal right to withdraw any or all funds from the accounts at any time and **each may have a right of survivorship.** 
    
    **If only one of the joint account holders is paying** into the account and he or she dies first, **it raises questions** about whether he or she intended to have the funds in the joint account go to the other joint account holder **alone or** to have those funds **distributed** according to his or her will.

    It is not disputed that the daughter took legal ownership of the balance in the accounts [held jointly with her father] through the right of survivorship. **Equity, however, recognizes a distinction between legal and beneficial ownership.** The beneficial owner of property has been described as “[t]he real owner of property even though it is in someone else's name”: ***Csak v. Aumon*** (1990), 69 D.L.R. (4th) 567 (Ont. H.C.J.), at p. 570.

    This appeal raises the following issues:

    - Do the **presumptions of resulting trust and advancement** continue to apply in modern times?
    - If so, on what standard will the **presumptions be rebutted?**
    - How should courts treat **survivorship** in the context of a joint account?
    - What evidence may courts consider in **determining the intent** of a transferor?

    A **resulting trust** arises when title to property is in one party's name, but that party, because he or she is **a fiduciary or gave no value for the property**, is under an obligation to return it to the original title owner.

    **Advancement** is a gift during the transferor's lifetime to a transferee who, by marriage or parent-child relationship, is financially dependent on the transferor... In the context of the parent-child relationship, the term has also been used because “the father was under a moral duty to advance his children in the world.”

    **Each are rebuttable presumptions of law.** A rebuttable presumption of law is a legal assumption that a court will make if insufficient evidence is adduced to displace the presumption. **The presumption shifts the burden of persuasion to the opposing party who must rebut the presumption.**

    **The presumption of resulting trust is the general rule for gratuitous transfers.** However, depending on the nature of the relationship between the transferor and transferee, the presumption of a resulting trust will not arise and there will be a presumption of advancement instead.

    Historically, the presumption of **advancement** has been applied in **two situations**. The first is where the transferor is **a husband** and the transferee is **his wife**. The second is where the transferor is a **father** and the transferee is his **child**, which is at issue in this appeal.

    Next, does the presumption of **advancement** apply between **parents and adult independent children?** 

    Given these social conditions, it seems to me that **it is dangerous to presume that the elderly parent is making a gift** each time he or she puts the name of the assisting child on an asset. The presumption that accords with this social reality is that the child is holding the property in trust for the ageing parent, to facilitate the free and efficient management of that parent's affairs. **The presumption that accords with this social reality is**, in other words, the presumption of **resulting trust**.

    - **First**, given that a principal justification for the presumption of advancement is parental obligation to support their dependent children, it seems to me that the **presumption should not apply in respect of independent adult children.**

    - **Second**, I agree with Heeney J. that it is common nowadays for ageing parents to transfer their assets into joint accounts with their adult children in order to have that child assist them in managing their financial affairs.

    There should therefore be **a rebuttable presumption that the adult child is holding the property in trust for the ageing parent** to facilitate the free and efficient management of that parent's affairs.

    The **remaining question** is whether the presumption of advancement should apply in the case of **adult dependent children**. ...begs the question of **what constitutes dependency for the purpose of applying the presumption.**

    ... I am reluctant to apply the presumption of advancement to gratuitous transfers to “dependent” adult children because it would be impossible to list the wide variety of the circumstances that make someone “dependent” for the purpose of applying the presumption. I am therefore of the opinion that the **rebuttable presumption of advancement with regards to gratuitous transfers from parent to child should be preserved but be limited in application to transfers by mothers and fathers to minor children.**

    It is **open** to the party claiming that the transfer is a gift to **rebut the presumption of resulting trust** by bringing evidence to support his or her claim. ...the presumption will only determine the result where there is insufficient evidence to rebut it **on a balance of probabilities**.

    Of course, the **presumption of a resulting** trust means that it will fall to **the surviving joint account holder to prove** that the transferor intended to **gift** the right of survivorship to whatever assets are left in the account to the survivor. Otherwise, the assets will be treated as part of the transferor's estate to be distributed according to the transferor's will.

**6:11 Remedial constructive trusts**

The emergence of the remedial constructive trust as a response to unjust enrichment on the breakdown of a marriage or common law marriage is a compelling narrative. It begins with the Supreme Court of Canada decision in ***Murdoch v. Murdoch***, [1973 CanLII 193 (SCC)](https://canlii.ca/t/1xv6k){:target=" \_blank"}, and the outcry that resulted. 

The trial judge, the Alberta Court of Appeal, and the majority in the Supreme Court of Canada agreed that **Irene Murdoch**’s work did not give her any rights to property acquired by the couple during their marriage. Because **Irene Murdoch** had not made a direct financial contribution to the purchase of the ranch property, the majority in the **Supreme Court of Canada saw no reason to look behind the legal title**.

!!! info "V. Gruben, A. Cameron & A. Chaisson, *‘The courts have turned women into slaves for the men of this world’: Irene Murdoch's Quest for Justice*"

    [I]n 1968 there was no special regime for dividing marital property under either Alberta law or the federal *Divorce Act*. Apart from minor exceptions (such as dower), the rules of ownership then applicable to separating spouses were no different from the rules that governed property disputes generally. Thus, for Irene to receive a property interest in the ranch, she would have to turn to **general property law principles**.

    Under general property law doctrine, the **starting point is formal title**. In appropriate circumstances, a court was entitled to look past formal title and award a share of the property to another person. There is said to be **a presumption of a resulting trust in favour of the party making the contribution**. This presumption can be **rebutted** by showing that the contributor intended the party on title to have the property for their own benefit, in other words **as a gift.**

    **This general rule, presuming a resulting trust in favour of a contributing party**, applied where a wife contributed financially to the acquisition of property and registered title in the name of the husband. It would be presumed that the wife intended to acquire an interest in that property.

    The **sole issue** before the Supreme Court of Canada was Irene's proprietary interest in the ranch. **The case was argued on the basis that a resulting trust existed in Irene Murdoch's favour.**

    **The majority rejected Irene's claim for a resulting trust.** **Martland J**, relying on the trial judge's finding that the money from Nash was a loan, concluded that **Irene Murdoch** made **no financial contribution** to the property necessary to ground a claim in resulting trust. 
    
    **Martland J** then turned to Irene's other claims for a resulting trust based on her **physical contribution** to the Brockway property. Martland J argued, “the trial Judge made no such finding, but was of the view that what [Irene Murdoch] had done, while living with [her husband] was **the work done by any ranch wife.**”

    **Justice Laskin's dissenting opinion strikes a markedly different tone.** Laskin J was of the view that Alex could not in good faith retain a full beneficial interest in the property given his wife's extensive contributions. The appropriate remedy, he maintained, would be the imposition of **a constructive trust based on unjust enrichment**.

    **Legal Consequences**

    While Irene Murdoch was not successful in her property claim, her case, and the public's reaction to it, **spurred matrimonial property law reform.**

    In fact the reforms surpassed even Laskin J's dissent, and **enabled women to presumptively receive an equal share of matrimonial property** on the termination of marriage.

In his dissent in Murdoch, **Laskin J** had not disputed the view of the majority that the ordinary work of a wife did not entitle her to a property remedy. Rather, he argued that the imposition of a constructive trust was justified on the facts of the case because Irene Murdoch contributed money and labour that was far beyond the ordinary housekeeping duties expected of a wife.

In ***Peter v. Beblow***, [1993 CanLII 126 (SCC)](https://canlii.ca/t/1fs3f){:target=" \_blank"}, **the Supreme Court rejected this distinction between ordinary and extraordinary contributions**, ruling that **a contribution to the household well-being** through housework and child care, without contributions of cash or other labour, could **provide the basis for a beneficial interest in property**.

Despite the attention that the Supreme Court has given to the constructive trust, or perhaps because of that attention, lower court decisions continued to exhibit considerable confusion over the continuing relevance of the resulting trust, as well as over when and how to use the remedial constructive trust to adjust property entitlements between intimate partners.

The Supreme Court clarified this area of doctrine in ***Kerr v Baranow*** and ***Vanasse v Seguin***, cited as ***Kerr v. Baranow***, [2011 SCC 10](https://canlii.ca/t/2fs3h){:target=" \_blank"}, two cases that raised similar legal issues and for which the Court released the decisions together. Both appeals dealt with **the claims of the female partner** in an intimate relationship for spousal support and **for a share of property to which the male partner held title**.

The trial judge found that **Ms. Kerr** was entitled to **a one-third interest** in the Wall Street property **by way of resulting trust**, to reflect her contribution to the property, and also **by way of a remedial constructive trust as a remedy** for her successful claim in unjust enrichment. The British Columbia Court of Appeal allowed Mr. Baranow's appeal. Ms. Kerr appealed to the Supreme Court, which ordered a new trial.

The trial judge determined that **Mr. Seguin** was enriched to the extent that his increased wealth was due to **Ms. Vanasse**'s efforts as **an equal contributor to the family venture**. The trial judge compared the net worths of the parties and determined that **Ms. Vanasse was entitled to a one-half interest in the prorated increase in Mr. Seguin's net worth** during the period of the unjust enrichment, minus the value of her interest in the family home and her RRSPs, a total of just under $1 million. The Ontario Court of Appeal held that Mr. Seguin was enriched only to the extent that the monetary value of Ms. Vanasse's services to the family exceeded the compensation already provided by Mr. Seguin. The Court of Appeal ordered a new trial to assess her claim on that basis. **The Supreme Court allowed Ms. Vanasse's appeal and restored the trial judge's order.**

In ***Kerr v. Baranow***, [2011 SCC 10](https://canlii.ca/t/2fs3h){:target=" \_blank"}, Cromwell J clarified the principle of unjust enrichment. **It is because of the unjust enrichment of one party at the expense of the other party, that a constructive trust may be imposed over the proceeds of that benefit.** If a constructive trust is imposed, then the next step is to calculate the amount of that award. Cromwell J adopted a second approach to calculating the monetary award by asking whether there has been a **“joint family venture.”**

Across Canada there are different approaches to the application of** equitable remedies, including constructive trusts**, in the family law context. Some of the Canadian provinces have enacted **legislation that makes the constructive trust argument less significant** in the context of disputes over property acquired during an intimate partnership. 

- In British Columbia, the Family Law Act, [SBC 2011, c 25](https://www.bclaws.gov.bc.ca/civix/document/id/complete/statreg/11025_00){:target=" \_blank"}, ss 3 and 81, now provides that legally **married** spouses and partners, who have lived in a marriage-like relationship for at least **two years or who have a child together**, have the right, on separation, to **an undivided half interest** in all family property as a tenant in common.
-  ***Carruthers v Carruthers***, [2021 SKCA 52](https://canlii.ca/t/jf72h){:target=" \_blank"}, the Saskatchewan Court of Appeal confirmed the **ongoing utility of the principle of unjust enrichment and the constructive trust** in the family law context.


In ***Moore v. Sweet***, [2018 SCC 52](https://canlii.ca/t/hw6vr){:target=" \_blank"}, the majority held that the **three elements** required to establish an **unjust enrichment**:
- (1) that the defendant had been **enriched**, 
- (2) that the plaintiff **suffered** a corresponding deprivation; and 
- (3) there was **no juristic reason** for the enrichment and corresponding deprivation.

In concluding that there had been an unjust enrichment and in imposing the remedy of the constructive trust, the Supreme Court (at para 91) clarified **the circumstances in which a constructive trust is appropriate**:

<div class="highlight red" markdown="1">
... courts will impress the disputed property with a constructive trust only if the plaintiff can establish two things: 

- first, that a personal remedy would be **inadequate**; and 
- second, that the plaintiff’s contribution that founds the action is **linked or causally connected** to the property over which a constructive trust is claimed (***PIPSC***, at para. 149; ***Kerr***, at paras. 50-51; ***Peter***, at p. 988). 

And even where the court finds that a constructive trust would be an appropriate remedy, it will be imposed **only to the extent of the plaintiff’s proportionate contribution** (direct or indirect) to the acquisition, preservation, maintenance or improvement of the property (***Kerr***, at para. 51; ***Peter***, at pp. 997-98).

**6:16. Institutional constructive trusts**

The institutional constructive trust arises in situations where **one person owes a duty of loyalty to, or in a fiduciary relationship with another person, and acquires property in breach of that duty.** 

In Soulos v Korkontzilas, Korkontzilas was acting as a real estate broker for Soulos, his client. Through Korkontzilas, Soulos made an offer of $250,000 to purchase a small commercial building in Toronto. The vendor made a counteroffer, asking for $25,000 more. Soulos countered with a price that the vendor was willing to accept, but Korkontzilas did not tell this to Soulos. **Instead, Korkontzilas’ wife bought the property in her name, and then transferred title to herself and her husband as joint tenants.** When Soulos discovered the outcome, he brought an action seeking an order that **Kokontzilas and his wife held the property under a constructive trust for his benefit.**

!!! info "***Soulos v. Korkontzilas***, [1997 CanLII 346 (SCC)](https://canlii.ca/t/1fr25){:target=" \_blank"}"

    This appeal requires this Court to determine whether a real estate agent who buys for himself property for which he has been negotiating on behalf of a client, may be required to return the property to his client **despite the fact that the client can show no loss**. This raises the legal issue of **whether a constructive trust over property may be imposed in the absence of enrichment of the defendant and corresponding deprivation of the plaintiff.** In my view, this question should be answered in the **affirmative**.

    The history of the law of constructive trust ... suggests that the constructive trust is **not only to remedy unjust enrichment**, **but to hold persons in different situations to high standards of trust** and probity and prevent them from retaining property which in “good conscience” they should not be permitted to retain. These goals were accomplished by treating **the person holding the property as a trustee of it for the wronged person's benefit**, even though there was no true trust created by intention. 
    
    **Good conscience** addresses not only fairness between the parties before the court, but the **larger public concern of the courts to maintain the integrity of institutions like fiduciary relationships** which the courts of equity supervised.

    The constructive trust imposed for breach of fiduciary relationship thus serves not only to do the justice between the parties that good conscience requires, but to hold fiduciaries and people in positions of trust to the high standards of **trust and probity that commercial and other social institutions require if they are to function effectively.**

    ... the dual reasons for which constructive trusts have traditionally been imposed: to **do justice between the parties** and to **maintain the integrity of institutions** dependent on trust-like relationships.

    The situations which the judge may consider in deciding whether **good conscience requires imposition of a constructive trust** may be seen as falling into two general categories. 
    
    - The **first** category concerns property obtained by **a wrongful act** of the defendant, notably breach of **fiduciary obligation** or breach of **duty of loyalty**. The traditional English institutional trusts largely fall under but may not exhaust (at least in Canada) this category. 
    - The **second** category concerns situations where the **defendant has not acted wrongfully** in obtaining the property, **but where he would be unjustly enriched to the plaintiff's detriment** by being permitted to keep the property for himself. 
  
    The two categories are not mutually exclusive. Often wrongful acquisition of property will be associated with unjust enrichment, and vice versa. However, **either situation alone may be sufficient to justify imposition of a constructive trust.** 
    
    I conclude that in Canada, constructive trusts are recognized both for wrongful acts like fraud and breach of duty of loyalty, as well as to remedy unjust enrichment and corresponding deprivation. **While cases often involve both a wrongful act and unjust enrichment, constructive trusts may be imposed on either ground.**

    This case requires us to explore the **prerequisites for a constructive trust based on wrongful conduct**. I would identify four conditions which generally **should be satisfied:**

    - (1) The defendant must have been under an **equitable obligation**, that is, an obligation of the type that courts of equity have enforced, in relation to the activities giving rise to the assets in his hands;
    - (2) The assets in the hands of the defendant must be shown to have **resulted from** deemed or actual agency activities of the defendant in **breach** of his equitable obligation to the plaintiff;
    - (3) The plaintiff must show **a legitimate reason** for seeking a proprietary remedy, either personal or related to the need to ensure that others like the defendant remain faithful to their duties and;
    - (4) There must be **no factors** which would render imposition of a constructive trust **unjust** in all the circumstances of the case; e.g., **the interests of intervening creditors must be protected.**

**Chapter 12. Priorities and Title Registration**

Title under Canadian law is a relative term. The question is always, **who has the better claim.** As a result, in addition to creating rules for what are and are not property rights, **the law of property includes doctrine to establish the priority or the order of competing claims.** 

In general, the common law adopted a simple approach: **first in time is first in right.** However, sometimes latecomers are preferred, as in the law of adverse possession.

Although procedural in nature, these systems (**deeds registration systems**) are designed to establish priority ranking, and thus can alter substantive rights.

**II. Priorities at Common Law and in Equity**

From **S. Levmore**, *Variety and Uniformity in the Treatment of the Good-Faith Purchaser*, where a defective transaction has occurred, and where the original owner and the purchaser are without fault, **the law must determine who should be entitled to what remedy.**

**Before registration systems**, such conflicts were resolved through the combined effect of legal and equitable principles. Given that property rights can be legal and/or equitable, four conflict permutations can arise:

- A legal interest and subsequent legal interest (L vs. L)
- A legal interest and subsequent equitable interest (L vs. E)
- An equitable interest and subsequent equitable interest (E vs. E)
- An equitable interest and subsequent legal interest (E vs. L)

!!! info "L vs. L"

    Imagine that A holds the fee simple in Blackacre. A grants **a legal lease** (a lease in which all the formal requirements for granting a lease have been met) for Blackacre to B for a period of 10 years. Later, A purports to sell the freehold unencumbered by the lease to C, who is unaware of the existence of the lease. 
    
    In this scenario, C is **a *bona fide* purchaser** for value without notice of the prior lease. Even so, C will be bound by the prior lease to B. In other words, B’s lease will have priority. 
    
    In this scenario, the common law rule is straightforward. **Timing is everything**; **first in time is first in right.**

    Two Latin phrases capture the principles at work. One is *nemo dat quod non habet* (one cannot give that which one does not have); The second is *caveat emptor* (buyer beware). Put another way, **the risk that the transaction is defective falls entirely on the purchaser.**

The excerpt from the decision in ***Rice v Rice*** outlines the rule in **the third permutation**. In this case, the purchaser in a property transaction took title, but did not complete payment to the vendor. The purchaser then granted a mortgage on the property to secure a loan, and disappeared with the funds. As between the **vendor**, who was owed the purchase price, and the **mortgagee**, who was owed the sum it had loaned, which had priority?
