## 2.1.A Indigenous Legal Traditions

The University of British Columbia sits on a portion of the traditional, ancestral and unceded territory of the Hun’qumin’um speaking Musqueam people. The age-old Musqueam village site, a portion of which is now Indian reserve, lies by the mouth of the Fraser River, just a few kilometres from the university campus.

In the 1980s, chiefs of the Gitxsan and Wet’suwet’en First Nations in north central British Columbia brought a case claiming ownership of and jurisdiction over 58,000 square kilometres of land. The Supreme Court of Canada decision in that case, ***Delgamuukw v. British Columbia***, [1997 CanLII 302 (SCC)](https://canlii.ca/t/1fqz8){:target=" \_blank"}, established the **basic outline of Aboriginal title** in Canada. 

In it, the SCC indicated that courts must consider “the aboriginal perspective on their practices, customs and traditions and on their relationship with the land” in determining what Aboriginal title is and whether it has been established. We will look at the test for proving Aboriginal title in Unit 8, but in that and in other areas of law, including family law and criminal law, the courts have consistently held that **Indigenous legal traditions must be considered**. As a result, Indigenous legal traditions are one of the sources of Canadian law. They are also **living** legal traditions in Indigenous communities across the country.

The readings on Indigenous legal traditions are by two of the most prominent and well-respected Indigenous legal scholars in Canada. **Val Napoleon** leads the Indigenous Law Research Unit in the Faculty of Law at the University of British Columbia. **John Borrows** undertakes an exercise in translation, presenting some elements of the Anishinabek legal traditions involving the relationships between humans and the other-than-human world in a manner that is intended to be familiar to common-law trained lawyers. He draws on the Anishinabek oral history for “cases” that he then uses to draw certain established principles.

The piece that follows, by Richard Overstall, emerged directly out of the litigation in ***Delgamuukw***. It explains the **basic elements of Gitxsan property law**. In Overstall’s words, he is attempting to reveal “how the Gitxsan relate to things in the world through the power, or daxgyet, created when a lineage’s power merges with the power of a particular area of land offered to the chief in a supernatural encounter.” In the final few paragraphs Overstall outlines some of the principal points of similarity and difference between Indigenous and European-derived legal systems.

## 2.1.B Property in the Common Law Tradition

The legal doctrines that structure the rules governing interests in land are among the oldest in the English common law. By comparison, the bodies of law that we now describe as contract law and tort law are relatively new constructs for organizing rules.

In this unit, we take several glimpses into the history of feudalism and the English common law, the origin for some of the fundamental principles that structure Canadian property law have their origins in English feudal society. Indeed, in The Constitutional History of England: A Course of Lectures Delivered (Cambridge, 1908) the English historian, F.W. Maitland, described feudalism as a society premised on a collection of reciprocal rights and responsibilities that revolved around property relations:

> A state of society in which the main social bond is between lord and man, a relation implying on the lord’s part protection and defence; and on the man’s part protection, service and reverence, the service including service in arms. This personal relationship is inseparably involved in a proprietary relation, the tenure of land—the man holds of the lord, the man’s service is a burden on the land, the lord has important rights in the land. (143)

The doctrine of tenure is a foundational concept in English and now Canadian property law. In brief, the doctrine of tenure provides that all land is held “of the Crown.” No person holds absolute title, or what is known as “allodial” title—land without a lord—in civil law systems. The Crown was the lord at the apex of English feudal society, so all land, with the exception of some Church land, was held of the Crown.

The history of the doctrine of tenure is the focus in the readings that follow, although you will also find a brief discussion of another foundational concept in the English common law—the doctrine of estates. The doctrine of tenure describes what may be thought of as the quality of the interest in land; the doctrine of estates describes its quantity, measured in time. We look at the doctrine of estates in more detail in Unit 5.

The glimpses into the history of property in the common law tradition reveal the origins of fundamental principles that remain in Canadian law, but they also reveal a recurring tension within the law of property between interests in land as a means to produce and reproduce a particular social order and, alternatively, land as a commodity, fungible and alienable at will, as part of a market economy.

The tension between these competing visions of land does not produce a linear development: property law has lurched between principles designed, on the one hand, to secure a particular social structure that placed a landed aristocracy at its pinnacle, and on the other, to free land from the constraints that limited its participation in a market economy.

However, it is useful to think about the history of the law of property as the gradual accumulation of the bundle of rights that we now think of as comprising ownership. Three of the most important in that bundle (and the statutes that recognized or confirmed those rights) are:

- security of tenure (Statute of Tenures, 1660);
- the right to alienate or to transfer an interest in property (Statute of Quia Emptores, 1290); and
- the right to determine to whom the property should devolve on death (Statute of Wills, 1540).

The general point is that to understand the present structure and shape of property law, it is helpful to understand something of its history and social context.

Although we hardly hear this nowadays, all land in Canada (with the important exception of Aboriginal title) is held of the Crown in “free and common socage.” This was the form of agricultural tenure: land held in free and common socage required payment to the lord in agriculture products or agricultural labour. The requirement to provide foodstuffs or labour was converted to an annual payment, which gradually became meaningless and disappeared, but the tenurial basis of ownership—that all land is held of the Crown—remained.
