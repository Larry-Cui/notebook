## 2.1.A Indigenous Legal Traditions

The University of British Columbia sits on a portion of the traditional, ancestral and unceded territory of the Hun’qumin’um speaking Musqueam people. The age-old Musqueam village site, a portion of which is now Indian reserve, lies by the mouth of the Fraser River, just a few kilometres from the university campus.

In the 1980s, chiefs of the Gitxsan and Wet’suwet’en First Nations in north central British Columbia brought a case claiming ownership of and jurisdiction over 58,000 square kilometres of land. The Supreme Court of Canada decision in that case, ***Delgamuukw v. British Columbia***, [1997 CanLII 302 (SCC)](https://canlii.ca/t/1fqz8){:target=" \_blank"}, established the **basic outline of Aboriginal title** in Canada. 

In it, the SCC indicated that courts must consider “the aboriginal perspective on their practices, customs and traditions and on their relationship with the land” in determining what Aboriginal title is and whether it has been established. We will look at the test for proving Aboriginal title in Unit 8, but in that and in other areas of law, including family law and criminal law, the courts have consistently held that **Indigenous legal traditions must be considered**. As a result, Indigenous legal traditions are one of the sources of Canadian law. They are also **living** legal traditions in Indigenous communities across the country.

The readings on Indigenous legal traditions are by two of the most prominent and well-respected Indigenous legal scholars in Canada. **Val Napoleon** leads the Indigenous Law Research Unit in the Faculty of Law at the University of British Columbia. **John Borrows** undertakes an exercise in translation, presenting some elements of the Anishinabek legal traditions involving the relationships between humans and the other-than-human world in a manner that is intended to be familiar to common-law trained lawyers. He draws on the Anishinabek oral history for “cases” that he then uses to draw certain established principles.

The piece that follows, by Richard Overstall, emerged directly out of the litigation in ***Delgamuukw***. It explains the **basic elements of Gitxsan property law**. In Overstall’s words, he is attempting to reveal “how the Gitxsan relate to things in the world through the power, or daxgyet, created when a lineage’s power merges with the power of a particular area of land offered to the chief in a supernatural encounter.” In the final few paragraphs Overstall outlines some of the principal points of similarity and difference between Indigenous and European-derived legal systems.

## 2.1.B Property in the Common Law Tradition

The legal doctrines that structure the rules governing interests in land are among the oldest in the English common law. By comparison, the bodies of law that we now describe as contract law and tort law are relatively new constructs for organizing rules.

In this unit, we take several glimpses into the history of **feudalism and the English common law**, the origin for some of the fundamental principles that structure Canadian property law have their origins in English feudal society. Indeed, in The Constitutional History of England: A Course of Lectures Delivered (Cambridge, 1908) the English historian, F.W. Maitland, described feudalism as a society premised on a collection of reciprocal rights and responsibilities that revolved around property relations:

> A state of society in which the **main social bond is between lord and man**, a relation implying on the lord’s part protection and defence; and on the man’s part protection, service and reverence, the service including service in arms. This personal relationship is inseparably involved in a proprietary relation, **the tenure of land**: the man holds of the lord, the man’s service is a burden on the land, the lord has important rights in the land. (143)

The **doctrine of tenure** is a foundational concept in English and now Canadian property law. In brief, the doctrine of tenure provides that all land is held “of the Crown.” No person holds absolute title, or what is known as “*allodial*” title, land without a lord, in civil law systems. **The Crown was the lord at the apex of English feudal society, so all land, with the exception of some Church land, was held of the Crown.**

The history of the doctrine of tenure is the focus in the readings that follow, although you will also find a brief discussion of another foundational concept in the English common law, **the doctrine of estates**. 

- The doctrine of tenure describes what may be thought of as the **quality** of the interest in land; 
- the doctrine of estates describes its **quantity**, measured in time. 

We look at the doctrine of estates in more detail in Unit 5.

The glimpses into the history of property in the common law tradition reveal the origins of fundamental principles that remain in Canadian law, but they also reveal a recurring tension within the law of property between interests in land as a means to produce and reproduce a particular social order and, alternatively, land as a commodity, fungible and alienable at will, as part of a market economy.

The **tension** between these competing visions of land does not produce a linear development: property law has lurched between principles designed, on the one hand, to secure a particular social structure that placed a landed aristocracy at its pinnacle, and on the other, to free land from the constraints that limited its participation in a market economy.

However, it is useful to think about the history of the law of property as the gradual accumulation of the bundle of rights that we now think of as comprising ownership. Three of the most important in that bundle (and the statutes that recognized or confirmed those rights) are:

- security of tenure (*Statute of Tenures*, 1660);
- the right to alienate or to transfer an interest in property (*Statute of Quia Emptores*, 1290); and
- the right to determine to whom the property should devolve on death (*Statute of Wills*, 1540).

The general point is that to understand the present structure and shape of property law, it is helpful to understand something of its history and social context.

## 2.1.C Property and the Doctrine of Reception

In his Commentaries on the Law of England, **William Blackstone**, the famous 18th century chronicler of the common law, wrote:

> the law is the birthright of every subject, so wherever they go they carry their laws with them. 

In a settler society, settlers remained subject to the English common law as it continued to develop in England, so long as it was applicable in the local circumstances. 

- **The date of reception marks the date at which developments in England stop being automatically incorporated into the law of a colony.** 

- **After the date of reception, the English law on the date of reception continues to apply as modified in the colony (not as modified in England).** Developments in England may matter, indeed the Judicial Committee of the Privy Council remains the court of last resort in Canada **until 1949**, but the mass of statutory developments in England after the date of reception do not apply unless specifically incorporated in a colony.

In British Columbia, the date of reception is **19 November 1858**. You’ll find this in the Law and Equity Act, RSBC 1996, c 253:

> Subject to section 3, the Civil and Criminal Laws of England, as they existed on November 19, 1858, so far as they are not from local circumstances inapplicable, are in force in British Columbia, but those laws must be held to be modified and altered by all legislation that has the force of law in British Columbia or in any former Colony comprised within its geographical limits.

Why 1858? **This was the year that Great Britain founded the Crown colony of British Columbia in an attempt to secure control of a territory** as thousands of American gold-seekers flooded north, heading for the Fraser River. Britain had established Vancouver Island as a proprietary colony of the Hudson’s Bay Company in 1849, but the Fraser River gold rush in 1858 prompted action on the mainland, and part of securing British Columbia as a British territory was asserting the English common law.

## 2.1.D The Doctrine of Tenure in Canadian Law

British Columbia (and most of Canada) received English law long after the incidents of tenure had disappeared. Nonetheless, certain fundamental elements of the English teneurial property system remain.

**Tenurial (not Allodial) Title**

All interests in land in Canada (with the important exception of Aboriginal title) are **held “of the Crown.”** Individuals do not hold absolute or allodial title. Instead, the Crown holds the underlying title and all other interests are held “of the Crown.”

**Free and Common Socage**

All land in British Columbia and common law Canada is held of the Crown in **free and common socage, the least onerous of the forms of tenure**. 

Initially, free and common socage included the provision of agricultural products or agricultural labour. This was converted to a monetary payment known as ‘rent’, but this has long since become a meaningless sum and it has disappeared. Even the Hudson’s Bay Company, when it was granted much of what is now the Canadian north (all lands draining into Hudson’s Bay) in free and common socage by the Crown in 1670, was only required to provide two elks and two black beavers when the monarch visited the territory as rent. The fact that the person who held land in free and common socage had to pay rent, even nominal rent, reinforced the tenurial nature of title in the common law. 

Although the need to make a payment has disappeared, and although we hardly hear the expression anymore, **free and common socage remains the basis of land ownership in Canada**.

<div class="highlight red" markdown="1">Socage is a historical feudal system of land tenure where a tenant held land in exchange for fixed, non-military services or cash rent.</div>

**Escheat**

Of the incidents on tenure, only escheat is relevant today. How so? 

The *Statute of Wills* (1540) formally recognized the right of landholders to direct the distribution of their real property on death. In some jurisdictions, this right is now subject to protections for immediate family members (in BC the *Wills, Estates and Succession Act*, [SBC 2009, c 13, s 60](https://www.bclaws.gov.bc.ca/civix/document/id/complete/statreg/09013_01){:target=" \_blank"}), and **if a person dies without a will, the rules of intestacy apply** (*Wills, Estates and Succession Act*, ss 19-25).

If there is no will and there is nobody who is entitled to a share of the estate under the *Act*, then **the property escheats to the Crown** (*Wills, Estates and Succession Act*, s 23(2)(f)). In effect, the Crown is entitled to regain and estate in land once the tenure granted has come to an end. See the *Escheat Act*, [RSBC 1996, s 120, s. 1](https://www.bclaws.gov.bc.ca/civix/document/id/complete/statreg/00_96120_01){:target=" \_blank"}.

<div class="highlight red" markdown="1">**Escheat** is a legal doctrine where ownership of a property or estate transfers to the government or the Crown if an owner **dies without a will (intestate) and without legal heirs**.</div>

## 2.3 Protections for Property

Canada is unusual among countries with a written constitution and bill of rights in that **it does not include explicit protection for private property**. It is also unusual among countries that wrote or re-wrote their bill of rights in the latter half of the 20th century in that it does not include a right to a healthy environment. It may be that Canada’s traditional dependence on resource extraction helps to explain both omissions. 

In any event, **the fact that there is no constitutional protection for private property does not mean that there are no legal protections.** This section considers some of the various protections for property that exist in Canadian law, including those at common law and statute-based.

- A. Protections for Private Property
- B. Expropriation and Constructive Expropriation

## 2.3.A Protections for Private Property

Protection for private property was omitted from the list of enumerated rights when Canada introduced its *Charter of Rights and Freedoms* in 1982. However, there is a property provision in the **Canadian Bill of Rights**, SC 1960, c 44, that John Diefenbaker’s Conservative government introduced in 1960, but it is an ordinary statute, not a constitutional document, and **the courts minimized its role**.

!!! warning "BILL OF RIGHTS"

    **Recognition and declaration of rights and freedoms**

    It is hereby recognized and declared that in Canada there have existed and shall continue to exist without discrimination by reason of race, national origin, colour, religion or sex, the following human rights and fundamental freedoms, namely,

    (a) the right of the individual to life, liberty, security of the person and **enjoyment of property**, and the right not to be deprived thereof except by due process of law;

In 2006, the Conservative Party election platform included the following commitment:

<div class="highlight green" markdown="1">
A Conservative government will:

- Propose an amendment to the Constitution to include the right to own property, as well as a guarantee that no person shall be deprived of their just right without due process of law and without full, just, and timely compensation.
</div>

However, the minority Conservative government that emerged from that election did not act on this part of its platform, and **the commitment to introduce a property rights guarantee in the *Charter* has not appeared since.** There is little appetite for re-opening the constitution, and no groundswell of public opinion that private property is under threat, notwithstanding the lack of constitutional protection.

In a stable, constitutional democracy, such as Canada, with a long history of protection for property at common law, it seems that the choice about whether to entrench property in the constitution is primarily a choice about whether to involve courts in deliberations about the **balance between private property and public regulation** or whether to leave it within legislatures and the realm of elected officials.

## 2.3.B Expropriation and Constructive Takings

It may seem odd to include a section on **expropriation** (known as “**compulsory acquisition**” in many other common law jurisdictions and “**eminent domain**” in the US) and *de facto* expropriation or **constructive takings** (“**regulatory takings**” in the US) within “Protections for Property”, but the extent to which, and the conditions under which, the law allows the government to take property or regulate property, reveals much about the degree to which society protects private interests in land.

**Expropriation**

The terms under which a government may expropriate or authorize the expropriation of private property are **set out by statute in each of the provinces**. These statutes are **subject to the common law rule** that statutes proposing to take interests in land are **to be strictly interpreted**. In other words, the statutory intent to take property must be clear and plain, and any ambiguity will be interpreted in favour of the property owner. They are also subject to **a presumption at common law that the state will compensate** private owners for the expropriation of their property unless the statute provides otherwise.

In British Columbia, the *Expropriation Act*, RSBC 1996 c 125, governs acts of expropriation. It begins with a definition of “expropriate”:

> 1  "**expropriate**" means the **taking of land** by an expropriating authority under an enactment without the consent of the owner

!!! note "Activity"

    Go to the Expropriation Act and review the following sections: 1 (definition of expropriation), 4(2), 6(1), 10(2), 14, 18, 30(1), and 31(1).

In brief, a person whose land is the subject on an expropriation proceeding cannot “**dispute the right of an expropriating authority to have recourse to expropriation**” (4(2)). However, they do have right to **notice** (6(1)), and to request an **inquiry** (10(2)), including a public hearing to determine “whether the proposed expropriation of land is necessary to achieve the objectives of the expropriating authority with respect to the proposed project (14(1)), **but not consider the necessity of the project** (14(2)).

Where expropriation is approved, the owner of the land is **entitled to compensation** (30(1)), based on market value and reasonable damages for disturbance (31(1)). 

<div class="highlight green" markdown="1">The basic principle is one of **reinstatement**, meaning the owner of the expropriated property should be put in a substantially equivalent condition by means of substituted property.</div>

**Constructive Takings**

Expropriation involves the taking of an interest in land; **constructive takings** (formerly *de facto* expropriation and *de facto* taking) involves the **regulating of an interest in land to such an extent as to amount to a taking**. In these cases, the courts construct the regulation as a taking for which the **state must compensate**.

Were you taking a course in US property law, the doctrine of regulatory taking would almost certainly consume a great deal more of your attention than it will in this Canadian course. This is a function of the fact that the doctrine exists on the margins of Canadian property law. Canadian courts have found constructive takings (for which the owner of the property interest must be compensated even though they still hold formal legal title) in a handful of cases. 

Generally speaking, **Canadian courts have been reluctant to assert** themselves by declaring that the public regulation of private interests in land has gone too far and therefore that the owner must be compensated. However, the decision of the Supreme Court of Canada in ***Annapolis Group Inc. v. Halifax Regional Municipality***, [2022 SCC 36](https://canlii.ca/t/jshfv){:target=" \_blank"}, has the potential to be transformative.

**(b) English Common Law**

!!! info "P Butt, *Land Law* 6th ed."

    **Feudalism in Western Europe**

    - The doctrine of tenure was inextricably linked with the social system of feudalism. 
    - "Commendation", by which people placed themselves under the protection of a more powerful neighbor, the weaker becoming the "vassal" of the "lord" ... giving rise to mutual duties of support and protection.
    - Smaller landowners would transfer ownership to their more powerful neighbors, receiving in exchange the right to remain in occupation of the land and reap the produce. This practice easily coalesced with commendation, thus laying the foundation of feudal "tenure".
    - In its mature form, commendation consisted of three parts.
        - The first was the act of "homage", when by placing his hands between the hands of the lord, a person became the lord's "man", his vassal.
        - The second was the oath of "fealty", hallowing homage with religious sanctions.
        - The third was "investiture", by which the lord symbolically invested the vassal with the land. In this result, the vassal no longer owns the land, but "holds" it from the lord, the vassal has become a "tenant".

    **Feudalism in England**

    - "Feudalism" was a direct result of the Norman Conquest.
    - It is clear that by the time of the *Domesday Book* (1086) the feudal system of land tenure had been adopted throughout England.
    - What especially distinguished English feudalism from its Continental counterpart was its universality. This was based on the premise that William the Conqueror was the paramount lord of all the land in England. And so a grant from the King became the basis, actual or fictional, of landholding. No longer was any land allodial.
    - As on the Continent, so in England, tenure was the chief means of securing the performance of important public duties.

    **The English Doctrine of Tenures**

    - The English doctrine of tenure was wider in scope than its Continental counterpart. In England there were no theoretical restrictions on the types of services that could be imposed on a grant of land, and a very wide variety arose. By the time of Littleton, the various forms of tenures could be classified in the following categories: knight service, serjeanty, frankalmoin, and socage.
    - In addition to the services due from the feudal tenant, the fact of tenure gave rise to other rights and obligations, known as "incidents of tenure." The key incidents were: homage, fealty and suit of court, aids, relief and primer seisin, wardship and marriage, and escheat.

!!! info "B Ziff, "Warm Reception in a Cold Climat: English Property Law and the Suppression of the Canadian Legal Identity"

    There are different **reception rules** affecting colonial holdings,

    - in the case of conquered or ceded colonies, the starting point was that the pre-existing local law would remain in force until altered by the new sovereign. 
    - for settled colonies, the general rule is that the laws of English form part of the laws of the colony in so far as these are applicable to conditions in the colony.

    Which property laws, designed initially for application in England, were regarded as sufficiently suitable to the circumstances, physical, economic and social, in this part of the Empire? The short answer is: **most English laws.**

    Two reasons why this pattern of adherence exists,

    - **The first explanation ins instrumental in nature.** Prudence points to the reception of a given rule when the absence of adoption creates uncertainty. It is also convenient: it would often be pointless to re-invent the wheel. More important, property law is like a gossamer cloth. Unravel it at your peril. 
    - **A second reason is normative.** One finds within imperial discourse of this period a resolute confidence in the superiority of English political institutions, and this is manifested in part in a belief in the right-headedness of English justice. The common law was seen as a repository of centuries of collected wisdom. It was as much a symbol of progress and the development of civilization as any to which one might point.

**(3) Property, Class and Poverty**

!!! info "J Waldron, *Homelessness and the Issue of Freedo*m"

    Jeremy Waldron argues that freedom is inseparable from access to physical space. Since every human action must occur somewhere, property rules determine where people are allowed to exist and what they are permitted to do. Private property gives owners the power to exclude others, but homeless people often have no private space of their own in which they can perform basic activities such as sleeping, eating, washing, cooking, or using the bathroom.

    Waldron observes that in a society where all land was privately owned, homeless people would effectively have nowhere they could legally be. **Their ability to exist and act therefore depends on public spaces and other forms of common property.** Yet governments increasingly regulate streets, parks, and public areas, banning activities that are necessary for survival. As a result, homeless people can be punished not for harmful conduct, but simply for carrying out ordinary human functions in the only places available to them.

    The article’s key argument is that **homelessness is not merely a problem of poverty or material deprivation; it is also a problem of freedom.** By denying homeless people access to places where they can perform basic human activities, society restricts their liberty in a fundamental way. Waldron concludes that if society tolerates homelessness, **justice requires allowing homeless individuals to use public spaces to meet essential needs rather than criminalizing those activities.**

!!! info "RC Ellickson, *Controlling Chronic Misconduct in City Spaces: of Panhandlers, Skid Rows, and Public-Space Zoning*"

    Ellickson examines how cities should manage public spaces in the face of persistent street behaviours such as panhandling, bench-squatting, public drunkenness, and other forms of chronic nuisance. He argues that public spaces are valuable commons that promote mobility, recreation, commerce, and democratic interaction, but they are also vulnerable to misuse. When disruptive behaviours persist, they can discourage ordinary citizens from using public areas, contribute to perceptions of disorder, and reduce the value of shared spaces.

    Ellickson contends that chronic street misconduct should be understood primarily as a **land-management problem** rather than solely **a criminal law or free speech issue**. He proposes that cities adopt a system of **public-space zoning**, analogous to land-use zoning. Under this model, different public spaces would be subject to different behavioural standards. “Red Zones” would tolerate more disorderly conduct and serve as havens for marginal populations, “Yellow Zones” would maintain moderate standards suitable for most downtown activity, and “Green Zones” would impose stricter rules to provide quiet, orderly refuges.

    Ellickson concludes that a single set of rules for all public spaces is inappropriate. Effective urban governance requires tailoring regulations to the varying purposes and users of different public environments.

    **Key Argument**

    **Ellickson’s central argument is that cities should regulate public behaviour through a system of “public-space zoning,” allowing different public spaces to have different standards of conduct. This approach balances the needs of disorderly or marginalized individuals with the interests of ordinary citizens who rely on public spaces for safety, movement, recreation, and social interaction.**

    **Main Supporting Points**

    - **Public spaces are commons** and can suffer from a “tragedy of the commons” when chronic misconduct discourages others from using them.
    - **Chronic nuisances create cumulative harms**, including annoyance, fear, reduced public use, and signals of social disorder.
    - **Rules and order are compatible with freedom**, because orderly spaces often make participation possible for a larger number of people.
    - **Different public spaces serve different purposes**, so uniform rules are inefficient and unfair.
    - **Public-space zoning** offers a practical compromise between complete tolerance and blanket prohibition of street misconduct.

!!! info "*Victoria (City) v. Adams*, [2008 BCSC 1363](https://canlii.ca/t/215hs){:target=" \_blank"}"

    In this case, homeless individuals challenged Victoria bylaws that prohibited erecting temporary shelters such as tents, tarps, and cardboard structures on public property. The City argued that the restrictions were necessary to preserve public parks and maintain order. The defendants argued that, because there were far fewer shelter beds than homeless people, many individuals had no choice but to sleep outdoors and required temporary shelter for protection from the elements.

    The court found that Victoria had over 1,000 homeless residents but only a fraction of that number in available shelter spaces. Expert evidence showed that sleeping outdoors without adequate overhead protection exposed homeless individuals to serious health risks, including hypothermia. The court concluded that **sleep and shelter are basic necessities and that preventing homeless people from erecting even rudimentary shelters interfered with their rights to life, liberty, and security of the person under s. 7 of the *Charter***.

    The judge rejected the City's argument that the case was really about property rights. Instead, the claim concerned personal safety and bodily integrity. Because the bylaws were arbitrary and overbroad in light of the shelter shortage, **they violated s. 7 and could not be justified under s. 1.** The bylaws were declared inoperative insofar as they prevented homeless persons from erecting temporary shelter.

    **Key Legal Issue:** Does a municipal bylaw that prohibits homeless people from erecting temporary shelter on public property violate s. 7 of the Canadian *Charter* when there are insufficient shelter beds available?

    **Court's Reasoning**

    - Homeless people had **no realistic alternative**
        - Victoria had significantly more homeless people than shelter spaces.
        - Many homeless individuals were forced to sleep outside.
    - Shelter is **necessary for basic survival**
        - Exposure to rain, cold, and weather creates serious health risks.
        - Some form of overhead protection is necessary for physical safety.
    - The claim was **not about property rights**
        - The defendants were not claiming ownership or exclusive control of public land.
        - They sought only the ability to erect temporary shelter while sleeping outdoors.
    - Section 7 was engaged. The bylaw interfered with:
        - **Life** (health risks from exposure),
        - **Liberty** (freedom to make basic choices necessary for survival),
        - **Security of the person** (physical and psychological integrity).
    - The bylaw violated fundamental justice. The prohibition was:
        - **Arbitrary**
        - **Overbroad**

    ***Victoria v Adams*** stands for the principle that **governments cannot prohibit homeless individuals from erecting basic temporary shelter in public spaces when insufficient shelter alternatives exist**, because doing so violates s. 7 *Charter* rights to life, liberty, and security of the person.

!!! info "Governing the Belongings of the Precariously Housed: A Critical Legal Geography"

**(4) Protections for Property**

!!! info "B Ziff, *Taking Liberties: Protections for Private Property in Canada*"

    - **Absence of Express Property Rights:** The Canadian *Charter of Rights and Freedoms* does not explicitly protect private property against state confiscation, largely due to historical political opposition from stakeholders like Alberta and Prince Edward Island.
    - **Indirect *Charter* Protections:** Despite lacking explicit property clauses, the *Charter* **indirectly** affects property rights by **prohibiting unreasonable searches and seizures, and protecting freedom of expression, religion, and equality under law.**
    - **Regulation of Public Property:** The *Charter* restricts how the state can regulate public spaces, though Canadian courts have not developed extensive doctrines protecting access to quasi-public spaces like shopping centers.
    - **Non-Constitutional Statutory Protections:** The Canadian Bill of Rights (alongside provincial counterparts like Alberta's legislation) explicitly recognizes a right to the enjoyment of property and due process, though it applies primarily at the federal level.
    - **Limitations of Statutory Remedies:** Non-entrenched protections can be overridden by explicit legislative action, as demonstrated in ***Authorson v. Canada (Attorney General)***, [2003 SCC 39](https://canlii.ca/t/51p6){:target=" \_blank"}, where statutory immunity shielded the government from liability regarding veterans' funds.

**(c) Expropriation Provisions in Free Trade and International Investment Agreements**

- **Role of ISDS Clauses:** Investor-state dispute settlement (ISDS) clauses, prominently featured in agreements like NAFTA and its replacement, CUSMA, **allow foreign investors to bypass domestic courts** and sue states directly before international arbitration tribunals for breaching obligations to protect investments.
- **Expropriation and Compensation:** Treaties like NAFTA mandate **compensation for direct or indirect expropriation** (measures tantamount to nationalization), protecting foreign investors against the total or near-total deprivation of investment value.
- **Policy and Environmental Concerns:** Critics argue that takings regimes and broad investor protections can throttle domestic public policy, particularly environmental regulations and public welfare initiatives, by exposing governments to heavy financial liability.
- **Weaponization of ISDS:** Early rulings under NAFTA Chapter 11 demonstrated how foreign corporations could successfully challenge local environmental bans, public health measures, and municipal zoning decisions, prompting significant backlash from public interest and social movements.
- **Evolution in CUSMA:** The shift from NAFTA to the Canada-United States-Mexico Agreement (CUSMA) significantly **scaled back or eliminated ISDS mechanisms** between certain countries to restore a better balance between protecting foreign investors and preserving state sovereignty.
- **Defining Indirect Expropriation:** Modern agreements and international guidelines attempt to clarify the blurry line between compensable indirect expropriation and legitimate, non-discriminatory public policymaking (such as health and environmental rules) that should not trigger compensation.
