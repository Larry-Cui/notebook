## 4.1 The Concept of Possession

<div class="highlight green" markdown="1">
"Possession is very strong; rather more than nine points of the law."<br>
~ Lord Mansfield ***Corp of Kingston upon Hull v Horner*** (1774) Lofft. 576 at 591, 93 ER at 815.

“Possession is good against the whole world except the person who can shew good title.”<br>
~ ***Asher v Whitlock*** (1865) LR 1 QB1 at 5

“A person in possession of land in the assumed character of an owner and exercising peaceably the ordinary rights of ownership has a perfectly good title against all the world but the rightful owner.”<br>
~ ***Perry v Clissold*** [1907] AC 73 at 79
</div>

You may well have heard a version of the famous aphorism, reproduced above and attributed to the Earl of Mansfield (Lord Chief Justice of the Court of King’s Bench), that possession is nine points of, or nine-tenths of the law. 

In ***Corp of Kingston upon Hull v Horner*** (1774), the Mayor of Kingston Upon Hull claimed the right to levy duties on certain goods imported by water to the city. The claim was based on an argument that the corporation had possessed the right to levy import duties since the early as the 1400s. Lord Mansfield upheld the right based on long possession of the right. While not an accurate state of the law, the aphorism does speak to the importance that the **concept of possession** has played in the law of property in common law systems.

Property interests in land in common law systems are not absolute. Remember from Unit 2, **there is no concept of allodial title.** As a result, the important question is not who has absolute title, but rather who has the better claim to an interest in land? 

The second and third quotations reproduced above help to reveal the role that the concept of possession plays in determining **who has the better claim**. As you can see, the fact of possession places the possessor high up on the list of claimants, perhaps second only to the person who can demonstrate with a title deed or some other valid instrument of transfer that they are the “rightful owner”.

With the advent of **title registration systems**, the focus of Unit 7, **the concept of possession is less important than it once was when dealing with interests in land.** 

The doctrine of **adverse possession** (sometimes called **squatters’ rights**) is a much diminished, but still a lingering reminder of the importance that the doctrine of possession once played in resolving disputes over interests in land. However, **possession remains more significant in the realm of personal property**, and we begin with a dispute over a baseball.

## 4.2 Adverse Possession

Possession is more commonly a factor in disputes over goods, over personal property. However, **possession does continue to play a role with respect to land in the doctrine of adverse possession.** Adverse possession establishes the circumstances in which someone who is in actual possession of an interest in land can defeat the claim of someone else who holds title to that interest.

**A. The Test for Adverse Possession**

British Columbia has almost abolished adverse possession, but a small window remains open and claims continue to arise. 

The opening exists for land that has **not yet been registered** in the title registration system or where first registration is contested (*Land Title Act*, [RSBC 1996, c 250](https://www.bclaws.gov.bc.ca/civix/document/id/complete/statreg/96250_00){:target=" \_blank"}, s 23(4)), and where all the elements of successful claim in adverse possession have been **made out prior to 1975** (*Limitation Act*, [SBC 2012, c 13](https://www.bclaws.gov.bc.ca/civix/document/id/complete/statreg/12013_01){:target=" \_blank"}, s 28).

A claim of adverse possession works through limitations periods that establish time limits for when a title holders may bring actions against someone who is in possession of their land. **In British Columbia, those time limits were set at 20 years for private landowners and 60 years for the Crown.** 

As a result, in order to establish a claim to adverse possession, an adverse possessor had to establish **possession of land (including the intention to possess and actual occupation) for at least:** 

- **20 years prior to 1975** in the case of privately held land, and 
- **60 years** in the case of Crown land.

**In addition** to establishing possession, the adverse possessor must also establish that their possession was:

- open and notorious;
- exclusive;
- peaceful;
- without permission; and
- continuous.

The question in the recent Supreme Court of Canada decision, ***Nelson (City) v. Mowatt***, [2017 SCC 8](https://canlii.ca/t/gxjs8){:target=" \_blank"}, was whether the adverse possessor’s use of the property had also to be inconsistent with the owner’s present or future use of the land.

!!! note "Activity"

    Read the excerpt of the SCC decision in ***Nelson (City) v. Mowatt***, [2017 SCC 8](https://canlii.ca/t/gxjs8){:target=" \_blank"} in the *Reader*, **320-327**. 
    
The SCC **rejected inconsistent use as an element** in adverse possession in British Columbia, but upheld the trial court’s decision that the use had not been sufficiently continuous: there was a 4-year gap in the 1910s in which it appears that the lot was not occupied.

!!! danger ""

    **Q:** Does the need for continuity require that the same adverse possessor establish unbroken occupation?

    **A:** No. **An occupier’s time on the land can be passed to the next occupier**.

Although adverse possession claims continue to arise in British Columbia, their frequency will decline as we move further away from **1975** (**the year by which all the elements of a claim to adverse possession must be established**). 

In the land law of some other jurisdictions, particularly those that have not fully adopted title registration systems, adverse possession remains a continuing feature of their property law. ## B. Adverse Possession and Race

The relevance of the common law doctrine of adverse possession arose in the interpretation of Nova Scotia’s *Land Title Clarification Act*, [RSNS 1989, c 250](https://nslegislature.ca/sites/default/files/legc/statutes/land%20titles%20clarification.pdf){:target=" \_blank"}. 

Nova Scotia had introduced a precursor to this statute in the 1960s as one means to address **a racist land policy** in which Black settlers to Nova Scotia in the 18th and 19th centuries received “tickets of location” or “licences of occupation” to parcels of land while White settlers received title deeds. Without formal legal title to their land, Black settlers were not able to use it as security for a loan and nor could they pass it to their children or otherwise direct its disposition in a will. However, many descendants of the original Black settlers remained on the land, albeit without formal title. **The legislation created a process that was intended to enable the descendants of Black settlers to formalize their title to parcels of land within a number of designated areas.**

In 1995, Thomas and Ruby Beals obtained a certificate of title to a parcel of land in North Preston, a predominantly Black community outside Halifax, under the *Land Title Clarification Act*. They each died intestate in 2004 and 2009. Rosina Beals and Lionel Beals, two of their children, applied for a certificate of title under the *Land Titles Clarification Act*. They lived on the land and their siblings renounced any claim. However, the Minister rejected their claim under the *Land Titles Clarification Act* on the grounds that the legislation was designed and intended only to deal with land where title had not been granted. **Title had been granted in this instance, so the *Act* did not apply.**

Rosina and Lionel had turned to the *Act* because they could not afford the cost of probating the estate. They appealed the Minister’s decision on the grounds that it was not reasonable, but the NSSC ruled that the decision was reasonable. The legislation was intended to serve as a means to clarify title, something that had already occurred in this instance when their parents had received title certificates.

In the course of its ruling in ***Beals v. Nova Scotia (Attorney General)***, [2020 NSSC 60](https://canlii.ca/t/j58p8){:target=" \_blank"}, the NSSC took judicial notice of a number of historical facts relating to the history of the colony’s land policy.

Nova Scotia (Attorney General)***, [2020 NSSC 201](https://canlii.ca/t/j8jwb){:target=" \_blank"}, the NSSC relies on these facts in determining that the common law doctrine of adverse possession does not establish the standard that claimants must meet under the *Land Title Clarification Act*.

## 4.3 Finders

**Can the finder of property keep the found object? It depends.** 

If the person who lost the property， “the true owner”， claims the object, then he or she will usually be able to recover it. However, if the “true owner” does not come forward or has abandoned the property, then the finder will be able to keep the found object unless someone else has a prior subsisting claim. The basis of that prior subsisting claim is commonly a property interest in the location on which the object is found. 

As a result, the contest in most “finders” cases is **between the finder and the person on whose land the object was found.**

**Abandonment** is the mirror image of possession. An object is abandoned when its **owner has no intention to possess and loses or relinquishes physical control**.

## 4.4 The Transfer of Interests through Delivery

As we will investigate in more detail in Unit 6, there are a number of guiding principles or maxims that animate the body of rules of Equity. **Equity is a distinct branch of law that developed in the Court of Chancery alongside the three Common Law courts.** In the context of gifts, two equitable maxims are particularly important:

<div class="highlight green" markdown="1">
“equity will not assist the volunteer” and<br>
“equity will not complete an imperfect gift”.
</div>

**A complete gift** requires:

- the donor’s **intention to donate**;
- the donee’s **intention to accept or receive**; and
- **delivery** of the gift.

Here we focus on the concept of “delivery”, which was once an important element in the *inter vivos* transfer of all property interests, and **remains important in the realm of gratuitous transfers or gifts**. As you will see in the readings, “delivery” can include not just **physical delivery** of the item to be given, but also **“constructive delivery”**.

!!! danger ""

    **Q:** In addition to the two equitable maxims introduced above, the court adds another in its analysis. What is it?

    **A:** Possession is *prima facie* evidence of property

The judgment in ***Nolan v Nolan*** deals with **the third element of a complete gift**, **delivery**, even though the judge indicates that the analysis is unnecessary because **the second element**, **donative intent**, has not been established. Because the analysis of delivery is not necessary for the decision, that part of the judgment is understood, within the common law method, as *obiter dicta* and therefore not binding. However, it may well be persuasive authority. Moreover, this decision will only ever be persuasive in a Canadian court.

Note the description in the judgment of the different types of delivery, including **“actual” delivery** and **“constructive” delivery**. 

Constructive delivery may occur when **the donor gives “power and control to the exclusion of others” without actually handing over the object of the gift**. Examples include the **delivery of the keys** to a car or a safety deposit box. The object of the gift (the car or the contents of the safety deposit box) has not been delivered, but the donor has given effective control over them.

!!! danger ""

    **Q:** Can delivery precede the intention to give and to receive?

    **A:** Yes, **delivery may occur at any time**, “but must occur while the donative intention subsists. **At any stage until delivery occurs, the donor can validly retract the gift.**
