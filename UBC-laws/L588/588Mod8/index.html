    Module 8: Ethics, Advocacy & Criminal Law

    In this Module we return to the topic of advocacy but in a somewhat different context.

    Advocacy is of course central to a lawyer’s professional role. As we have reviewed there are several general ethical and professional obligations that attach to an advocate for a client. These obligations apply broadly for lawyers.

    In the criminal law context, however, advocacy takes on even more duties and obligations due to the greater stakes involved for a lawyer’s client: generally the client’s personal freedom is at stake. The entire legal power of the state may be deployed against a lawyer’s client. Therefore, there are special ethical duties and professional obligations relating to advocacy for both criminal defence lawyers and crown prosecutors.

    8.1: Historical Context & Cases

    Before we examine the modern era of ethics and criminal law, it is helpful to have some historical context. Two cases can help illustrate the evolution of criminal law ethics: Queen Caroline's Case & R v Boucher.

    Queen Caroline’s case

    The first is an English case involving legal proceedings brought in 1820 by King George IV against his estranged wife, Queen Caroline.

    A bill called the “Pains and Penalties Bill” was introduced in Parliament by the King to dissolve his marriage to Caroline and remove her royal title and privileges. The 1820 trial of Queen Caroline involved evidence of semi-public nudity in a hotel suite by a member of the royal family traveling abroad. It was the subject of intense discussion in both parliament and broader British society. For the first time the British media extensively covered legal proceedings containing salacious details involving the behaviour of the British monarchy. Witnesses supporting the King’s motion presented evidence that the Queen had been seen in the arms of her Italian lover in various states of undress during her travels and that they bathed together. One of the Queen Caroline’s Italian servants testified before the House of Lords that the Queen employed a male exotic dancer and demonstrated aspects of the dance before the assembled peers.

    Queen Caroline’s lawyer, Lord Henry Brougham, responded to accusations against the Queen by threatening to reveal secrets about George IV’s personal life that would damage the monarchy. The accusations leveled against the King and Queen during the proceedings had the potential of undermining the reputation of the British monarchy. If Brougham were to introduce the evidence in defence of his client, the Queen, it would have had the potential to throw the British monarchy into chaos. It was suggested to Brougham that his duty as a “good citizen” required him to promote the welfare of Britain and “pull his punches” by not asserting the right of recrimination against the King. Brougham responded with a now infamous speech which asserted that the zealous defence of his client is, essentially, all that matters.

    This case established the "Rule in Queen Caroline’s case."

    This is a common-law principle in criminal law that requires a cross-examiner to confront a witness with the contents of a prior inconsistent statement before the introduction of extrinsic evidence. The witness must be confronted with the time, place, persons present and the substance of an impeaching statement before extrinsic evidence could be admitted as proof that the statement had been made.

    The rule was laid down in the Queen Caroline's Case, (citation: 2 Br. & B. 284, 129 Eng. Rep. 976 (1820)) which states:

    If it be intended to bring the credit of a witness into question by proof of anything he may have said or declared touching the cause, the witness is first asked, upon cross-examination, whether or not he has said or declared that which is intended to be proved.

    Boucher v. The Queen, 1954 CanLII 3 (SCC)

    This case involves Boucher, who was charged with murder, but our concern here is with the closing address of crown prosecutor.

    One of the most quoted statements from Boucher on the proper role of Crown counsel in criminal proceedings is from pp 23-24 of the decision:

    It cannot be over-emphasized that the** purpose of a criminal prosecution is not to obtain a conviction**, it is to lay before a jury what the Crown considers to be credible evidence relevant to what is alleged to be a crime. Counsel have a duty to see that all available legal proof of the facts is presented: it should be done firmly and pressed to its legitimate strength but it must also be done fairly. The role of prosecutor excludes any notion 'of winning or losing; his function is a matter of public duty than which in civil life there can be none charged with greater personal responsibility. It is to be efficiently performed with an ingrained sense of the dignity, the seriousness and the justness of judicial proceedings.”

    8.2: B.C. Code & Advocacy in Criminal Law

    Rule 5.1 in general relates to the lawyer as advocate in adversarial proceedings. Many of the sub-sections of the rule pertain to criminal law and specifically the duty of criminal lawyers relating defence and prosecution.

    R v Johnson 2014 ONSC 2093 (cited as R. v. Cameron, 2014 ONSC 2093) involved an applicant trying to strike his guilty plea. The accused’s lawyer entered a guilty plea of trafficking cocaine and assault. Key points here are:

    • After plea was entered, Crown prosecutor read in the facts of the case. Defense counsel confirmed the accuracy of facts.
    • The accused person here, Johnson, said he never told his lawyer that he was guilty to the charges, and that he actually insisted to his lawyer that he was NOT guilty and wanted to go to trial. Apparently the lawyer thought that Johnson’s chances at trial were not good, and Johnson said that his lawyer advised him to just say “YES” to the guilty plea as read by the Judge.
    • Johnson then said he lied to court during pre-inquiry questioning because he was just following his lawyer’s instructions. He maintained his innocence and said he wanted to go to trial.

    R. v. Cameron, 2014 ONSC 2093

    The issue here became whether Johnson’s guilty plea was voluntarily given to his lawyer and accurately reflected his understanding of the facts of the case. Another issue involved whether Johnson’s lawyer properly advised him about the implications and possible consequences of the guilty plea.

    Defense counsel stated that he never conducted a “plea comprehension inquiry” with Johnson prior to his guilty plea, the inquiry is supposed to clarify with the accused that they are pleading guilty and consequences resulting from that.

    The problem though is that the Judge directly asked defense counsel if he’d had an opportunity to discuss the guilty plea with Johnson and counsel clearly said that he did discuss it, also confirming that the facts were correct, the same facts that Johnson was now disputing too.

    The court concluded that Johnson didn’t voluntarily enter his guilty plea, and was satisfied on balance of probabilities that he didn’t believe that he was guilty at any time.

    Court went on to say there was a “fraud” perpetrated on the court, and defense counsel was most responsible for this. Although Johnson was the one who plead guilty and lied to the court, the court said that defense counsel could not represent a client entering a guilty plea if that client doesn’t admit that they committed the offense. If that happens, the court said a fraud is done to the court, leading to a miscarriage of justice. They cited the Ontario Court rules, similar to the BC rules, which we just reviewed. Basically lawyers cannot assist in a client’s dishonesty.

    Court was harshly critical of defense counsel...they didn’t meet their professional obligations to court, breached duties to client and court in several ways...all flowing from the inappropriate guilty plea when the client was actually still maintaining his innocence. They were all serious breaches of counsel’s ethical obligations.

    Interesting question to this case that I want you to consider: what if you believe that the evidence is so overwhelming that a client couldn’t credibly maintain their innocence?

    MISLEADING THE COURT

    R. v. Legebokoff, 2016 BCCA 386

    Cody Alan Legebokoff appealed his four first-degree murder convictions under s. 686(1)(a)(iii) of the Criminal Code, alleging a miscarriage of justice based on an appearance of unfairness and a violation of his right to counsel of choice.

    Specifically, the issue was whether the trial judge had an affirmative duty to immediately disclose his view that defence counsel had acted unprofessionally and unethically during a pre-trial change-of-venue application—rather than waiting until after the trial and sentencing to release his written reasons (2014 BCSC 1794), and whether that delayed disclosure created an appearance of unfairness.

    Conclusion

    The Court of Appeal for British Columbia dismissed the appeal and upheld the convictions, concluding that:

    • No Duty to Disclose: A trial judge is not under an affirmative duty to immediately inform an accused of concerns regarding defence counsel’s conduct unless it is clear that counsel's continued involvement will imperil trial fairness or undermine public confidence in the administration of justice.

    • Judge's Criticisms Were Unfounded: Although not strictly required to resolve the appeal, the Court categorically rejected the trial judge's criticisms, finding that defence counsel acted properly within the adversarial system and did not mislead the court.

      • No Duty of Impartiality: The trial judge incorrectly assumed defence lawyers must be impartial. In an adversarial system, counsel are entitled to forcefully and fairly advocate for their client, provided they do not present false evidence.
      • Factual Errors by the Judge: The judge's claims of "distortion" and "hypocrisy" relied on minor typos (like writing "Anderson 360") and ignored the fact that universal networks (like CNN and CBS) and U.S. publications were already properly identified or understood on the record.

    • No Appearance of Unfairness: Because the judge had no duty to disclose his views earlier, the conduct occurred at an early pre-trial stage, the appellant was competently represented throughout the trial, and there was no suggestion the judge's views affected the trial's conduct, a well-informed, reasonable person's confidence in the administration of justice would not be shaken by the delayed release of the reasons.

    R. v. Jenkins, 2001 CanLII 28399 (ON SC)

    Issue

    Whether defence counsel, Mr. Powell, should be permitted to withdraw mid-trial—eight weeks into a first-degree murder trial and mid-way through the accused's cross-examination—after the accused disclosed confidential information that fundamentally contradicted the core of the defence already presented to the jury, creating a conflict between counsel's duty of solicitor-client confidentiality and his duty of candor not to deceive the court.

    Conclusion

    Justice Abbey granted the application to withdraw.

    While the Crown argued that counsel could remain on the record by adopting limited representation and simply remaining silent rather than actively advancing a falsehood, the court held that in these specific circumstances, silence alone in the face of what had already been presented to the court would constitute a deception of the tribunal and violate counsel's ethical duty. Because the application was made promptly, in good faith, and addressed a serious ethical conflict that limited representation could not cure, Mr. Powell was permitted to withdraw and facilitate an orderly transfer of the case to successor counsel.

    DEFENDING SEXUAL ASSAULT CASES

    Sexual assault cases are often an inherently more sensitive area of law, and even in civil cases involving sexual abuse, they are difficult hearings to conduct.

    In the criminal context you often have even more emotionally difficult testimony arising from survivors of alleged sexual assaults, particularly in context of cross-examination, i.e., cross-examination by an accused’s defense counsel. It can raise many questions on how the complainant is treated, particularly when questions are directed towards an alleged victim’s background or life history.

    Here's a criminal defense lawyer giving a CLE talk about working as criminal defense lawyer in sexual assault cases he’d handled in 1988. His advice:

    Generally if you “destroy the complainant and prosecution, you destroy the head. You cut off the head of the Crown’s case and the case is dead. My own experience is that the preliminquiry is the ideal place in a sexual assault trial to try to win it all.”
    Further down he says "You have to go in there as defense counsel and “whack the complainant hard." ...you get all the medical evidence, you get all the children’s aid society reports and you've got to attack the complainant with all you've got so that they will say "I'm not coming back in front of 12 good citizens to repeat this bullshit story I've just told the judge."

    This type of role and vision of defense counsel, especially in context of sexual assault criminal trials, was dealt with in R v Mills, which dealt directly with the production of a sexual assault complainant’s private records and Mills challenging the constitutionality of producing these criminal code records.

    In this SCC case, you had JJ MacLachlin and Iacobucci talk about many things like the constitutionality of Canada’s “Rape Shield Law.” It’s a long case but focusing just on ethical issue here, they talk about context here and equality concerns between the rights of an accused to defend themselves against criminal charges vs rights of complainant to privacy, understanding myths and stereotypes in context of sexual violence is essential to determining the limits of someone defending themselves against charges of sexual assault.

    R. v. Mills, 1999 CanLII 637 (SCC)

    The ethical issue here concerned the limits of cross-examination, The rights to a defence in this context vs rights of complainants.

    The key principle here is seeking the truth, the truth seeking goal of trials. The Court said that it had stated in previous decision that Charter provisions declaring equality between genders places limits on cross-examination. Also, that the complainant should not be unduly harassed and pilloried to extent of being a victim of an insensitive court system. The Court restated that eliciting evidence from complainant to make inferences relating to consent or credibility of victims, based on groundless myths or fantasized stereotypes, is not proper.

    THE ACCUSED IS NOT PERMITTED TO “WHACK THE COMPLAINANT” through the use of myths or stereotypes relating to sexual assault victims.

    • So what is “Whacking the complainant?”
    • After the court discussed some myths relating to sexual assault victims it defined “whacking” as tactics that seek to exploit myths and stereotypes inherent in sexual assault cases, in order to secure a favorable outcome for their client.

    Some of the questions to ask here in context of Mills:

    • What BC Code rules could be used to control “Whacking” a complainant?
      • Maybe 5.1-1, Duties of defense protecting an accused as far as possible within law of being convicted: relying on legal evidence & you can rely on technicalities or any defense as long as it is not false or fraudulent.
    • The SCC has reaffirmed that criminal defense counsel don’t have that same latitude in their cross examination that they may have in other criminal contexts.
      • Courts have repeated that in most adversarial contexts, cross examination is given wide latitude, sexual assault cases pose particular dangers and they have limits, cannot resort to myths or “folk tales” to destroy credibility of a complainant, or how abuse victims are supposed to act after trauma.
    • What does this mean in the context of the Code?
      • Defense Counsel have to work with facts, with the truth, their clients aren’t "second class litigants" but in the sexual assault context in particular they cannot resort to myths or stereotypes to destroy credibility of a complainant.
    • In a sense, this is taking the ethical obligations of all defense lawyers in criminal cases to rely on facts, but emphasizing the importance of not relying on disproven myths about supposed behaviour of victims in sexual assault cases.
    • Any conduct by a defence lawyer that promotes or exploits stereotypes in sexual assault cases violates their ethical duty to not act in a discriminatory fashion.
      • This would include cross-examination on what the complainant was wearing, whether they immediately reported the incident, whether they spoke to a psychiatrist, their socio-economic status, past drug or alcohol use or lifestyle, or marital status.
    • IMPORTANT NOTE: This is not to suggest that all cross-examination on these factors is improper.
      • The question is whether the implicit purpose of the cross-examination is to suggest that the complainant “is the kind of person to consent” or “the kind of person to lie about consent.” These are some of the classic rape myths.
    • I can tell you in working in the civil context, with hearings of sexual abuse and survivors testifying to institutionalized sexual abuse in residential schools by staff and other students,there are good reasons why these reasons exist...they are grounded in the importance of FACTS....
    • It doesn’t mean that the defense cannot pursue an aggressive defence, they can and should do that,
      • but they can’t resort to using things that aren’t factually proven, they can’t use disproven myths that MIGHT HAVE BEEN USED IN THE PAST about the conduct of sexual assault victims relating to credibility, consent, how they ”should” respond following traumatic act like sexual assault, and so forth.

    SCC said in Mills that “[t]he accused is not permitted to ‘whack the complainant’ through the use of stereotypes regarding victims of sexual assault.” The majority also appeared to recognize that this whacking was trying to be addressed by Parliament through Criminal Code revisions to producing evidence.

    Despite the Mills decision and changes to Criminal Code, “whacking” continues. As Professor Elaine Craig has observed,

    [it] seems implausible that members of the legal profession would...today continue to invoke stereotypes that are irrelevant, discriminatory and harmful, and outrageously outdated... Yet these are the stories the defence lawyers in Canada continue to tell about the women who have accused their clients of rape.

    In addition to a “whacking” style of advocacy, there is also what might be called “under the radar” conduct of defence lawyers that impairs the fair treatment and adjudication of sexual assault cases.

    Examples of this conduct include shielding accountability through cultural incompetence, deliberate ignorance of the client’s guilt and relying on objectively “truthful evidence” notwithstanding that it is being introduced for a false purpose.

    This conduct, in particular, does not lend itself well to judicial or evidence regulation because the trial judge is often unaware of what defence counsel knows or should know about their client’s guilt. While Parliament has taken the lead in reforming our substantive and procedural law, the necessary cultural change to give justice a chance in sexual assault cases requires leadership from the profession. Our Law Societies need to step in and clearly set out, in Commentaries to the relevant Rules and in Continuing Legal Education (CLE) programs, that advocacy or conduct grounded in stereotypical norms about sexual assault violates the ethical norms of the profession. They also need to push for greater cultural competence training in law school.

    • There is a lot of discussion about this in Canadian and American contexts about the limits of advocacy and a zealous defense by criminal defence lawyers in this context. Legal ethicist, Abby Smith talks about this, and the question of how much “unmitigated zeal” is necessary on behalf of people who are accused of doing terrible things.
    • Important to remember that background investigations into complainants are still conducted, I think even more so in the US context in criminal defense work and it is viewed as an important part of their work. You have to ensure though that any information gathered by those investigations can factually apply to help your client in the case.

    ACQUIRING KNOWLEDGE OF YOUR CLIENT’S GUILT

    In R. v. Delisle the accused was convicted of assault causing bodily harm and sentenced to four years imprisonment.

    This case is worth reviewing because it provides insight into the type of inadequate defence work that contributes to wrongful convictions.

    In this case the accused had maintained his innocence to his lawyer throughout, had indicated that he wanted to testify, and had identified a third party as one of the perpetrators. Defence counsel did not believe the accused’s story, did not interview the third party and refused to allow the accused to testify. Following the verdict of guilty, the third party admitted to the accused’s lawyer that the, and not the accused, was one of the perpetrators. The third party provided affidavit evidence which was tendered on appeal.

    The issue here was more professional than ethical, basically the competence, or incompetence, of the accused’s lawyer at his criminal trial.

    R. c. Delisle, 1999 CanLII 13578 (QC CA)

    The key points here involved the accused’s lawyer committing a significant error in preventing the accused from testifying. In the context of this case’s facts, the court found that was a major error involving a lack of competency on part of counsel, and also found the same issues in the lawyer’s not attempting to meet the third party identified by the accused as the perpetrator.

    The Quebec Court of Appeal held that a new trial be ordered.

    Basically the court found that the only thing that prevented a case of wrongful conviction here was that the third party came forward to provide the affidavit evidence. The court said that this was an event beyond the control of the judicial system, and had the 3rd party not come forward, it was very unlikely that a new trial would have been ordered and this would have been a case of wrongful conviction, with the accused being sentenced to four years in prison for a crime they did not commit.

    This case also has some comments on the ethical and legal principles related to lawyers and competency:

    The credibility of the judicial system would be seriously compromised if one were no longer able to count on the presence of competent counsel to defend the accused. What good would it do to create the best justice system and to ensure its continued existence through rights entrenched in the Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms if an accused is left in ignorance of his rights and is not properly speaking "defended" at his trial?

    The right to effective assistance of counsel, as a constituent element of the right to make full answer and defence and the right to a fair trial, has its origins in the common law, in the Criminal Code (s. 650(3)) and in ss. 11(d) and 7 of the Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms, as a principle of fundamental justice.

    From a larger perspective, this right to competent counsel constitutes one of the components of the right to effective assistance of counsel, just like the right to retain and instruct counsel, or even to the presence of counsel of one's choice, or the right to be represented by counsel who is not in a conflict of interest.

    8.3: Ethical & Professional Obligations: Criminal Defence Counsel

    The classic question asked by many law students, especially those who may find criminal law interesting, inevitably is some variation of the following:

    • Why/how can you do this work day in, day out?
    • Why/how can you defend some of these individuals?

    The textbook readings for this topic outline a number of reasons or explanations used by criminal defence lawyers to justify their work. In particular the “neutral-conduit” argument is very commonly used because it is very appealing.

    In the neutral-conduit view, defence lawyers do not just do their work for one individual, they do that work for society as a whole. Lawyers are a neutral conduit, or channel, for justice.

    This justification is practically also effective because an individual can be very unappealing and an individual crime (or series of crimes) can be horrendous. But society as a whole can only see justice done if those accused of these crimes have access of legal representation through fair procedures in a properly functioning justice system.

    Pleading Innocents Guilty:

    As we discussed in the previous topic, criminal charges are frequently dealt with through an accused pleading guilty. Most criminal charges never go to a full trial. There are several ethical considerations here that are mentioned in the Scott article and also covered in Rules 5.1-7 & 5.1-8 of the Code. They involve considerations on the part of both counsel in a criminal trial, defense and the prosecution. We will return to plea bargaining later in this Module to examine the prosecutor’s perspective.

    For now we are examining defense counsel’s role here. The case of R v Johnson (Cameron) involved an apparent guilty plea by an accused that turned out not to be quite a plea of guilty but something else...

    R. v. Cameron, 2014 ONSC 2093

    To paraphrase an essential point from Johnson, defense counsel cannot assist their client to plead guilty unless the client is absolutely clear on:

    • Admitting their guilt to the offence(s)
    • Admitting the facts required to prove the offence on the essential elements,

    Misleading the Court:

    • R v Legebokoff, 2016 BCCA 386
    • R v Jenkins, (2001), 152 CCC (3d) 426 (Ont Sup Ct)

    Being honest with the court is a cornerstone of the justice system. It is expected from all the parties and actors, including lawyers, accused and judges.

    In the Legebokoff and Jenkins cases we see different examples of conduct which called into question whether the court had been misled.

    In Legebokoff, the issue concerned the judge’s criticisms after the trial and in his decision concerning the conduct of defence counsel, specifically the manner in which defence counsel had conducted an application to change the venue of the trial. The accused (who was convicted) complained that this amounted to a miscarriage of justice.

    In Jenkins, we deal with the issue of client perjury. Lawyers generally do not have to accept a client who perjures themselves. They can generally withdraw from the case under those circumstances. However, there are many considerations when a lawyer opts to withdraw from a case. Jenkins deals with the ability and right of the lawyer to withdraw after a client has committed perjury.

    Acquiring Knowledge of Your Client’s Guilt:

    • R v Delisle, (1999), 133 CCC (3d) 541 (Que CA)

    Criminal defense counsel, of course, will in many cases have strong private opinions as to a client’s guilt or innocence. Nevertheless experienced defense counsel will not allow those opinions to interfere with their ethical and professional obligations.

    Criminal defense lawyers can cross a line into problematic situations in those cases when they allow their private opinion of a client’s guilt to prevail over their client’s assertions of innocence.

    The Delisle case is a good illustration of this scenario, where there was a failure to investigate combined with a disbelief on part of defence counsel which arguably interfered with his work, ethical and professional obligations.

    Defending Sexual Assault cases:

    • R v Mills, [1993] 3 S.C.R. 668

    How defence counsel treats sexual assault complainants at trial raises some serious issues about victim abuse and emotional trauma, justice for both an alleged victim and an accused, fairness and equality.

    In 1988, Cristin Schmitz, an experienced Canadian criminal defence lawyer, gave a talk at a continuing legal education event about the role of criminal defence counsel in sexual assault trials. The following is an excerpt from his talk:

    [W]hack the complainant hard” at the preliminary inquiry.
    Generally, if you destroy the complainant in a prosecution...you destroy the head. You cut off the head of the Crown’s case and the case is dead. My own experience is the preliminary inquiry is the ideal place in a sexual assault trial to try and win it all. You can do things... with a complainant at a preliminary inquiry in front of a judge which you never try to do for tactical, strategic reasons, sympathy of the witness, etc., in front of a jury... [A]nd you’ve got to attack the complainant with all you’ve got so that he or she will say [‘]I’m not coming back in front of 12 good citizens to repeat this bullshit story that I’ve just told the judge.[’]
    ~ Cristin Schmitz, "‘Whack’ Sex Assault Complainant at Preliminary Inquiry" The Lawyers Weekly (May 27, 1988) at 22

    Over 10 years later, the Supreme Court of Canada decision in R v Mills dealt with the constitutionality of a provision of the Criminal Code of Canada that created a rule excluding certain kinds of records referred to by Schmitz at this talk. The Supreme Court also critically addressed his “whack the complainant” advice to criminal defence lawyers in sexual assault cases.

    Module 8 - Discussion Activity: Case law & Criminal law ethics

    Please choose one of the following discussions or scenarios to respond to:

    DISCUSSION #1

    Recently in the news, we learned that Dr. Tracy Pickett, director of B.C. Women's Hospital sexual assault service, committed suicide and her body was found in a local park.

    We then found out that it was her first time testifying as an expert witness on the injuries of a deal person, specifically, on a murder trial regarding the death of a 13-year old girl in a Burnaby, BC park. The accused, Ibrahim Ali, a refugee from Syria, is accused of sexually assaulting her and then strangling her to death.

    As a result, she had failed to show up and finish her cross-examination. Dr. Picket had testified in direct that the constellation of injuries found on the girl "very strongly indicates non-consensual activity." During cross-examination defence counsel went on to pose the following question "Looking at any one of these injuries individually, you can't say consent or non-consent just based on looking at the injury, right?" and Dr. Picket responded "No, I cannot." See the article here.

    Given the timing of her suicide, some suspect it may have been related to her role in trial and the vigorous cross-examination she was exposed to as an expert witness.

    Discuss whether the cross-examination of the expert by defence should be considered over-zealous by suggesting that a thirteen year old engaged in consensual sex prior to being murdered as a possible defence of his client, whose DNA was located inside her vaginal areas.

    Answer

    Issue

    Whether defence counsel's cross-examination of Dr. Tracy Pickett was overzealous or ethically improper by suggesting that the 13-year-old victim may have engaged in consensual sexual activity before her murder.

    Rules

    Under Rule 5.1-1 of the BC Code, a lawyer acting as an advocate must represent the client resolutely and honourably within the limits of the law. Criminal defence counsel may challenge the prosecution's evidence and advance any lawful defence, but must not rely on false or fraudulent arguments.

    In R. v. Legebokoff, the court recognized that defence counsel is entitled to advocate forcefully and fairly for their clients. They are not required to be impartial, provided they do not mislead the court or present false evidence.

    However, the right to vigorous advocacy has particular limits in sexual assault cases. In R. v. Mills, the Supreme Court emphasized that cross-examination must respect the complainant's dignity and equality. Defence counsel cannot exploit myths or stereotypes about sexual assault victims to undermine their credibility or suggest consent without a proper factual basis. The Court rejected tactics aimed at humiliating or unfairly discrediting complainants rather than establishing the truth. Defence counsel may vigorously challenge the prosecution's evidence, but must not rely on unsupported allegations or stereotypes about the victim.

    Application

    Defence counsel challenged Dr. Tracy’s conclusion by asking whether any individual injury could independently establish consent or non-consent. Dr. Pickett acknowledged that it could not.

    This question appears to be a legitimate examination of the limits of her expert opinion. Defence counsel was entitled to test the strength of the medical evidence and whether it supported the prosecution's conclusion. However, if defence counsel relied on the implied suggestion without supporting evidence, merely to create doubt or influence the jury through unfounded and improper assumptions, the argument could cross the ethical boundary identified in Mills.

    Conclusion

    In my view, defence counsel's suggestion from his questions was overzealous and ethically questionable. Although counsel had the right to challenge Dr. Tracy medical evidence, using the uncertainty surrounding individual injuries to suggest consent, without a proper factual basis, went beyond legitimate advocacy. As emphasized in Mills, the right to a vigorous defence does not justify relying on unfounded assumptions or stereotypes in sexual assault cases.

    DISCUSSION #2

    Consider the calls for greater use of expert witnesses to protect victims of sexual assault and consider whether the introduction of expert witnesses and time limits on cross-examination would properly balance the needs of defence counsel to zealously advocate for their clients.

    Answer

    In my view, greater use of expert witnesses and reasonable time limits on cross-examination would properly balance the protection of sexual assault complainants with the accused's right to a fair trial.

    Expert witnesses can help judges and juries understand the psychological effects of trauma and avoid misconceptions about sexual assault victims. As recognized in R. v. Mills, the accused's right to make full answer and defence must be balanced against the privacy and equality interests of complainants. Expert testimony can promote this balance by reducing reliance on stereotypes when assessing evidence. Reasonable time limits on cross-examination would also protect complainants from unnecessarily prolonged or repetitive questioning. Under Rule 5.1-1 of the BC Code, defence counsel must advocate resolutely and honourably within the limits of the law. Zealous advocacy does not require unlimited questioning, particularly where it causes unnecessary distress without contributing to the truth-finding process. Judicially managed time limits would encourage focused questioning while preserving legitimate challenges to the prosecution's evidence.

    Ultimately, these measures would strengthen rather than undermine the fairness of sexual assault trials. Effective advocacy should be measured by the relevance and quality of questioning, not its duration or aggressiveness. Protecting complainants from unnecessary harm is entirely consistent with defence counsel's professional obligations and the accused's right to a fair trial.

    DISCUSSION #3

    A Manitoba lawyer hired a private investigator to spy on a Judge. Review the most recent article here and discuss the ethical and professional responsibility issues raised by Lawyer A's conduct. In your answer, analyze one of the following issues:

    • A lawyer's duties to the court and the administration of justice.
    • The importance of judicial independence and why lawyers must avoid conduct that could reasonably be perceived as intimidating, influencing, or interfering with a judge.
    • Whether hiring a private investigator to surveil a judge involved in a pending case constitutes professional misconduct, even if no direct contact with the judge occurs.
    • A lawyer's obligations regarding honesty, integrity, and preservation of evidence when facing regulatory or criminal scrutiny.
    • The ethical implications of deleting communications and instructing others to destroy potentially relevant records.
    • The relationship between a lawyer's personal conduct and the reputation of the legal profession.
    • The significance of remorse, acceptance of responsibility, and candour in disciplinary proceedings.
    • The appropriate disciplinary response where a lawyer engages in conduct that risks undermining public confidence in the independence and impartiality of the judiciary.

    Answer

    1. A lawyer's duties to the court and the administration of justice.

    A lawyer's duty to the court goes beyond representing the client's interests. Under BC Code Rule 5.1-1, lawyers must advocate resolutely and honourably within the limits of the law. As discussed in R. v. Legebokoff, lawyers may advocate forcefully but must not mislead the court. Lawyer A's decision to surveil a judge presiding over his case crossed that boundary. It undermined the respect and integrity that lawyers owe to the administration of justice.

    2. The importance of judicial independence and why lawyers must avoid conduct that could reasonably be perceived as intimidating, influencing, or interfering with a judge.

    Judicial independence requires judges to decide cases without pressure or interference. Hiring a private investigator to follow a judge involved in a pending case creates a reasonable concern that the lawyer is seeking information to pressure or discredit the judge. Even without evidence of actual influence, such conduct threatens public confidence in judicial impartiality. Lawyers must challenge judicial decisions through proper legal procedures, not personal surveillance.

    3. Whether hiring a private investigator to surveil a judge involved in a pending case constitutes professional misconduct, even if no direct contact with the judge occurs.

    In my view, hiring a private investigator to surveil a judge involved in an ongoing case constitutes serious professional misconduct, even without direct contact. The investigator acts on the lawyer's instructions, and the surveillance itself can reasonably be perceived as intimidation or interference. Lawyer A's claim that the surveillance was merely passive does not remove his professional responsibility. The article reports that he admitted a breach of integrity in disciplinary proceedings.

    4. A lawyer's obligations regarding honesty, integrity, and preservation of evidence when facing regulatory or criminal scrutiny.

    Lawyers must act honestly and preserve potentially relevant evidence when facing an investigation. In R. v. Jenkins, the court recognized that a lawyer's duty of candour may prevent even remaining silent where silence would deceive the court. Although that case involved different circumstances, it illustrates the importance of honesty in legal proceedings. Lawyer A's efforts to conceal communications with the investigator were inconsistent with these professional obligations.

    5. The ethical implications of deleting communications and instructing others to destroy potentially relevant records.

    Lawyer A's decision to delete emails and instruct the investigator to destroy records was particularly serious. According to The Tyee, the disciplinary panel rejected the explanation that the emails were deleted for security reasons and considered the actions a deliberate attempt to conceal misconduct. In my view, deliberately destroying potentially relevant records shows dishonesty and undermines the integrity of legal investigations. Lawyers should preserve evidence and cooperate honestly with lawful investigations.

    6. The relationship between a lawyer's personal conduct and the reputation of the legal profession.

    A lawyer's professional responsibilities extend beyond courtroom appearances. Even conduct outside formal legal proceedings can damage public confidence in the profession. Lawyer A's surveillance of a judge and subsequent attempts to conceal it raised serious concerns about his integrity. The public expects lawyers to respect the justice system, not undermine it. Such conduct damages the reputation of the entire profession.

    7. The significance of remorse, acceptance of responsibility, and candour in disciplinary proceedings.

    Genuine remorse and acceptance of responsibility are important in disciplinary proceedings because they demonstrate that a lawyer understands the seriousness of the misconduct. Lawyer A initially apologized and acknowledged his breach of professional obligations, but later described the criminal proceedings as politically motivated and minimized the surveillance. In my view, these inconsistent positions raise doubts about the sincerity of his remorse and whether he has fully accepted responsibility.

    8. The appropriate disciplinary response where a lawyer engages in conduct that risks undermining public confidence in the independence and impartiality of the judiciary.

    In my view, serious disciplinary sanctions were justified because surveilling a judge threatens judicial independence and public confidence in the justice system. According to the article, Lawyer A received a lifetime prohibition on practising in Manitoba and agreed to a three-year prohibition across Canada as part of the resolution of his criminal charges. Such sanctions reflect the seriousness of the misconduct and help protect the public. Professional discipline should also make clear that lawyers cannot interfere with judicial independence, regardless of their personal beliefs or the interests they represent.

    8.4: Ethical & Professional Obligations: Crown Prosecutors

    In this final topic we examine the role of prosecutors in the context of criminal law ethics and professional responsibilities.

    One of the important concepts here is the exercise of “prosecutorial discretion.” When we examine the cases in this topic, you will see that many of them that engage ethical and professional issues turn on exercising (or failing to exercise) this discretion.

    Public Prosecution Service of Canada Deskbook

    The federal Public Prosecution Service of Canada publishes a “Deskbook” that is regularly updated and is meant to guide prosecutors in “objective, fair, transparent and consistent” conduct, as described in the Preface to the Deskbook:

    The Public Prosecution Service of Canada Deskbook (PPSC Deskbook) is a means of achieving this goal. It is a compilation of the directives and guidelines that provide instruction and guidance to federal prosecutors, whether employees of the PPSC or private-sector agents, in the exercise of their prosecutorial discretion. It is therefore essential that the Deskbook be consulted, understood, and adhered to by federal prosecutors.

    In applying the Deskbook to the case before them, federal prosecutors make decisions without the fear of political interference or improper or undue influence. They are, however, accountable to the Director of Public Prosecutions and, through the Director, to the Attorney General, Parliament and ultimately, the Canadian public for the way in which they have exercised this important responsibility.

    The Deskbook is a permanent work in progress: all federal prosecutors should be conscious of the need to suggest changes where policies are unclear or outdated. PPSC managers are obliged to monitor application of the policies to ensure they are properly applied.

    In particular, you may want to look at the following specific duties of Crown Prosecutors that are outlined in the Deskbook prior to our examination of the cases that follow:

    • The duty to ensure that the mandate of the Director is carried out with integrity and dignity.
    • The duty to maintain judicial independence.
    • The duty to be fair and to maintain public confidence in prosecutorial fairness.
    • The duty to maintain objectivity.
      • Inflammatory remarks and conduct.

    Activities

    Please review the following summaries that will review regulating the conduct of crown prosecutors and highlight the key points of the cases in this topic.

    Regulation of Prosecutors by Law Society:

    Consider some of the issues of regulating Crown Prosecutors conduct through the Law Society vs regulation by their employer, the Government of Canada.

    If you examine the statement above from the Deskbook preface, you will see that accountability in regards to exercising discretion is directed specifically to the “Director of Public Prosecutions and, through the Director, to the Attorney General, Parliament and ultimately, the Canadian public.”

    The question then arose as to whether Crown Prosecutors conduct inside or outside of prosecutorial discretion would also be subject to regulation by a Law Society. This was an issue in the Krieger case.

    Krieger v. Law Society of Alberta, 2002 SCC 65

    The issues involved here were duty to disclose (on the part of a prosecutor) and disciplinary powers and jurisdiction of a disciplinary body.

    the facts involved a prosecutor in a murder case failing to disclose relevant information and an accused filed a complaint with the law society.

    The prosecutor challenged the law society’s jurisdiction and the Supreme Court of Canada was called upon to hold whether the law society had jurisdiction. To review the allegation that the prosecutor had acted dishonourably or in bad faith in failing to disclosure the information.

    The SCC ultimately did decide that the law society had jurisdiction, it was constitutional matter, they had jurisdiction to regulate the legal profession in Canada pursuant s.92.13 Constitutional Act.

    Prosecutors, as members of a law society, must be members of the law society and therefore subject to code of professional conduct. All conduct that is not protected by the doctrine of prosecutorial discretion is subject to the conduct review process as the disclosure of relevant evidence is not a matter of prosecutorial discretion but rather a legal duty, the law society possess jurisdiction to to review an allegation that a prosector acting honestly or in bad faith failed to disclosure relevant information.

    Here we are looking at prosecutorial discretion versus professional misconduct.

    One of the key issues here is the limited powers of the Attorney General (AG). An AG can find that a prosecution has acted in bad faith and can remove them in extreme cases, remove them, but they can’t restrict their membership with the law society or suspend them.

    Consider Code 5.1-3 and the application here.

    Regulation by Courts

    The Anderson case concerned two issues before the Supreme Court of Canada:

    • (1) whether Section 7 of Canada’s Charter of Rights and Freedoms requires the Crown Prosecutor to consider and give notice of an accused’s Aboriginal status when making decisions that limit the sentencing options available to a judge and
    • (2) whether the decision to tender a Notice to seek greater punishment, without considering such status, is a matter of “core” prosecutorial discretion, and if so, the standard by which it may be reviewed.

    When you are reading the decision pay particular attention to the second issue as it directly involves the concept of “prosecutorial discretion” and its regulation here by the court in a constitutional challenge.

    R. v. Anderson, 2014 SCC 41

    Should internal discipline be made public for prosecutors? Are the guidelines provided to prosecutors more guidelines or aspirational things that are similar to the codes of ethics? Do these constitute policy guidelines that courts can rely upon by law societies and disciplinary hearings?

    ... an accused with Aboriginal status, pled guilty to impaired driving charges (this was his fifth conviction) and the crown served a notice of seeking a mandatory minimum sentence of not less than 120 days imprisonment.

    Anderson's counsel argued that the proviso in the criminal code imposing the mandatory sentence and requiring the filing of the notice violated his s.7 rights and it transferred what is a judicial function from the judicial branch to prosecutor himself. He also argued that it violated s.15 of the Charter, by depriving an Aboriginal person from an opportunity to argue for a non-custodial sentence.

    The SCC rejected the constitutional arguments of Anderson’s counsel.

    The SCC held that the constitutional argument conflated the role of the prosecutor and the sentencing judge by imposing upon the prosecutor a duty that applies only to judges.

    The SCC also did not accept that imposing a constitutional requirement on Crown prosecutors to consider an accused’s Aboriginal status and exercising prosecutorial discretion was consistent with fundamental justice.

    The Court’s opinion was rather that this would be contrary to long standing and deeply rooted approaches in Canada and to the division of responsibility between the Crown prosecutor and the courts. It would make a matter that is not properly subject to judicial oversight up for routine judicial review and the court said that would be contrary to Canadian constitutional traditions.

    The court did hold that the decision to issue the notice was a matter of prosecutorial discretion. It was only reviewable for abuse of process and it said that prosecutors have independence in the exercise of their discretion to ensure their freedom from judicial or political interference.

    This is where the court starts to talk about the Krieger decision. The court outlines some clarifications. They said that prosecutorial discretion is a single expansive term that covers all decisions regarding the nature and extent of the prosecution and the Attorney General’s participation in it.

    It covers a wide range of prosecutorial decision making including things like repudiation of plea agreement, pursing a dangerous offender application, proceeding by summary conviction or indictment charges, charging an accused with a single offence or multiple offences. It also includes negotiating a guilty plea and agreement on sentencing, pursuing an appeal, agreement on joint submission and those sorts of matters. It includes tendering the notice given that it fundamentally alters the extent of prosecutor, that was the court’s fundamental resonating.

    What does prosecutorial discretion not include?

    It does not include tactics or conduct before the court or the satisfaction of constitutional obligations, such as providing proper disclosure. What the court said is that prosecutorial discretion provides no shield to a Crown prosecutor who has failed to fulfill their constitutional obligations such as the duty to provide proper disclosure to the defence.

    Prosecutorial discretion can also be reviewed for abuse of process to determine if it undermines the integrity of the judicial process or results in unfairness in a trial. Also relevant there, they can examine improper motives, acting in bad faith and if a Crown decision was motivated by prejudice against Aboriginal persons it would certainly meet the standard.

    Crown prosecutors are not bound to provide reasons for their decision in all instances. To require the Crown to explain its decision the applicant must establish a proper evidentiary foundation to the allegation of an abuse of process. Absent that, prosecutorial authorities do not have to provide reasons for their decisions, absent evidence of bad faith or improper motives.

    What the court said here is that a failure to comply with a Crown policy may be relevant to establishing an evidentiary threshold but in itself, Crown policies and guidelines do not have the force of law. They cannot themselves be subject to charter scrutiny in the abstract.

    Tactical decisions by lawyers are subject to a high degree of deference. Sanctions should be directed at the conduct of the litigants but not to the ligation itself.

    This is a fairly complex decision.

    Abuse of process refers to crown conduct that is egregious and undermines trial fairness and or the integrity of the justice system. Is this standard appropriate? Rigorous? Insufficient?

    Exercise of Prosecutorial Discretion: Charging/Screening Decisions

    The R v K(M) case involved the exercise of discretion in laying charges.

    The court considered the issue of whether prosecutorial discretion was subject to review by the court. Part of the considerations in this case involved whether charges should have been laid at all in a case such as this. The court considered the effects on an overloaded judicial system when discretion relating to the laying of charges is not properly exercised.

    When it comes to prosecutorial discretion, you should know that there is a little bit of a difference within Canada in terms of the Crown prosecutor’s role in charging: The decision whether to lay a charge against someone or whether to screen the charges.

    • In some provinces (British Columbia, Quebec and New Brunswick), prosecutors in Canada are involved in the decision to lay a charge.
    • For the rest of Canada, charges are laid by police and Crown are responsible or screening and determining if they should proceed to trial.

    Under both, whether to charge or whether to screen the charges there are general principles that prosecutors must adhere to. They are only to prosecute an offence when there is a reasonable prospect of conviction and it is in the public interest to do so.

    So that leaves the issues of mandatory charge or prosecution policies that remove the public interest.

    For British Columbia, Crown prosecutors are involved in the decision of whether to lay charges.

    R. v. K. (M.), 1992 CanLII 2765 (MB CA)

    R. v. K (M) is Manitoba case that involved a decision to screen the charge. The facts of this case were an argument between a father and a mother over their eight year old son. The son opened a package of sunflower seeds which fell on the carpet. Their two year old picked up the seeds and started to choke on them. The father punished the eight year old boy by physically hitting him and kicking him with his foot. The mother reported the father and he was charged with assault.

    The mother later attempted to reconcile however the charge was not withdrawn. A trial judge convicted the father of common assault and he filed an appeal. The appeal was allowed by the Manitoba Court of Appeal. The court said that prosecutorial discretion at the laying of charges is subject to judicial review although only in the absolute clearest of cases.

    In this case the court said that it was not sound policy to mandate that every violation of the law requires laying of charges in prosecuting a case.

    Such a policy would nullify prosecutorial discretion which would be exercised in favour of such values like family life. The court’s task in this case was not one of determining whether the child may or may not be kicked. This case could not be judged by a nice calculation as to the degree of force and in the judge’s opinion this case should never have been proceeded with.

    The judge talked in the opening paragraph about rising crime rates in Winnipeg and the situation getting very bad where citizens were getting afraid to walk at night, mugging, sexual assaults, an over-burdened police force and burgeoning crime in general and then the judge opined that instead of going after real criminals men and women who wantonly attack innocent neighbours, the whole engine of the state has been concentrated in this case in the prosecution of a citizen who has been accused of using excessive force in the disciplining of his children.

    If you were the prosecutor, would you have withdrawn the charge? Why or why not?

    Would you be more troubled by the prosecution of the father or perhaps the comments of the Court of Appeal or some of the comments of the Judge?

    Do you see any pros or cons in terms of policies for mandatory polices regarding pursuing certain types of cases?

    Plea Bargaining Re-Visited: The Prosecutor’s Duty

    With Nixon, we revisit the issue of plea bargaining in the context of a plea resolution agreement.

    The Crown withdrew a plea resolution agreement after determining that it was not in the administration of justice. The accused appealed, ultimately reaching the Supreme Court of Canada. The main issue was whether there was a constitutional violation of the accused’s rights and an abuse of process, and also considered the exercise of prosecutorial discretion.

    R. v. Nixon, 2011 SCC 34

    We have already discussed some of the professional considerations related to plea bargaining and the obligations of criminal defence counsel and Crown prosecutors. Nixon is a 2011 Supreme Court of Canada decision.

    It dealt with an accused driving her vehicle through a stop sign. It collided with another vehicle, killing the husband and wife and seriously injuring the child. The accused was charged with several offences including driving causing death, impaired driving causing bodily harm, dangerous driving causing death. Ultimately there was a plea resolution agreement reached between Crown and the defence.

    Before the guilty plea was entered, the Assistant Deputy Minister (ADM) determined that the plea was not in the best interests of the administration of justice and so the Crown withdrew the plea.

    At trial, the accused pled guilty to careless driving and was acquitted on the remaining counts. The Crown’s appeal was allowed and the trial judge’s orders were set aside and then the accused appealed to the Supreme Court of Canada. Ultimately the Supreme Court of Canada dismissed the appeal. The analysis here hinged on whether there was a violation of the accused’s s.7 rights pursuant to the Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms. The court found no basis for finding a violation of s. 7 rights.

    What the court looked at was the ADM’s decision to repudiate the plea agreement and the court said that it fell very clearly within the core elements of prosecutorial discretion. Here the prosecutorial discretion was not spent with the decision to initiate proceedings nor did it end with the plea agreement. The court said that as long as the proceedings were ongoing, and the Crown was still required to make further decisions about whether the prosecution should be continued, or how it should be conducted, prosecutorial discretion applies.

    The decision to proceed with the prosecution was a decision for the Crown alone to make, absent a number of exceptions. So the ADM’s determination in this case that the Crown Counsel’s assessment of this case was erroneous, that was their decision and having regard to the seriousness of the offences, the conclusion that it was not in the public interest to terminate the prosecution could hardly be regarded as evidence of misconduct.

    The Prosecutor Calling Witnesses

    One of the most important decisions a prosecutor has to make involves whether or not to call witnesses. The decision can have a decisive impact on both defence counsel’s conduct and the ultimate ruling in the case.

    For example, a decision by the prosecution to not call a witness may compel the defense to call that witness. This affects important strategic decisions such as the ability to cross-examine a witness.

    A Supreme Court of Canada decision in R. v. Cook, 1997 CanLII 392 (SCC) dealt with the issue of whether the Crown has a mandatory duty to call certain witnesses as part of its case. The Cook decision and this issue is referenced and revisited in the Hillis decision that we are examining next.

    R. v Hillis, 2016 ONSC 451

    • General Rule of Prosecutorial Discretion:

      • The court acknowledged that, as a general rule established in cases like R. v. Cook, the Crown has the discretion to choose which witnesses it will or will not call, and how it presents its case. These decisions are generally immune from judicial review unless they involve an "oblique motive," an abuse of process, or result in unfairness and prejudice to the accused.

    • The Duty of Fairness and the Role of the Crown:

      • Justice Pomerance emphasized that the Crown is not a standard litigant seeking a conviction at all costs, but rather a "quasi-minister of justice" and custodian of the public interest. The court held that the Crown may not omit reliable exculpatory evidence from its case solely because it might help the accused. Holding back evidence to assist the prosecution's chances of winning violates the Crown's core mandate to present relevant, credible evidence fairly.

    • Tactical Disadvantages to the Defence:

      • While full disclosure of witness statements had been made to the defence, the Crown's strategy of dropping crucial witnesses (such as police officers and a blood spatter expert who could provide exculpatory or neutral expert evidence) forced the defence to call them instead. The court noted that this unfairly disadvantages the defence, as a party generally cannot cross-examine its own witnesses and forfeits the right to address the jury last.

    • Conclusion

      • The court concluded that this case qualified as one of those exceptional situations where judicial intervention is required.
      • Justice Pomerance ruled against the Crown's strategic omission of the witnesses and issued an order directing the Crown to call the specified witnesses as part of its case at trial.

    The Prosecutor Presenting the Case

    Recall at the beginning of this Module that we reviewed R v Boucher, which dealt with a crown prosecutor’s closing address to a jury. Almost a half century later, this issue was revisited again in Levert. When you read this case and listen to the analysis, can you identify why it was problematic?

    R. v. Levert, 2001 CanLII 8606 (ON CA)

    The Court of Appeal for Ontario addressed the Crown prosecutor’s closing remarks regarding his own role in the trial, where he told the jury he was "not in this to win or lose" and that "the concept of winning is foreign to the Crown prosecutor."

    • Risk of Invidious Comparison: Rosenberg J.A. expressed concern over Crown counsel explicitly defining their role to the jury. Doing so risks creating an unfair comparison between the prosecution and the defence, potentially leading jurors to give more weight to the Crown’s submissions as "objective" while discounting defence counsel's arguments as biased.

    • Parallels to Boucher v. The King: The court noted that such remarks come perilously close to the conduct criticized by the Supreme Court of Canada in Boucher v. The King (1954). In Boucher, Rand J. warned that emphasizing the Crown's neutral attitude can mislead unsophisticated jurors into thinking an impartial authority has already investigated the facts and determined the accused is guilty, usurping the jury's exclusive role as fact-finder.

    • Actions Over Words: While Crown counsel's phrasing was drawn directly from Rand J.’s famous quote in Boucher, that a prosecutor's function excludes winning or losing and is a matter of public duty, Rosenberg J.A. emphasized that prosecutors should demonstrate fairness through even-handed conduct rather than "wrapping themselves in the Boucher flag."

    The Court’s Conclusion

    • No Prejudice to Trial Fairness: Although the court stated that comments of this nature are "best avoided," it concluded that they did not render the trial unfair.

    • Distinction from Boucher: Unlike the prosecutor in Boucher, Crown counsel in Levert did not suggest, expressly or implicitly, that he had conducted a pre-trial investigation and determined the appellant was guilty, and he explicitly reminded the jury to convict only if satisfied beyond a reasonable doubt. The court also doubted the jury was so naïve as to unfairly discount the defence's submissions based on the remark.

    • Overall Disposition: While the court did find a separate Crown remark (characterizing the complainant as the "perfect victim") to be improper, it held that none of the Crown's conduct caused a miscarriage of justice or brought the administration of justice into disrepute, and ultimately dismissed the appeal.

    8.5: Summary

    In this Module we learned that ethical and professional obligations in the criminal law area vary depending upon the context and parties involved. However, in general the obligations are more stringent here than in the non-criminal, civil context. Defense counsel have duties that tend to enhance existing obligations from non-criminal litigation.

    The BC Code itself acknowledges this by its separate treatment of criminal lawyers, as does government policy in dealing with its own lawyers.

    The important takeaway from this section to recall both the enhanced duties in criminal law context and also the enhanced difficulties in carrying out these duties, because of the emotional and professional considerations that are often involved in these cases.
