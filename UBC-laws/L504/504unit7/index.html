    Title registration is now the dominant system for recording interests in land in the Canadian common law provinces. The particulars of each title registration system vary from province to province, but the basic structures are similar.

    This unit draws on cases from several jurisdictions to develop the foundational principles, but the focus is on British Columbia’s title registration system as an example of how title registration systems operate. The instances where the rule in British Columbia diverges significantly from the norm are noted.

    7.1 Introduction to Title Registration

    South Australia introduced the first title registration system in the common law world in the mid-nineteenth century. That system, labelled Torrens title after its principal proponent Robert Torrens, was emulated in many colonies around the British Empire, including western Canada.

    England would adopt another variation of title registration, and Ontario would base its system on that model.

    Title registration systems provide one method for recording interests in land. The common law deeds system is another, and many jurisdictions, including most the states in the United States, chose to retain and augment it with a deeds registry.

    The important difference between title registration systems and deeds registries is that the former operate to guarantee title, while the latter simply facilitate the discovery of who holds title. This is something of a simplification, but the guarantee of title is the foundational and defining element of title registration.

    Unit 6 set out the priority rules at common law and how the nemo dat quod non habet maxim operates to protect existing or settled interests in land, providing a form of static security.

    The guarantee of title in registration systems, on the other hand, operates to minimise the risks of a transaction in land. In doing so, title registration systems shift the risk of a transaction from the purchaser, who bore all the risk at common law, to the holder of an interest in land. As a result, there is a policy choice to prefer dynamic over static security in the implementation of title registration.

    The fee simple is the cornerstone of a title registration system. “Title” usually refers to the fee simple interest; the person with the fee simple interest holds title.

    Once a fee simple is registered, all other (and lesser) interests in land are registered against the fee simple interest. In British Columbia, life estates, easements, mortgages, etc. are all registered against a fee simple interest as charges.

    Title registration systems are built around four general principles:

    • the registration principle;
    • the indefeasibility principle;
    • the abolition of notice principle; and
    • the assurance principle

    These principles, and the sections that animate them in the British Columbia system, are set out below.

    A. Registration Principle

    Unit 5 described the transfer of property interests as a two-step process:

    • the contract for purchase and sale; and
    • the transfer.

    The contract sets out the terms of purchase and sale. At common law, the transfer of an interest in land occurred when the vendor signed, sealed and delivered the title deed. Remember Stevie Wonder’s Signed, Sealed, Delivered I’m Yours from Unit 5.

    s. 20

    Unregistered instrument does not pass estate

    20(1) Except as against the person making it, an instrument purporting to transfer, charge, deal with or affect land or an estate or interest in land does not operate to pass an estate or interest, either at law or in equity, in the land unless the instrument is registered in compliance with this Act.

    (2) An instrument referred to in subsection (1) confers on every person benefited by it and on every person claiming through or under the person benefited, whether by descent, purchase or otherwise, the right

    • (a) to apply to have the instrument registered, and
    • (b) in proceedings incidental or auxiliary to registration, to use the names of all parties to the instrument, whether or not a party has since died or become legally incapacitated.

    (3) Subsection (1) does not apply to a lease or agreement for lease for a term not exceeding 3 years if there is actual occupation under the lease or agreement.

    The registration principle, embodied in British Columbia’s Land Title Act, RSBC 1996 c 250, section 20, provides that no interest in land transfers until the transferring instrument is registered. It is not delivery of a transfer instrument that marks the transfer, but rather the registration of that instrument.

    However, note the exception that leads off section 20: “except as against the person making it.” This means that so far as everyone else in the world (everyone except the transferor) is concerned, nothing transfers until the transfer instrument is registered. However, so far as the transferor is concerned, although they may remain on title until the transferee registers the transfer instrument, the interest is gone to the transferee.

    B. The Indefeasibility Principle

    The concept of indefeasible title lies at the heart of title registration systems.

    The indefeasibility principle provides that once an interest in land is registered, then any defects in title are cured. The curtain metaphor is also often used to describe this principle. Registration draws a curtain on all past transactions and provides conclusive evidence that the person registered as the owner of an interest is the owner of that interest.

    This principle is embodied in the Land Title Act, RSBC 1996 c 250, section 23(2):

    23(2) An indefeasible title, as long as it remains in force and uncancelled, is conclusive evidence at law and in equity, as against the Crown and all other persons, that the person named in the title as registered owner is indefeasibly entitled to an estate in fee simple to the land described in the indefeasible title ...

    This is the guarantee of title. In British Columbia, the concept of indefeasibility, the guarantee of title, only applies to fee simple interests, not to charges. This is crucial distinction and one that we will come back to when we turn to charges (all lesser interests in land) later in this unit.

    C. The Abolition of Notice

    Do you remember the role that the doctrine of notice played in the common law priorities discussed in Unit 6?

    The bona fide purchaser for value of a legal interest without notice of a prior equitable interest has priority over the holder of the prior equitable interest.

    Many title registration systems purport to abolish the doctrine of notice. In that case, prior but unregistered interests do not affect title. In this way, the registry can serve, to use another common metaphor, as a mirror that reflects the state of title. Purchasers may rely on this reflection as an accurate statement of the state of title. They do not need to concern themselves with unregistered interests.

    The abolition of notice in British Columbia’s title registration system appears in the Land Title Act, RSBC 1996 c 250, section 29(2). The language in the section seems clear and unequivocal that purchasers need only to be concerned with registered interests in land. However, as we will see below, it is far from clear whether or not the province’s title registration system abolishes the doctrine of notice.

    D. The Assurance Principle

    At common law owners who were wrongfully deprived of their interests in land because of fraud would recover their interests. The fraudster had nothing to give, so nothing transferred to the person purporting to acquire the interest. This is the effect of nemo dat quod non habet (meaning "no one can give what they do not have").

    However, under title registration systems, the guarantee of title that accompanies registration means that the holder of an interest in land who is deprived of that interest because of fraud might not be able to recover their interest. In place of returning an interest to the person deprived of land because of fraud, many title registration systems provide compensation. This is the assurance principle and embodied in the Land Title Act, RSBC 1996 c 250, section 296(2).

    s. 296(2)

    Remedies of person deprived of land

    296(2) A person, in this Part referred to as the "claimant",

    • (a)who is deprived of any estate or interest in land

      • (i) because of the conclusiveness of the register, in circumstances where, if this Act had not been passed, the claimant would have been entitled to recover the land from the present owner, and
      • (ii) in consequence of fraud or a wrongful act in respect of the registration of a person other than the claimant as owner of the land, and

    • (b)who is barred by this Act or by any other Act, or otherwise precluded from bringing an action

      • (i) for possession, or any other remedy for the recovery of land, or
      • (ii) for rectification of the register,

    may, subject to subsections (3) and (4), proceed in court for the recovery of damages against the person by whose fraud or wrongful act the claimant has been deprived of the land.

    7.2 Indefeasible Title & Fraud

    Under the indefeasibility principle, the person who is registered as the holder of an interest in land is the holder of that interest. Registration is conclusive proof of ownership.

    But what about circumstances were the person is on title because of fraud? If the person on title is a party to the fraud, then the answer is simple: registration does not protect the fraudster. There is a clear fraud exception to indefeasibility set out in the Land Title Act, RSBC 1996 c 250, section 23(2)(i).

    s. 23(2)(i)

    Effect of indefeasible title

    23(2) ... subject to the following:

    • (i) the right of a person deprived of land to show fraud, including forgery, in which the registered owner has participated in any degree;

    However, when the person on title is a bona fide purchaser for value who has acquired the interest without knowledge that they have dealt with a rogue, then the answer is more complicated.

    • At common law, the doctrine of nemo dat quod non habet meant that someone who purchased on the basis of a forged instrument acquired nothing; the rogue had nothing to give.
    • In title registration systems, the bona fide purchaser may acquire title even if they have acquired that interest from a rogue, but when and if they do acquire title depends on the nature of the system.

    The core of title registration is the concept of indefeasible title. This is the guarantee of title. However, there are two different approaches to indefeasible title when it comes to fraud: deferred indefeasibility and immediate indefeasibility.

    7.3 Registration of Charges

    In British Columbia, the concept of indefeasibility only applies to the fee simple interest.

    All other interests in land, known as charges, are treated differently.

    Instead of being “indefeasibility entitled” to the registered fee simple interest (Land Title Act, RSBC 1996 c 250, s 23(2)), the holder of a registered charge is only “deemed to be entitled” (Land Title Act, RSBC 1996 c 250, s 26 (1)). As a result, instead of registration guaranteeing the interest, as it does with a fee simple interest, registration of a charge only creates a rebuttable presumption.

    7.4 Title Registration and the Abolition of Notice

    As you know from the discussion of common law and equitable priorities in Unit 6, a legal interest may displace a prior equitable interest if the legal interest is acquired by bona fide purchaser for value without notice of the prior equitable interest.

    One of the principles of title registration is the abolition of the common law doctrine of notice. The intent of this principle to allow people who deal with property to rely on the registry. The purpose is to reduce the risk for those transacting in property.

    However, as you will see in the cases and the other material, notice may still matter if the actions of the party with notice of the prior unregistered interest amount to fraud. In equity, notice of a prior interest and the attempt to defeat that prior interest amounts to fraud. This is labelled “equitable fraud”.

    The question in British Columbia is whether the “except in the case of fraud” provision at the beginning of Land Title Act, RSBC 1996 c 250, section 29(2) includes equitable fraud.

    • Does notice amount to fraud? Or,
    • is there only fraud from the purposes of the fraud exception in section 29(2) if there is notice and some additional wrongful or fraudulent act?

    The answer is not so clear.

    The dispute in Szabo v. Janeil Enterprises Ltd., et al, 2006 BCSC 502 involved an unregistered easement.

In this case, the easement involved a water pipeline that crossed one property to serve another property.

    The first issue in the cases involving notice of an unregistered property interest is whether the party involved had notice of the unregistered interest. You will see in Land Title Act, RSBC 1996 c 250, section 29(2) that notice may be “express, implied, or constructive.” How does the court characterize the form of notice in Szabo v Janeil?

    The second issue involves the effect or impact of notice. In this, there are different approaches in the case law. As you read Szabo v Janeil note the court’s description of the different approaches to the abolition of notice in the caselaw.

    The different approaches can be seen in Jager The Cleaner v. LI's Investments Co. Ltd., 1979 CanLII 329 (BC SC) as the standard-bearer of one approach and Woodwest Developments Ltd. v. Met-Tec Installations Ltd., 1982 CanLII 3944 (BC SC), as representative of the other.

    Note the following statement in Jager that:

    The question in every case must be whether a fraud would in fact be committed if the purchaser were to claim the protection of the Act; fraud, which is never lightly to be inferred, must, I think, be established by the particular facts of the case and cannot be presumed.

    Read the article by Douglas Harris and May Au, “Title Registration and the Abolition of Notice” (2014) 47 UBCLR, on the history of the abolition of notice principle and its current expression across title registration jurisdictions.

    T.G. Youdan, The Length of a Title Search in Ontario

    At common law, title to land is not absolute. It is based on possession and it is relative to the rights of others. We say that a person owns land when he has a better right to possession of it than others. Moreover, a vendor can only prove his title by showing “that he got it lawfully from someone else, and that someone else from someone else, and so on.”

    Proof of title was facilitated by the use of an abstract. There were two main parts to this process. First, the vendor (usually by his solicitor) “verified” the abstract by,

    ... producing for examination by the purchaser or his solicitor the original deeds or documents abstracted, and the probates or office copies of the wills and other documents, of which the originals cannot be produced; also by furnishing proper evidence of every fact material to title.

    The second part of the process was the examination of the abstract and this was ordinarily done by conveyancing counsel, instructed by the purchaser's solicitor, who would also put forward objections and requisitions arising out of the abstract.

    The rule eventually established was that, in the absence of contractual provision to the contrary, the vendor was required to show a chain of title back to a good root of title at least sixty years old.

    From the eighteenth century onwards various registration systems emerged. All were designed to minimize the purchaser's risk in land transactions. The first-generation statutes established what are now called deeds registration systems.

    Deeds registration systems encourage the owners of property interests to register their rights. The title deeds are noted in the government records and the documents are stored in a state-operated registry office. In general terms, a purchaser can rely on the registry to ascertain what interests currently exist in the property. The register and the documents can then be inspected by a prospective purchaser to determine who owns what.

    However, deeds registration systems provide no guarantee that the seller has a valid title. The fact that a document is registered does not mean that it is authentic. Therefore, purchasers are still required to conduct a search of the chain of title to establish the veracity of each link.

    There are three main variations of deeds registration systems.

    • In one, priority is based on the order of registration. This is referred to as a race system; the first to register is accorded priority.
    • In a second approach, the key effect of registration is to afford notice of a prior right: purchaser is deemed to have notice of all registered instruments and therefore takes their interest subject to whatever effect notice of the prior interest might have under the priority rules at common law and in equity.
    • A third approach, and the most common deeds system in Canada, is a hybrid of the two: a so-called “race-notice” system. Under a common form of this system, priority is accorded to an otherwise valid subsequent interest if two circumstances exist:
      • the second interest is acquired without notice of the first (usually constructive notice is considered insufficient), and
      • the subsequent interest is registered first.

    Greg Taylor: The Law of the Land: The Advent of the Torrens System in Canada

    Unlike common law or deeds registration systems where the holder of an interest in land is always subject to the claim of the person wrongfully deprived of that interest, title registration guarantees that the person registered as the holder of title is the title holder. To use the common metaphor, title registration draws a curtain between the registry and all prior transactions, making the latter irrelevant to the existing state of title. With few minor exceptions, registration cures any defects in title.

    To varying degrees, title registration systems also abolish the common law doctrine of notice; the holder of a registered interest is unaffected by notice of unregistered interests.

    The Colony of Vancouver Island became the first jurisdiction in what would become Canada and the second common law jurisdiction anywhere to adopt title registration when it passed the Land Registry Act 1860. The act introduced a two-tier system in which a titleholder acquired “absolute” title on first registration, but could only acquire “absolute and indefeasible” title (the state guarantee of title) five years after first registration. British Columbia abandoned the stepped approach to indefeasible title when it abolished absolute titles in 1921; thereafter, registered titles were indefeasible.

    Taylor notes little appetite for the system in Quebec, either historically or today. He attributes this to the difficulties that several centuries of European settlement would pose to creating a single land registry, to the challenge of adapting Torrens registration to a civil law tradition, and to “a general lack of enthusiasm for legal imperialism from English Canada.”

    D.C. Harris, Indefeasible Title in British Columbia: A Comment on the November 2005 Amendments to the Land Title Act

    Indefeasible Title

    [I]f at common law a forged instrument in the chain of title might undermine an owner's interest, in a title registration system the principle of indefeasibility secures the registered owner's interest unless that person has participated in fraud to acquire that interest.

    With this exception, title registration operates on the principle that the person named in the registry as the owner of an interest in land is the owner of that interest. As a result, prospective purchasers have to go no further than the registry to determine conclusively who holds title. The effect is to simplify the transfer of property interests and to reduce the risk for those acquiring interests. Title registration systems protect the sanctity of the transaction; they provide dynamic security.

    Within title registration systems there are two variations of the indefeasibility principle: immediate indefeasibility and deferred indefeasibility.

    • Under the former, the person acquiring an interest in land holds indefeasible title even if they acquire their interest, acting in good faith, on the basis of a forged instrument.
    • In a deferred system, indefeasible title is delayed until the person acquiring the interest does so from the person who is the registered owner and is, therefore, at least one step removed from the rogue and the forged transfer instrument.

    Title registration systems mark a clear departure from the common law where the person wrongfully deprived would recover their interest. It reflects a choice in jurisdictions with title registration systems to privilege the sanctity of the transaction over settled property interests.

    Lawrence v. Maple Trust Company, 2007 ONCA 74

    EE Gillese JA:

    Ownership of a person's home is fraudulently transferred. The property is then mortgaged. In a contest between the two innocent parties: the homeowner and the lender of mortgage monies, who wins?

    This appeal answers that question in favour of the homeowner.

    The issue in this case is simply enough stated. Maple Trust innocently acquired its interest in the Property in good faith, for valuable consideration and without notice of any fraud. In those circumstances, is the charge against the Property in favour of Maple Trust valid and enforceable as against the true owner of the property, despite having been acquired from a fraudster?

    Ontario's Position

    Ontario advances a view of the legislation based on the theory of “deferred indefeasibility”. On this theory, there are three classes of parties:

    • the original owner;
    • the intermediate owner, who is the person who dealt with the party responsible for the fraud; and,
    • the deferred owner, a bona fide purchaser or encumbrancer for value without notice who takes from the intermediate owner.

    Only a deferred owner would defeat the original owner's title. This is because the intermediate owner, as the party who acquired an interest in title from the fraudster, had an opportunity to investigate the transaction and avoid the fraud whereas the deferred owner did not.

    On the theory of deferred indefeasibility, registration of a void instrument does not cure its defect, thus neither the instrument nor its registration gives good title. However, good title can be obtained by a deferred owner from an intermediate owner.

    In the instant case, Ms. Lawrence is the original owner and Maple Trust, having dealt with the fraudster, Thomas Wright, is the intermediate owner. There is no deferred owner. Had an innocent deferred owner been involved, however, Ontario maintains that the innocent deferred owner would have obtained good title as against the original owner.

    Analysis

    Deferred indefeasibility is consistent with s. 155, ... it is consistent also with an interpretation of s. 68(1) that recognizes the common law principle that a fraudster can never take good title so as to become the owner but also enables Maple Trust to be recognized as the registered owner for the purposes of the deferred owner. As Maple Trust registered its charge and its charge was not obtained by fraud, a bona fide purchaser for value without notice (i.e. a deferred owner) could rely on a combination of ss. 68(1) and 78(4) to take an indefeasible interest from Maple Trust.

    Deferred indefeasibility is consistent also with an historical analysis of what is now s. 78. Further and most importantly, in my view, deferred indefeasibility is also preferable for policy reasons. Under the theory of immediate indefeasibility, the innocent homeowner has no defence to a mortgagee's action for possession. The homeowner is exposed to the loss of her home through eviction with the only available remedy being to make a claim for loss of value of the property from the Fund. The idea that a person who buys a specific parcel of land with a specific house on it should be compensated in damages runs contrary to the notion that real property, in such circumstances, is not fungible.

    Moreover, unlike the intermediate owner, the homeowner has no opportunity to avoid the fraud.

    Conclusion

    Under the theory of deferred indefeasibility, the party acquiring an interest in land from the party responsible for the fraud (the “intermediate owner”) is vulnerable to a claim from the true owner because the intermediate owner had an opportunity to avoid the fraud. However, any subsequent purchaser or encumbrancer (the “deferred owner”) has no such opportunity. Therefore, in accord with s. 78(4) and the theory of deferred indefeasibility, the deferred owner acquires an interest in the property that is good as against all the world.

    Gill v. Bucholtz, 2009 BCCA 137

    [1] This appeal ... raises some basic questions about the Torrens land registration system of this province that have not previously reached this court. Historically, the relevant provisions of the Land Title Act, R.S.B.C. 1996, c. 250 (the “Act”) and its predecessor R.S.B.C. 1979, c. 219, gave “mixed if not conflicting signals” as to whether the system was one of ‘deferred’ or ‘immediate’ indefeasibility.

    [3] The facts obviously raised for interpretation the central ‘indefeasibility section’ of the Act, s. 23, and the ‘fraud exception’ in s. 23(2)(i). At the date of registration of the first mortgage in favour of Mr. and Mrs. Bucholtz, it read as follows:

    • An indefeasible title, as long as it remains in force and uncancelled, is conclusive evidence at law and in equity, as against the Crown and all other persons, that the person named in the title is indefeasibly entitled to an estate in fee simple to the land described in the indefeasible title, subject to the following:
      • (i) the right of a person deprived of land to show fraud, including forgery, in which the registered owner has participated in any degree;

    [4] At paras. 11-6 of his reasons, the chambers judge outlined three principles he took from the foregoing provisions of the Act.

    • The first was the “indefeasible quality of title as long as it remains ‘in force and uncancelled’”, subject to the exception that “as against a titleholder who has participated to any degree in a fraud in acquiring title, the true owner can recover title” under s. 23(2)(i).
    • Second, he noted that the Act does not give to a registered charge, such as a mortgage, the indefeasible quality afforded to a registered fee simple interest. The holder of a charge that has been registered is merely “deemed” by s. 26(1) to be entitled to the interest reflected in the instrument. As this court ruled in Credit Foncier Franco-Canadien v. Bennett (1963) 1963 CanLII 839 (BC CA), 43 W.W.R. 545, the words “shall be deemed” in the section that corresponded to what is now s. 26(1) establish only a rebuttable presumption of entitlement. In the result, a mortgage forged by a fraudster who was purporting to be the registered owner remained a nullity even though it had been registered and then assigned to a bona fide lender.

    [5] The third principle noted by the chambers judge, in relation to registered interests less than fee simple, of nemo dat quod non habet. In the chambers judge’s words, “one cannot validly or effectually give that which one does not have.

    [17] It is often said (echoing the Privy Council in Gibbs) that the goal of a Torrens system is to protect any person relying on the register. But all Torrens schemes are not the same. ... the legislative goal may or may not be to protect all persons dealing with the registered owner. Some Torrens statutes, for example, provide immediate indefeasibility, while others provide deferred indefeasibility ...

    [18] Section 23(2) is of course of central importance. It establishes that an indefeasible title is conclusive evidence as against all persons: that the “person named in the title as registered owner” is “indefeasibly entitled to an estate in fee simple to the land”, subject to the listed exceptions, including sub-para. (i), “the right of a person deprived of land to show fraud, including forgery, in which the registered owner has participated”. (My emphasis.) On its plain meaning, this section deals with title to fee simple belonging to a registered owner. It does not on its face extend to lesser interests, and the exception is not limited to any particular time, e.g., at such time as the title remains in force and is uncancelled. It is, in other words, a true exception to the indefeasibility of title held by a registered owner.

    [19] Section 26 deals with registered charges. It provides:

    • (1) A registered owner of a charge is deemed to be entitled to the estate, interest or claim created or evidenced by the instrument in respect of which the charge is registered, subject to the exceptions, registered charges and endorsements that appear on or are deemed to be incorporated in the register.

    • (2) Registration of a charge does not constitute a determination by the registrar that the instrument in respect of which the charge is registered creates or evidences an estate or interest in the land or that the charge is enforceable.

    As was emphasized in Credit Foncier, the underlined wording is considerably weaker than the ‘indefeasible’ and ‘conclusive’ wording of s. 23(2).

    • Section 26(1) states only that the registered owner of a charge is “deemed to be entitled” to the estate or interest on the register, subject to appropriate exceptions; and
    • s. 26(2), to which there was no counterpart when Credit Foncier was decided, now warns that the registration of a charge does not constitute a determination by the registrar that the charge in fact creates an interest in land or that it is enforceable.

    [26] On its plain meaning, the exception in s. 23(2)(i) to the indefeasibility of title applies and the phrase “void instrument” in s. 25.1(1) includes a mortgage taken from a person who obtained her title by fraud or forgery, as occurred in this case. The Act preserves the nemo dat rule with respect to charges, even where the holder has relied on the register and dealt bona fide with a non-fictitious registered owner. The mortgagees in this case did not acquire any estate or interest in Lot 4 on registration of their instruments because having been granted by a person who had no interest to give, those instruments were void, both at common law and under s. 25.1(1).

    [27] The Legislature of British Columbia would appear to have adopted the policy that the cost of frauds perpetrated against mortgagees and other chargeholders should be borne not by the public (as the funders of the Assurance Fund) but by lenders and other chargeholders themselves. Whether this policy choice is a good one or not is not for us to decide. We must give effect to the language of the statute in its ordinary and grammatical meaning.

    [28] I would allow the appeal and order that the mortgages be cancelled as encumbrances against the plaintiff’s title.

    Douglas Harris and May Au, Title Registration and the Abolition of Notice (2014)

    Systems of land law must balance competing goals of securing title for existing interests in land with facilitating their transfer.

    Title registration systems operate to facilitate transfers of interests in land. They reflect a choice to enhance the security of transfers of interests, providing what has been characterized as dynamic security at the expense of the static security of existing interests.

    One of the cardinal principles of title registration is the abolition of the doctrine of notice.

    • In equity, if purchasers of a legal interest have notice of a prior equitable interest, then they take their interest subject to that prior interest. To do otherwise is to perpetrate a fraud.
    • Most title registration systems abolish notice; prior unregistered interests do not affect purchasers who register their interests, whether or not they have notice of the prior interest, except, so many title registration statutes provide, in the case of fraud.

    This article investigates the evolution of provisions purporting to abolish notice in Torrens title jurisdictions, it describes the variety of provisions that emerged, it reviews the longstanding uncertainty in British Columbia over the extent to which the doctrine of notice is abolished, and it considers a number of proposals for reform.

    It concludes that the uncertainty is a function of an unresolved policy choice between static and dynamic security, and that the British Columbia Court of Appeal or the legislature needs to intervene to clarify that choice.
