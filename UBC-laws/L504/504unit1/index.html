    504A A99_B 2025W1 Property Law Unit 1

    The Nature of Property

    Overview

    This unit introduces the idea of property, asks why rights to private property exist, situates the emergence of property doctrine in histories of racism and colonialism, and investigates how various justifications for property are deployed in novel claims to private property. In doing so, it introduces ideas about the nature and function of the institution of property, in common law and Indigenous legal orders, that recur throughout the course.

    At the end of this unit, students should be able to:

    • Begin to locate the emergence of property in histories of liberalism, racism, and colonialism;
    • Identify the different categories of property and explain their principal characteristics;
    • Describe and classify the different understandings of the idea of property;
    • Explain common property as well as the ideas of the “tragedy of the commons” and “tragedy of the anti-commons”;
    • Describe the idea of stare decisis and the role of precedent in the common law;
    • Recognize different elements in a judgment, including the decision of the majority, concurring decisions, and dissenting decisions;
    • Describe and evaluate the different justificatory traditions for private property;
    • Recognize when decision-makers are using one or more of the justifications for private property in their determinations about property rights;
    • Evaluate claims for rights to private property based on the various justifications for private property;
    • Build connections between particular property rules and their social context; and
    • Describe the doctrine numerus clausus.

    1.1 What is Property?

    A. Introduction

    This section introduces the idea of property as a right, or a bundle of rights, not a thing. It presents a broad typology of property, differentiating between private property, common property, and state property. The unit then considers competing understandings of private property and asks how those different understandings inform the judgments in a dispute over hunting crocodiles and Native title claims in Australia.

    Finally, it examines what is arguably the core interest in a property right, the right to exclude, in the context of a labour dispute where the place of work is a grocery store in a shopping centre.

    Activity

    Read the preface to the Reader, iii-v, and the excerpts from CB MacPherson and Gary Potts, 1-9.

    What do you think Potts means by “authority comes from the land itself”?

    MacPherson introduces several key ideas including that of “Property as a Right, Not a Thing”. He also describes various types of property:

    • private property,
    • common property, and
    • state property.

    Note his emphasis on private property as the right to exclude and on common property as the right not to be excluded. We will return to the right to exclude below.

    Gary Potts introduces another perspective on the relationship between people and the land in the excerpt from “The Land is the Boss: How stewardship can bring us together.” Potts was the Teme Augama Anishinabai Chief who led the blockading that sought to prevent road building and logging in Anishinabai territory in the 1980s.

    In 1973, the Teme Augama Anishnabai registered a caution in Ontario’s land title office against 10,000 kms/sq of Crown land. The effect of a caution is to freeze all action on that title until the issue for which the caution has been placed has been resolved and the caution lifted. The province pursued legal action to remove the caution and was successful in the Ontario courts, so in the late 1980s it issued logging permits. The Teme Augama Aninishinabai blockaded and appealed their case to the Supreme Court of Canada. That court would rule against them in 1991 in Ontario (Attorney General) v. Bear Island Foundation, 1991 CanLII 75 (SCC).

    B. The Right to Exclude

    CB MacPherson identified the right to exclude and the right not to be excluded as the core concepts of private and common property. The extent to which the capacity to exclude lies at the heart of private property is much debated.

    Activity

    Read the excerpts from Thomas Merrill on the right to exclude in the Reader, 16-22, and then Cheryl Harris on “Whiteness as Property”, 9-16.

    Can you write an epigram that provides a functional definition for either “state property” or “common property”? Post your epigram to the “Property Epigrams” discussion thread.

    Thomas Merrill canvases the shared and contested ground in different attempts to articulate one or more essential constituent elements of property, or to deny that there are essential elements. In doing so, he refers to the famous epigram for private property penned by the US legal scholar and lawyer, Felix Cohen:

    To the world:

    Keep off X unless you have my permission, which I may grant or withhold.

    Signed: Private Citizen

    Endorsed: the state

    Each part of the epigram reveals something important about the nature of property.

    • “To the world” distinguishes property from contract. In contract law, rights and responsibilities are created by parties through agreements or bargains; only the contracting parties hold these rights or responsibilities. On the other hand, property rights are claims against everyone else in the world. Property law defines relations between the person claiming the interest and everyone else, not just those with whom she has a contractual relationship.

    • “Keep off” emphasizes the importance of the capacity to exclude others.

    • “X” may refer to a tangible or intangible object.

    • “Unless you have my permission, which I may grant or withhold” reveals the capacity of the person holding the property interest to act as a gatekeeper.

    • The signature line and endorsement reveal the private and the public aspects of private property. It is the individual who holds the right and has the decision-making power, but that power is backed by the power of the state.

    Cheryl Harris connects the right to exclude as the conceptual core of private property with the strategies of domination and power that were deployed to appropriate the land of Indigenous Americans and the labour of Black Americans. In this, Harris argues that whiteness was a form of property that provided immunity from slavery and that legitimize the possession of land by whites.

    The origins of whiteness as property lie in the parallel systems of domination of Black and Native American peoples out of which were created racially contingent forms of property and property rights. I further argue that whiteness shares the critical characteristics of property even as the meaning of property has changed over time. In particular, whiteness and property share a common premise, a conceptual nucleus, of a right to exclude.

    As a result, Harris argues that the “origins of property rights in the United States are rooted in racial domination” and built around “the construction of whiteness as property”. How might you understand her argument as applying in a Canadian context, or in the context of the country where you are studying?

    C. Essentialist and Nominalist Definitions of Private Property

    The decision from Australia’s High Court (the equivalent of the Supreme Court of Canada) in Yanner v Eaton involves a claim to Native Title. You will find a brief overview of the case in the headnote, beginning at 22 in the Reader.

    To presage a later discussion, one of the crucial differences between Native title in Australia and Aboriginal title in Canada is that the former is statute-based while the later is embedded in Canada’s constitution. One of the implications is that in Australian law, the state can extinguish Native title with the grant of an inconsistent right. In Canada, the law requires the clear and plain intention to extinguish Aboriginal title, not just the grant of an inconsistent right. Moreover, the capacity to extinguish Aboriginal title, even where the state includes a clear and plain intention, is constrained by the protection of Aboriginal rights and title in section 35 of the Canadian Constitution. We will focus on Aboriginal title in Unit 5.

    Activity

    Read the excerpt from Yanner v Eaton in the Reader, at 22-29. The decision revolves around the Australian High Court’s interpretation of the meaning of “property” in the Fauna Act, section 7:

    • All fauna, save fauna taken or kept during an open season with respect to that fauna, is the property of the Crown and under the control of the Fauna Authority.

    Under Australia law, Native Title rights can be extinguished by the Crown’s grant of an inconsistent right. The issue is whether the Fauna Act, by making fauna (including crocodiles) “the property of the Crown”, extinguishes a Native Title claim to hunt crocodiles. The answer turns on the definition of property.

    We are interested in this case less for its discussion of Native Title than for the different definitions of property within it.     ‘property’ which the Fauna Act and its predecessors vested in the Crown was therefore no more than the aggregate of various rights of control by the Executive that the legislation created.

    What about Justice McHugh’s statement in his dissenting judgment on page 27:

    Whatever else property may mean in a particular context, it describes a relationship between owner and object by reference to the power of the owner to deal with the object to the exclusion of all others, except a joint owner.

    Categorize the decisions of Gleenson J and McHugh J using Merrill's typology: single variable essentialism; multi-variable essentialism; and nominalism. Where does each fit?

    Can you begin to see some of the implications of the lack of consensus about the core meaning of property?

    D. The Right to Exclude in Context

    If the right to exclude sits at the core of the idea of private property (something about which there is considerable disagreement), then how does and should the context affect the application of that right? Harrison v. Carswell, 1975 CanLII 160 (SCC) considers the right to exclude in the context of a shopping mall.

    When Harrison v Carswell reached the Supreme Court of Canada it encountered two of the most prominent Canadian judges of the twentieth century, Bora Laskin and Brian Dickson, who wrote opposing judgments.

    Bora Laskin, the first Jewish member of the SCC and the Chief Justice when the case is heard, was nearing the end of a distinguished career on the bench.

    Justice Dickson, on the other hand, was just from a corporate law practice and in the early stages of a judicial career that would see him write some of the defining Charter of Rights and Freedoms cases and Aboriginal rights cases. Here we see a fundamental disagreement over the respective roles of courts and legislatures, and over the processes of social and legal change.

    Justice Dickson for the majority of the Supreme Court of Canada (SCC) and Justice Laskin in dissent take very different approaches to the role of precedent and the institutional roles of courts and legislatures. It is not surprising that they reach different conclusions about the appropriate outcome.

    Sophie Carswell, an employee at a Dominion grocery store in the Polo Park Shopping Centre in Winnipeg, was charged by the manager of the mall with trespass. She and others had been picketing within the mall, in front of the Dominion store as part of a lawful labour dispute with her employer. The mall manager asked her and the other employees to leave on the grounds that picketing was not allowed within the mall. When they did not, Carswell was charged with trespass.

    Activity

    Read the excerpts from Harrison v Carswell and the comments that follow in the Reader, at 30-39.

    The debate between justices about the role of precedent revolves around the application of an earlier SCC decision, R v Peters, which also involved picketing within shopping mall.

    Laskin J concludes that he is not bound by the SCC’s decision in Peters:

    This Court, above all others in this country, cannot be simply mechanistic about previous decisions, whatever be the respect it should pay to such decisions. What we would be doing here, if we were to say that the Peters case, because it was so recently decided, has concluded the present case for us, would be to take merely one side of a debateable issue and say that it concludes the debate without the need to hear the other side. [34]

    Why does Laskin J conclude that Peters is not binding?

    If, as Laskin J holds, Peters is not binding, then what legal principles should decide the case? How is the context of the dispute important? The social context? The legal context?

    What do you make of the following passage from Laskin’s judgment:

    An ancient legal concept, trespass, is urged here in all its pristine force by a shopping centre owner in respect of areas of the shopping centre which have been opened by him to public use, and necessarily so because of the commercial character of the enterprise based on tenancies by operators of a variety of businesses. To say in such circumstances that the shopping centre owner may, at his whim, order any member of the public out of the shopping centre on penalty or liability for trespass if he refuses to leave does not make sense if there is no proper reason in that member's conduct or activity to justify the order to leave. [35]

    On the question of the role of the courts, Dickson J adopts a highly deferential attitude to the legislature. He does not see it as the proper role of the court to engage in the balancing of interests that Laskin J proposes. Instead, Dickson J concludes that courts have a much more limited role:

    The submission that this Court should weigh and determine the respective values to society of the right to property and the right to picket raises important and difficult political and socio-economic issues, the resolution of which must, by their very nature, be arbitrary and embody personal economic and social beliefs. It raises also fundamental questions as to the role of this Court under the Canadian Constitution. The duty of the Court, as I envisage it, is to proceed in the discharge of its adjudicative function in a reasoned way from principled decision and established concepts. I do not for a moment doubt the power of the Court to act creatively， it has done so on countless occasions; but manifestly one must ask， what are the limits of the judicial function? [31]

    Where does Dickson J think the balancing of “difficult political and socio-economic issues” should occur?

    1.2 Why Private Property?

    In the excerpt included at the beginning of the Reader, C.B. MacPherson suggests:

    property is not thought to be a right because it is an enforceable claim: it is an enforceable claim because it is thought to be a human right. This is simply another way of saying that any institution of property requires a justifying theory.[6]

    In this topic we canvas some of the justifications for private property, paying particular attention to the economic justification. We also re-visit the typologies of property, and focus on private property and common property in particular.

    A. Justifications for Private Property

    The various justificatory traditions for private property include arguments based on economic efficiency and prosperity, utilitarian cost-benefit analysis, the flourishing of human freedoms, theories of personhood and personal identity, a labour theory of property, and entitlements derived from occupancy.

    For some of you, this material may seem overly theoretical and detached from the law of property. However, we will see again and again that judges turn to one or more of these justifications for private property, either explicitly or implicitly, in deciding whether or not something should be labelled property and in adjudicating competing claims to property.

    Activity

    Read the excerpt from Carole Lewis in the Reader and then the questions and comments, at 49-59.

    Have you seen any of the justificatory traditions that Lewis discusses at work in the two cases you have read already? Think back to Justice Laskin’s judgement in Harrison v Carswell where he appears to suggest that the property interest in a shopping mall ought to be treated differently than a property interest in a home. Why? Does the personhood justification for property help to explain the distinction he makes?

    Richard Posner, one of the prominent scholars working in the field of law and economics (discussed in comment 7), suggests that private property must have three characteristics in order to ensure the most efficient allocation of resources:

    • exclusivity,
    • universality, and
    • transferability.

    If Garett Hardin’s articulation of the tragedy of the commons describes an inefficient outcome (the over-grazed pasture) of an open access regime, then Michael Heller’s characterization of the tragedy of the anti-commons describes an inefficient outcome (unused storefronts in post-USSR Moscow) from the excessive fragmentation of property interests. If ownership rights are sliced too finely, the costs of coming to agreements among owners inhibit the use of otherwise productive resources.

    B. Private Property on Indigenous Lands

    Indigenous land holdings in Canada exist in a number of different forms. We will analyze one of these forms, Aboriginal title in Unit 5, but there are also Indian reserves and treaty lands.

    Indian reserves in Canada are parcels of land held by the Federal government in trust for the Indian bands to which they were allotted.

    Treaty lands are those territories recognized in a treaty with the Federal (and sometimes provincial government) as belonging to, and under the jurisdiction of the Indigenous Nation that is a party to the treaty.

    Some have argued that Indigenous peoples need the capacity to recognize and allocate private property rights on reserves or other Indigenous lands. One of the principal advocates for greater recognition of private property rights on Indigenous lands has been Manny Jules, former chief of the Kamloops Indian Band. In 2010, he wrote a preface to Beyond the Indian Act: Restoring Aboriginal Property Rights, which included the following passage:

    To fully realize the full value of our land we need a secure property-rights system. To restore our property rights we need to first protect our underlying collective title. The little bit of land we currently have can never be lost. The reversionary and expropriation rights must always be held by us. Once our collective title is secure, we can issue the type of individual title that we choose. Some of us may choose to issue fee simple and others may choose to implement leasehold. This title must be registered in a Torrens system—the best system in the world. This will ensure our lands will be worth as much as any other lands in Canada, should we so choose. (xi)

    Canada, British Columbia, and the Nisga’a Nation concluded the first modern land claims agreement in British Columbia when they signed the Nisga’a Final Agreement in 1999. The Nisga’a Final Agreement designates approximately 2000 km sq. as Nisga’a Lands and indicates that the Nisga’a Nation holds those lands in fee simple.

    We will discuss the fee simple at length when we get to the discussion of Estates in Unit 5, but for now it is enough to know that the fee simple, as section 3.3 of the Final Agreement indicates, is the largest estate or interest in land in the common law.

    Activity

    In brief, the legislation enables individual Nisga’a to convert a village or nation entitlement, which were only transferable to other village or Nation members, to a fee simple interest that is transferable to anyone. However, notice how the Nisga’a have limited the land that might be converted to fee simple ownership to residential lots within the villages.

    Consider the arguments for, as well as some of the cautions expressed about the allocation of fee simple interests to individual Nisga’a. Which of the justificatory traditions for private property are most prevalent?     1.3 Novel Claims to Property

    What things should be owned and how should things that are owned be distributed? This section uses a number of famous property law cases to consider the moment at which something will be labelled property, or not labelled property. In these “novel” claims to property cases, the various justifications for property appear frequently and often explicitly as judges reach for moral arguments to found their decisions.

    A. Property in Published News?

    International New Service v Associate Press is a classic case involving a claim by one group of newspapers to establish a property interest in published news in order to prevent another group of newspapers from publishing that news.

    The case remains interesting more than a century after it was decided and has enduring relevance because the judges are confronted with the question of whether or not to confer the status of property. Often that question is created by technological innovation and the new possibilities that emerge from it, or from some social change that rearranges relations between people with respect to space. In this case, it appears that some combination of factors, including the telegraph, the war in Europe, the business structure of the newspaper industry, the role and power of newspapers in disseminating news, and the personalities involved, produced the litigation.

    In terms of the personalities involved, William Randolf Hearst was the head of INS. He is also the figure that Orson Wells used as the basis for his tragic hero in Citizen Kane.

    Melville Elijah Stone headed Associated Press.

    Because it could not get the stories itself, INS:

    • bribed employees of AP newspapers to provide copies of the stories before they were published;
    • induced AP member newspapers to violate association by-laws to furnish stories before they were published; and
    • copied news from bulletin boards and early editions of AP papers on the east coast, and then sold the news items to its papers, competitors of the AP papers, on the west coast.

    The US courts were quick to grant an injunction (an order to stop doing something) against the first two activities, bribing and inducing breach. It is the third activity, the systematic collection of news published by AP and its member newspapers and distributing it to competitor papers, that occupied the USSC.

    INS did not deny any of the allegations. Instead, it argued that it was common practice in the newspaper business to take a fact or story from another paper as a “tip”, confirm its contents and publish the story. In fact, AP commonly followed the “tip” practice itself, taking published stories from other papers.

    Nonetheless, Melville Stone, the general manager of AP, thought it was time to establish the concept of “property in news”. News was a valuable commodity worth protecting as property. Earlier attempts to protect news as property through legislation had not been successful. The expressive form was protected under copyright law (papers could not plagiarize), but the factual content was not. Stone turned to the courts to establish property in published news.

    The issue for the USSC was: Does Associated Press have a property interest in published news? Or more broadly, can anyone of have property interest in published news? If so, what is the extent of that interest?

    B. Nuisance and Property in Spectacle?

    In Victoria Park Racing v Taylor, the plaintiff company, owned a horse-racing track and held regular events that Justice Rich describes as “a competition in the comparative merits of racehorses.” [69] A fence enclosed the grounds and the plaintiff charged the public for admission.

    One of the defendants, Taylor, built an elevated platform on land he owned adjacent to the track to overlook the racecourse. Taylor then arranged with a radio station to place a commentator on the platform who, through a telephone, broadcast live radio reports of the races and betting odds. The broadcasts quickly became popular and stimulated off-course betting, drawing business away from the racecourse itself.

    Victoria Park sought an injunction. On what grounds? All the justices agreed that Taylor and the Commonwealth Broadcasting Corporation had hurt the business interests of Victoria Park. There was no doubt that Victoria Park created something of value， a spectacle that people will pay to see， and that the value was diminished by Taylor’s actions. Does reducing value create a legal right to redress?

    None of the justices would disagree with Chief Justice Latham’s comment that:

    ... the mere fact that damage results to a plaintiff from such a description [of the horse racing] cannot be relied upon as a cause of action [that is, as providing a legal right to a remedy]. [69]

    In this case, does Victoria Park have a legal remedy, and if so what is the basis in law?

    Activity

    Read the excerpt from Victoria Park Racing in the Reader, at 68-73.

    Nuisance is a doctrine in tort law that regulates conflicting land uses. It serves to protect interests in land from various forms of indirect and ongoing interference that arise in neighbouring properties, things such as noise, odours, fumes, vibrations, etc.

    What significant hurdle must Victoria Park overcome in order to establish that Taylor’s use of its property amounts to a nuisance? Rich J makes a finding of nuisance. How does he reach that conclusion?

    Latham CJ and Dixon J concur that there is no nuisance and no property in spectacle. How to they reach this decision?

    Consider Latham CJ’s statement: “In my opinion, the law cannot by an injunction in effect erect fences which the plaintiff is not prepared to provide.” [68] Do you think that property law has a role to play in erecting fences?

    Finally, does the image of the viewing platform, the one that was used to overlook the racecourse, influence your feelings about the correctness of the decision?

    C. Property in Body Parts?

    The excerpt from Moore v The Regents of the University of California, a case involving an individual’s claim to property in excised body parts, contains majority and dissenting opinions that follow a parallel structure, making the points of disagreement clear. The first few pages of the judgment from Panelli J provide a clear introduction to the facts of the case. There are a number of important issues about a doctor’s fiduciary duty to his or her patient in this case, but the interesting question for our purposes is whether John Moore has a claim under the tort of conversion against his doctor.

    In order to establish a claim in conversion, interference with ownership or the right of possession, Moore must establish a property interest.

    • What role does an economic analysis play in the decision of the majority?
    • What do you take from the two decisions about the morality of commodifying body parts?

    In 2014, an Ontario court faced the question of whether excised human tissue was personal property. In Piljak Estate v. Abraham, 2014 ONSC 2893, the plaintiff sought an order to be allowed to do genetic testing on excised tissue as part of an effort to establish medical negligence. The capacity of the court to grant the order turned, in part, on whether the excised tissue was personal property. The court concluded that it was personal property, but declined to grant the order for other reasons. Read paras. 21-27 of the decision. Note the variety of sources used to define property.

    Piljak Estate v. Abraham, 2014 ONSC 2893

    IS EXCISED HUMAN TISSUE PERSONAL PROPERTY?

    [21] Rule 32.01 permits only “the inspection of real or personal property”. Is a tissue sample taken from a human being for the purpose of diagnostic testing “personal property”?

    [22] In my view, this is not a simple question. Neither party has provided me with any jurisprudence determining that issue and neither party has presented a principled approach to determining the question.

    [23] All definitions of “property” involve ownership or other legal rights with respect to a thing or object. “Property” has been defined as a “thing belonging to someone”. It is described as “that which belongs exclusively to a person; in a legal sense, the aggregate of rights that are subject to ownership. The term may denote the thing or object to which the rights or interest apply or to the legal relationship that exists with respect to those rights.” It is an “external thing over which the rights of possession, use and enjoyment are exercised” or alternatively the “right of ownership” or “the right to possess, use and enjoy” a “determinate thing”.

    [24] “Personal property” or “personalty” is that class of property dealing with rights in a “chattel” or any “movable or intangible thing that is subject to ownership and not classified as real property.”[6]

    [25] The moving defendants have provided me with an article from the Canadian Medical Association Journal dealing with rights to access excised human tissue and in so doing make conclusions about who “owns” the tissue. It notes that human tissue is excised either for diagnostic purposes/medical care or for research purposes. Diagnostic tissue, such as in the case before me, is tissue obtained in a procedure for patient care and that in accordance with regulations under the Public Hospitals Act must be sent to a laboratory or pathology department for examination and diagnosis. The sampled and processed tissue is retained as archived diagnostic tissue for a minimum of 20 years in the clinical archives of the pathology department (although excised tissue not specifically sampled is typically discarded within weeks).

    [26] The authors state that it “is unquestionably true that patients own their tissue before it is excised”, and while it has never been squarely dealt with by a Canadian court, they conclude that diagnostic tissue, once excised becomes a “component of the medical record” (as required by regulation under the Public Hospitals Act). As such, “both possession and ownership are transferred to the institution” and “by virtue of it being part of the medical record, diagnostic tissue is therefore owned by the institution or hospital.” At best a patient is entitled to “reasonable access.” The authors note that their conclusion has been supported by American jurisprudence. While this is not binding on me I find the reasoning compellable and I adopt its conclusions.

    [27] Ms. Piljak’s excised tissue is therefore owned by Sunnybrook Hospital, whose pathology department performed the diagnostic tests and in whose archives the tissue is kept. As the excised tissue is subject to rights of ownership, and since the tissue is clearly a moveable, I conclude that it is personal property to which inspection and testing under rule 32.01 may apply.

    D. The Limits of New Property

    The final readings in Unit 1 introduce the numerus clausus concept, borrowed from the civil law tradition, and considers a possible explanation for it. As you move through this course, you may feel that property law in the common law tradition is little more than a jumble of rules without any organizing frame or structure to provide it with coherence. If so, you won’t be alone, but the numerus clausus doctrine establishes certain constraints on that jumble. Most importantly, it provides that the number of property interests is finite, or at least that the courts must exercise great restraint in recognizing new ones.

    Chapter 1 The Nature of Property

    CB MacPherson, "The Meaning of Property" in Property: Mainstream and Critical Positions (Toronto: University of Toronto Press, 1978), 1-15.

    Property a Right, Not a Thing: ... to have property is to have a right, not a thing. Property is a right because it is an enforceable claim to some use or benefit of something, whether it is a right to a share in some common resource or in an individual right in some particular thing. What distinguishes property from mere momentary possession is that property is a claim that will be enforced by society or the state, by custom or convention or law. ... a right in the sense of an enforceable claim to some use or benefit of something...

    • Common property, ... th right to use the common things, however limited, is a right of individuals. Common property is created by the guarantee to each individual that he will not be excluded from the use or benefit of something.
    • Private property is created by the guarantee that an individual can exclude others from the use or benefit of something. In the case of private property, the right may, of course, be held by an artificial person, that is, by a corporation or an unincorporated grouping created or recognized by the state as having the same (or similar) property rights as a natural person.
    • There is another kind of property which appears not to be an individual right at all. This may called "state property": it consists of rights which the state has not only created but has kept for itself or has taken over from private individuals or corporations.

    Conventional Typology of Property Systems

    • State/public Property: the State or its agencies have the right to determine rules of access and use, but a duty (at least in theory) to manage publicly owned resources for the public welfare. Individual members of the public do not necessarily have a right of access or use, but they have a duty to observe access and use rules promulgated by controlling/managing agency.
    • Private Property: owners have the exclusive right to undertake socially acceptable uses to the exclusion of non-owners and have a duty to refrain from socially unacceptable uses. Non-owners have a duty to refrain from preventing owners' socially acceptable uses, but have the right to prevent or be compensated for socially unacceptable uses.
    • Common Property: each member of the ownership group has the right to access and use group-owned resources in accordance with access and use rules established collectively by the group, and a duty not to violate access and use rules.
    • Non-Property/Open Access: no individual has a duty to refrain from accessing and using a resource. No individual or group has the right to prevent any other individual or group from accessing and using the resource as they choose.

    TW Merrill, "Property and the Right to Exclude" (1998) 77 Neb L Rev 730 at 730-39

    The Supreme Court is fond of saying that "the right to exclude" is "one of the most essential sticks in the bundle of rights that are commonly characterized as property." ... My claim is simply that in demarcating the line between "property" and "non-property," the right to exclude others is a necessary and sufficient condition of identifying the existence of property.

    Points of Consensus:

    • First, nearly everyone agrees that the institution of property is not concerned with scarce resources themselves ("things), but rather with the rights of persons with respect to such resources.
    • Second, there is consensus that the concept of property includes the rights of persons with respect to both tangible and intangible resources.
    • Third, property means something different than mere possession. Property refers not to a state of fact but to a norm. Property rights generally trump possessory rights.
    • Fourth, property cannot exist without some institutional structure that stands ready to enforce it.
    • Finally, the concept of property is not limited to private property, but includes also what may be called common property and public property.

    Three Schools of Thought Regarding the Right to Exclude:

    • Single Variable Essentialism: the right to exclude ("sole and despotic dominion") is both a necessary and sufficient condition of property.
    • Multi-Variable Essentialism: the essence of property lies not just in the right to exclude others, but in a larger set of attributes or incidents, of which the right to exclude is just one. Under this version, the right to exclude is a necessary but not a sufficient condition of property.
    • Nominalism: this school of thought views property as a purely conventional concept with no fixed meaning, an empty vessel that can be filled by each legal system in accordance with its peculiar values adn beliefs. On this view, the right to exclude is neither a sufficient nor a necessary condition of property.

    Yanner v Eaton, (1999) 166 ALR 258 (HCA)

    Majority:

    According to Australian native title case law, a pre-existing native title will be extinguished by the granting of an inconsistent right.

    In the Fauna Act, as elsewhere in the law, "property" does not refer to a thing; it is a description of a legal relationship with a thing. "Property" is a term that can be, and is, applied to many different kinds of relationship with a subject matter.

    There are several reasons to conclude that the "property" conferred on the Crown is not accurately described as "full beneficial, or absolute, ownership".

    • First, this is the difficulty in identifying what fauna is owned by the Crown.
    • Secondly, assuming that the subject matter of the asserted ownership could be identified or some suitable criterion of identification could be determined, what exactly is meant by saying that the Crown has full beneficial, or absolute, ownership of a wild bird or animal?
    • Thirdly, there are several aspects of the Fauna Act which tend to suggest that the property in fauna conferred on the Crown may not easily be equated with the property an individual may have in a domestic animal.
    • Finally, it is necessary to consider why property in some fauna is vested in the Crown. Provisions vesting property in fauna in the Crown were introduced into Queensland legislation at the same time as provisions imposing a royalty on the skins of animals or birds taken or killed in Queensland.

    The "property" which the Fauna Act and its predecessors vested in the Crown was therefore no more than the aggregate of the various rights of control by the Executive that the legislation created.

At common law, the only right of property in wild animals was "the exclusive right to catch, kill and appropriate such animals which is sometimes called by the law a reduction of them into possession."

    No double in Australia, the existence of common law native title rights meant that Aboriginals had similar rights over fauna. Section 7 of the Act reverses the common law rules and vests all rights of catching, killing and appropriating fauna in Queensland in the Crown.

    When the Queensland legislature declared that the property in fauna is vested in the Crown, it should be taken to have meant what it said. ...the property in fauna is and always remains in th Crown until it disposes of it or a person, acting in accordance with the Act, puts an end to the Crown's property in particular fauna.

    Harrison v. Carswell, 1975 CanLII 160 (SCC)

    Dickson J:

    I find it difficult, indeed impossible, to make any well-founded distinction between this case and Peters v. The Queen (1971), 2 C.C.C. (2d) 339n, 17 D.L.R. (3d) 128, decided by this Court four years ago in a unanimous decision of the full bench. The constitutional issue raised in Peters no longer concerns us; the only other issue was whether the owner of a shopping plaza had sufficient control or possession of the common areas, having regard to the unrestricted invitation to the public to enter upon the premises, as to enable it to invoke the remedy of trespass.

The duty of the court, as I envisage it, is to proceed in the discharge of its adjudicative function in a reasoned way from principled decision and established concepts.

    Society has long since acknowledged that a public interest is served by permitting union members to bring economic pressure to bear upon their respective employers through peaceful picketing, but the right has been exercisable in some locations and not in others and to the extent that picketing has been permitted on private property the right hitherto has been accorded by statue.

    Laskin CJC (dissenting):

    The observation I am about to make about the Peters case carry into two areas of concern respecting the role of this Court as the final Court in this country in both civil and criminal causes. Those areas are,

    • first, whether this Court must pay mechanical deference to star decisis, and
    • second, whether this Court has a balancing role to play, without yielding place to th Legislature, where an ancient doctrine, in this case trespass, is invoked in a new setting to suppress a lawful activity supported by both legislation and by a well-understood legislative policy.

    This Court, above all others in this country, cannot be simply mechanistic about previous decisions, whatever be the respect it would pay to such decisions.

    The considerations which underlie the protection of private residences cannot apply to the same degree to a shopping centre in respect of its parking areas, roads and sidewalks.

    Should he be allowed to choose what members of the public come into those areas when they have been opened to all without discrimination? Human rights legislation would prevent him from discriminating on account of race, color or creed or national origin ...

    A more appropriate approach, to which I adverted earlier, is to recognize a continuing privilege in using th areas of the shopping centre provided for public passage subject to limitations arising out of the nature of the activity thereon and to the object pursued thereby, and subject as well to a limitation against material damage.

    International News Service v Associated Press, 248 US 215 (1918)

    Pitney J:

    The only matter that has been argued before us is whether defendant may lawfully be restrained from appropriating news taken from bulletins issued by complainant or any of its members or from newspapers published by them, for the purpose of selling it to defendant's clients.

    As presented in argument, these questions are:

    • Whether there is any property in news;
    • Whether, if there be property in news collected for the purpose of being published, it survives the instant of its publication in the first newspaper to which it is communicated by the news-gatherer; and
    • Whether defendant's admitted course of conduct in appropriating for commercial use matter taken from bulletins or early editions of AP publications constitutes unfair competition in trade.

    In considering the general questions of property in news matters, it is necessary to recognize its dual character, distinguishing between the substance of the information and the particular form or collocation of words in which the writer has communicated it.

    • No doubt news articles often possess a literary quality, and are subject of literary property at the common law; nor do we question that such an article, as a literary production, is the subject of copyright by the terms of the act as it now stands ...
    • But the news element, the information respecting current events contained in the literary production, is not the creation of the writer, but is a report of matters that ordinarily are publici juris: it is the history of the day.

    ... the news of current events may be regarded as common property.

    Obviously, the question of what is unfair competition in business must be determined with particular reference to the character and circumstances of the business. The question here is not so much the rights of either party as against the public but their rights as between themselves ... And, although we may and do assume that neither party has any remaining property interest as against the public in un-copyrighted news matter after the moment of its first publication, it by no means follows that there is no remaining property interest in it as between themselves.

    Stripped of all disguises, the process amounts to an unauthorized interference with normal operation of complainant's legitimate business precisely at the point where the profit is to be reaped ... The transaction speaks for itself and a court of equity ought not to hesitate long in characterizing it as unfair competition in business.

    But in a court of equity, where the question is one of unfair competition, if that which complainant has acquired fairly at substantial ost may be sold fairly at substantial profit, a competitor who is misappropriating it for the purpose of disposing of it to his own profit and to the disadvantage of complainant cannot be heard to say that it is too fugitive or evanescent to be regarded as property. It has all the attributes of property necessary for determining that a misappropriation of it by a competitor is unfair competition because contrary to good conscience.

    Moore v Regents of the University of California, 793 P (2d) 479 (Cal Sup Ct 1990)

    Panelli J:

    We granted review in this case to determine whether plaintiff has stated a cause of action against his physician and other defendants for using his cells in potentially lucrative medical research without his permission. We hold that the complaint states a cause of action for breach of the physician's disclosure obligation, but not for conversion.

    conversion: a tort that protects against interference with possessory and ownership interests in personal property.

    There are several reasons to doubt that he (plaintiff) retain any such (property) interest.

    • First, no reported judicial decision supports Moore's claim, either directly or by close analogy.
    • Second, California statutory law drastically limits any continuing interest of a patient in excised cells.
    • Third, the subject matters of the Regents' patent, the patented cell line and the products derive from it, cannot be Moore's property.

    • First, a fair balancing of the relevant policy considerations counsels against extending the tort.
    • Second, problems in this area are better suited to legislative resolution.
    • Third, the tort of conversion is not necessary to protect patients' rights.

    B Ziff, "The Irreversibility of Commodification

    It has long been conventional to regard property rights within common law systems as being comprised of a stable, rigid or fixed set of entitlements. A party wishing to demonstrate that a right is proprietary must, in the normal course of events, show that the interest in question fits into the appropriate legal box. Civilian systems refer to this as the numerus clausus principle, and in recent literature this label has been applied to the common law position.

    Although the literal meaning of numerus clausus is "closed numbers," the common law list is not fixed. The door is not bolted shut, but rather left slightly ajar. The subsequent development of English law on the very issue in Keppell v Bailey exemplifies this last point. Only 14 years after Keppell was decided, the landmark case of Tulk v Moxhay established that covenants could run with freehold land in equity. The modern law of restrictive covenants is built upon the Tulk decision.
